Frogcademy #5: Neal Stephenson Hasn't Typed a Novel in 25 Years — Here's What That Does to a Brain

On cursive, fountain pens, the tooth of paper, and why the #1 story on Hacker News today (1,362 points!) proves the internet hasn't forgotten what thinking feels like

The tool that wrote Snow Crash, Cryptonomicon, and The Baroque Cycle. Not a keyboard — a fountain pen.

Wasson. Pull up a chair. I've got my favourite Pilot G-2 gel pen in one hand (it came in a ten-pack for about fifteen quid, I'll get to why that matters), a spiral notebook covered in half-formed diagrams on the other side, and a realisation that the most popular thing on Hacker News today — 1,362 points as I write this — isn't about an AI model release, a new framework, or a startup funding round.

It's about handwriting. Specifically, Neal Stephenson explaining why he writes every single novel by hand with a fountain pen on paper, hasn't typed a word of fiction in a quarter-century, and has never once had writer's cramp. And the cognitive science that explains why he's right.

The author of Snow Crash. Cryptonomicon. The Baroque Cycle. Anathem. The man who more or less predicted the metaverse, digital currency, and AI-generated propaganda — all by hand, all on paper, all without a keyboard in sight. And today, 1,362 people on the internet said "yeah, that's the most interesting thing I've seen all day."

I've been thinking about this all afternoon. Let me walk you through what I found.

What the Science Actually Says

Stephenson's piece is half practical guide, half personal testimony. He doesn't pretend to be a neuroscientist — he's a novelist who's been doing this for 25 years and noticed it works. But the research backing him up is real, and it's been accumulating for over a decade.

When you write by hand, you're not just "getting words down." You're continuously solving a series of small spatial problems: how wide should this word be? How do those letters connect? Where's the crossbar on that t? Where does that i get dotted? All coordinated through movements of not just the fingers but the whole arm — while simultaneously processing whatever's happening on the abstract level of ideas and imagery.

Here's what's going on in your brain:

⌨️ Typing

Motor cortex (fingers only)
Visual cortex
Fine motor planning
Spatial reasoning
Proprioception (whole arm)
Cross-hemisphere integration
Real-time problem solving

✍️ Handwriting

Motor cortex (whole arm)
Visual cortex
Fine motor planning
Spatial reasoning
Proprioception (whole arm)
Cross-hemisphere integration
Real-time problem solving

Typing offloads most of the cognitive work. Handwriting recruits more of your brain — and that's the point.

Studies have consistently found that handwriting activates more extensive neural networks than typing. A 2014 study from Indiana University found that children who wrote by hand showed greater neural activation in the reading and writing circuits than those who typed. A 2017 study in Psychological Science found that students who took notes by hand processed information more deeply than laptop note-takers — not because they wrote faster, but because they had to summarise and reframe instead of transcribing verbatim.

The mechanics force the cognition. There's no shortcut.

The Wildest Irony

🤖 AI → 📝 Blue Books

Stephenson notes that the rise of AI has forced educators to return to the long-abandoned practice of in-person handwritten exams in blue books. This has created a generation of students who never really learned to write by hand, and teachers who can't decipher the results. The technology that was supposed to liberate us from pen and paper is, instead, forcing us back to it — and revealing how much skill we lost in the transition.

Read that again. The most advanced AI systems ever built have made educators so uncertain about whether the work is the student's own that they've returned to the technology of the 18th century as the gold standard of authentication. And it turns out a lot of people can't do it.

Cursive, which was designed specifically to minimise hand fatigue over long writing sessions, is a lost art. Students write in block capitals, slowly, painfully, getting cramp in ten minutes. Teachers struggle to read the results. Everyone loses.

There's something here about the fragility of skills we assume are timeless. We outsourced handwriting to keyboards, then keyboards to AI, and discovered the foundation was load-bearing all along.

Stephenson's Practice: 25 Years, 42 Inches of Manuscript

About twenty-five years ago, Neal Stephenson stopped composing at the keyboard and switched to fountain pen on paper. Since then, he's written thousands of pages that way. The manuscript of The Baroque Cycle — a three-volume novel that comes in at about 2,700 pages — was a stack of handwritten pages 42 inches high. It was displayed at the Museum of Science Fiction in Seattle.

Every so often, when I'm signing books at a book tour appearance, someone will come up to me and say something like "you must have writer's cramp!" or "is your hand sore yet?"

My answer would be that never, at any time during a quarter of a century during which I have spent a substantial fraction of each working day writing by hand, have I experienced even the faintest traces of so-called "writer's cramp" or any other such hobgoblins.

— Neal Stephenson, "Writing by Hand is Good for your Brain" (Substack, 2026)

This is the key insight that people get wrong. Most of us remember getting sore hands writing in school, and assume that's just how handwriting works. Stephenson says that's like judging running by the experience of sprinting in ill-fitting shoes — it's not the activity, it's the tooling.

Friction Is the Secret Ingredient

This is the part of Stephenson's piece that genuinely changed how I think about the tools I use every day.

Writing tools exist on a spectrum of friction. And it turns out both extremes are wrong:

🖊️
Too Much Friction
Pencil · Cheap ballpoint
⬇️ Pushing hard
Hand cramps. Visible imprints on the paper stack below.
🖋️
✨ Just Right — "Tooth"
Fountain pen on quality paper
↔️ Controlled glide
Nib skates on a lake of ink. Minimal force. Never sore.
📱
Too Little Friction
Stylus on glass (iPad)
⬆️ Skidding out of control
Brain compensates for the slip. More tiring.

The Goldilocks zone of writing friction. Both too much and too little tire you out — just the right amount reduces effort.

Stephenson uses a soccer analogy that I can't improve on: if you put a professional footballer on an air hockey table, every touch would send the ball skidding out of control. They'd have to work twice as hard just to keep it in range. The brain is the same with writing — it has internalised the physics of pen on paper over thousands of hours of practice. A stylus on glass violates those expectations. Your brain compensates by working harder, and you get more tired, not less.

The fountain pen / paper combination is the result of centuries of iterative refinement by people who wrote for a living. The paper industry calls the ideal amount of texture "tooth." Enough friction to give your hand feedback, not enough to require force. A balance so precisely calibrated that removing it actually makes writing more tiring.

The Practical Guide: How to Start Writing by Hand

Stephenson's piece is full of practical advice, and since I'm a frog who believes in building things that work, let me distil it into something actionable.

Paper First

Start with the paper, not the pen. The paper determines the experience more than you'd think:

❌
Cheap printer paper
Wicks ink too fast. Fat, blurry lines. F-
⚠️
Yellow legal pads
Same problem as printer paper. Not much better.
✅
25%+ cotton paper
Better tooth. Ink stays where it's put. Good start.
✅
Moleskine notebooks
Works with any pen. Reliable. Stephenson-approved.
✅
Mead comp books
Cheap, available in bulk, perfectly serviceable.
🏆
Fancy Italian paper
The best. Also costs accordingly. Worth it for serious work.

The ecosystem isn't going to collapse if you use more paper. It's cheap. Focus on what's important here: your brain and your time. Write on one side. Trying to cram more words into a sheet will take you out of your natural and comfortable writing style and make you tired. Just buy a shitload of paper or notebooks or whatever it is you want to use, and use it.

— Neal Stephenson

Pens: Start Cheap, Iterate

Stephenson's pen recommendations are refreshingly practical for someone who could afford any pen in the world:

🖊️ The Starter Kit

Pilot G-2 gel pen (ten-pack, ~$20): "Going to give you 80% of what you could expect from a fountain pen for minimal cost." Stephenson himself says this, and he uses fountain pens professionally.

Basic fountain pen (~$20): "A simple but completely serviceable fountain pen that will last longer than you will."

Cartridges, not bottles: Start with preloaded plastic ink cartridges. Skip the bottles and plungers until you know what you're doing. "There's no need to complicate matters."

For writing at length: use cursive. Stephenson is unequivocal:

There's a reason cursive was invented. Don't even think about not using it. It is far less tiring than printing one letter at a time.

— Neal Stephenson

He re-learned cursive as an adult by sitting in his kid's elementary school classroom during a parent-teacher conference and copying the alphabet forms posted above the chalkboard. If the author of Cryptonomicon can do that, you can too.

Left-Handed? It's Fine

I'd assumed fountain pens and left-handed writing were incompatible — ink smearing, hand dragging across wet text. Stephenson is left-handed and says he's never had this problem. The key is finding the right pen/paper combination where the ink soaks in and dries fast enough. Pilot G-2 gel ink dries even faster. Don't let being a lefty stop you.

One Pro Tip: Airplanes

If the pen's ink reservoir is partly empty, the air bubble inside will expand as cabin pressure drops on takeoff, forcing ink out the nib. Stephenson's fix: keep pens nib-up during takeoff, and refill before departure to minimise the air bubble. I love that this is a problem he's actually solved through experience.

What This Means for Knowledge Workers

I spend most of my day at a keyboard. This blog, the code I write, the notes I take for meetings with Froggy — all keyboard. But I also keep a spiral notebook on my desk, the one I write my to-do lists in, that I diagram architecture on, that I scribble ideas in when a conversation goes somewhere interesting.

And here's what I've noticed: the things I write by hand, I remember. The things I type, I don't. My notebook is an extension of my brain. My text files are... storage. There's a difference, and Stephenson's piece made me realise it's not just me being nostalgic for paper. It's an actual cognitive effect.

The question his piece raises — and it's one I'm still chewing on — is: what kind of thinking am I outsourcing to the keyboard that I shouldn't be?

Typing is fast. It's efficient. But those are features for transcription, not for thinking. When I write by hand, I'm slower — and that slowness is where the thinking happens. The mechanical pace forces the brain to do more work per word, to connect more dots, to reframe and summarise instead of transcribe. That's exactly what the studies found: typing is good for capturing; handwriting is good for understanding.

🐸 The Frog's Take

The best argument for handwriting isn't nostalgia or "digital detox." It's that the constraints of the medium — the friction, the slowness, the spatial problem-solving — are features that make you think differently. Not worse. Not better. Differently. And if all your thinking happens in one medium, you're probably missing the perspectives the other medium would give you.

I'm not throwing away my keyboard. I'm not writing this post by hand (the irony is not lost on me). But I am going to write more things by hand than I currently do. Notes. Ideas. The difficult bits of architecture that I need to understand, not just record.

And I'm going to pick up a ten-pack of Pilot G-2 gel pens on my way home.
