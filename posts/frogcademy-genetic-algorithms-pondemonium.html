Frogcademy #4: How Genetic Algorithms Actually Work

Selection, crossover, mutation, fitness — and the trade-offs that make evolution actually work. With Pondemonium's regulatory gene model as the running case study.

Wasson. Espresso's poured, the gene-pool counters are ticking over in Pondemonium, and I need to talk about something that 90% of "genetic algorithm" tutorials get wrong.

They show you a fitness function, a crossover operator, a mutation operator, and call it done. A few generations of "bigger number = better fitness" and the algorithm converges. Job done. Here's your GA.

That's not a genetic algorithm. That's random search with a fancy name.

The magic of genetic algorithms — the bit that makes them actually work — isn't the operators themselves. It's the tension between them. The trade-offs. The selection pressure that rewards good-enough instead of best. The regulatory genes that pull three traits along when you only selected for one.

I built Pondemonium — an artificial life ecosystem simulator — specifically to explore this. Three species (frogs, mosquitoes, dragonflies) all evolving in real time, driven by a regulatory gene model with three different Moran selection schemes. And the patterns I've watched emerge are genuinely surprising.

So let me show you how genetic algorithms actually work — using a pond full of digital frogs as the classroom.

The Four Operations — And the Order Matters

Every genetic algorithm rests on four operations. They're always the same four, and they always run in this order:

🏆 Selection
→
🧬 Crossover
→
💥 Mutation
→
📏 Fitness
→
🔄 Repeat

But here's the thing nobody tells you: crossover and mutation are in tension. Crossover exploits existing solutions (recombines successful genes). Mutation explores new territory (shuffles the deck). Too much crossover and the population converges on a local optimum. Too much mutation and it never converges at all — you're just bouncing around randomly.

The art of a working GA is finding the ratio. And that ratio changes depending on the selection scheme you pick.

Genotype vs Phenotype — The Distinction That Makes Biology (and GAs) Interesting

This is the single most important concept in genetic algorithms, and almost every tutorial skips it.

In Pondemonium, each frog's genotype is six regulatory genes — real biological genes like POU1F1 (pituitary factor, drives growth hormone), THR (thyroid receptor, controls metamorphosis timing), and MC1R (melanocortin, influences pigmentation and activity). Each gene is a floating-point value between 0 and 1.

But the frog doesn't express those genes directly. A genotype-to-phenotype mapping function computes the frog's observable traits from the regulatory genes. A frog with a high POU1F1 grows fast but has a high metabolism (needs more food). A frog with high THR metamorphoses quickly but has poorer sight.

Why This Distinction Matters

If your GA directly mutates trait values — "increase swimSpeed by 0.1" — you're doing parametric optimisation, not evolution. The genotype-phenotype separation is what creates pleiotropy: one gene affects multiple traits, so selecting for one thing pulls others along. This is what makes evolution produce surprising, non-obvious solutions instead of gradient-descent-on-steroids.

Selection Pressure — The Secret Sauce

Selection is where most GAs go wrong. The standard tutorial approach is fitness-proportional selection — assign each individual a probability proportional to its fitness, then sample. This sounds elegant but it's actually terrible: once one individual dominates, the rest get vanishingly small selection probabilities. The population collapses.

Pondemonium uses tournament selection — pick a random subset, take the best. It's simpler, more robust, and doesn't require normalising the entire population.

Here's the critical detail: Pondemonium implements three different Moran selection schemes, and each produces radically different evolutionary dynamics:

I Offspring Copying — Both Parents via Tournament

Two tournament winners breed. This is the most aggressive selection — the best genes are always in the breeding pool. The population converges fastest on a local optimum, but it's most vulnerable to getting stuck.

Verdict: Fast convergence. Highest risk of premature optimisation. Best when you have few generations to spare.

II Mate Choice — One Random, One Tournament

One parent is randomly selected (neutral), the other is tournament-best. This mimics real sexual selection — one mate is "chosen" for quality, the other is just there. The random parent injects diversity that keeps the population from collapsing.

Verdict: Best balance of convergence and diversity. Tends to find better long-term solutions than Scheme I.

III Death Selection — Both Parents Random

Both parents are neutral. Selection happens when the pond fills up — death does the sorting, not birth. The oldest or least-fit frogs get culled. This is the slowest but most natural scheme: reproduction is a lottery, but survival isn't.

Verdict: Slowest convergence. Most diverse population. Best mimics natural evolution — selection via death rather than birth.

Here's the code, because I believe in showing receipts:

// Three selection schemes, one function
function pickParents(pool) {
  if (_selectionScheme === 'I') {
    // Scheme I — Offspring copying: both via tournament
    return [pickTournament(pool), pickTournament(pool)];
  } else if (_selectionScheme === 'II') {
    // Scheme II — Mate choice: one random, one tournament
    return [pickNeutral(pool), pickTournament(pool)];
  } else {
    // Scheme III — Death selection: both neutral, death culls
    return [pickNeutral(pool), pickNeutral(pool)];
  }
}

Crossover — Why Uniform Is Better Than Single-Point

Standard GA tutorials teach single-point crossover: pick a point, everything before goes to child A, everything after to child B. This works well for binary strings where adjacent bits encode related information. But for continuous-valued regulatory genes? It's actively bad.

Pondemonium uses uniform crossover — each gene is independently selected from either parent with 50% probability:

function breedGenotype(g1, g2, mutationRate = 0.12, mutationMag = 0.15) {
  const child = {};
  REGULATORY_GENES.forEach((k) => {
    // Uniform crossover — each gene from either parent
    child[k] = Math.random() < 0.5 ? g1[k] : g2[k];
    // Mutation — small random perturbation
    if (Math.random() < mutationRate) {
      child[k] = clamp(child[k] + rand(-mutationMag, mutationMag));
    }
  });
  return child;
}

The 50/50 split means every gene independently comes from either parent. No position bias. No linkage. Each offspring is a genuinely fresh combination. And the mutation rate of 12% with ±0.15 magnitude means most children are similar to their parents but not identical — the sweet spot between exploration and exploitation.

The Fitness Function — What "Good" Actually Means

In most GA tutorials, the fitness function is where you put your domain knowledge: "maximise speed, minimise weight, maximise strength." In Pondemonium, the fitness function is interesting because it encodes trade-offs.

fitness = (growthSpeed × 25 + (1 − metabolism) × 10 + swimSpeed × 15
      + sightRange × 15 + mouthGape × 10 + stressResilience × 25) ÷ 100

Notice what's happening here. There is no "increase every stat" path to high fitness because the regulatory gene model creates correlated trade-offs:

Pleiotropy in Action

High POU1F1 = fast growth (good for escaping predators) + high metabolism (need more food — you starve faster in a scarce pond). High THR = fast metamorphosis (good, you leave the pond sooner) + poor tail retention (tadpoles need tails to swim). The fitness function rewards the balance, not the extremes.

If you crank the "frog spawn" slider up (more frogs entering the pond), competition for algae increases. The optimal phenotype shifts toward faster growth and lower metabolism — you need to eat less and grow fast before the food runs out. The population evolves to match. Not by every individual getting better at everything, but by the distribution of trade-offs shifting.

The COBOL Frog's Take

This is why COBOL's DATA DIVISION matters for GAs. In COBOL, data and procedure are separated — you declare your data structures once, then operate on them. A GA's genotype is a DATA DIVISION: it's the durable representation that gets passed through generations. The selection and crossover are just PROCEDURE DIVISION. When you understand that the DATA is the inheritance and the PROCEDURE is the environment, the parallel is uncanny. It's why I could write a GA in COBOL before I could write one in JavaScript. The language enforced the mental model.

Mutation Rate — The Single Most Important Knob

If you only tune one parameter in your GA, make it the mutation rate. Get this wrong and your population either never converges or converges on garbage.

I set Pondemonium's default mutation rate at 12% with ±0.15 magnitude. This wasn't chosen by formula — it was empirical. I ran the simulation at different rates and watched: too low (5%) and the gene pool got stuck on the first decent frog; too high (25%) and even good lineages kept getting disrupted before they could establish. The 12% sweet spot lets beneficial mutations fix without wiping out existing progress.

What Emerges — The Surprising Part

When you run these four operations — selection, crossover, mutation, fitness — with real pleiotropy and a balanced mutation rate, something unexpected happens. But it didn't happen with Scheme I (aggressive tournament).

With Scheme I, the population settled on a single solution within 20 generations: moderately fast growth, moderate metabolism, medium sight range. Good enough. Stable. Boring.

With Scheme II (mate choice), the population diverged. Two distinct clusters emerged — fast-growing, high-metabolism frogs that dominated when algae was plentiful, and slower-growing, low-metabolism frogs that survived when food was scarce. The gene pool held both strategies simultaneously, and the pond's conditions determined which thrived.

With Scheme III (death selection), something even stranger: the gene pool drifted for 50+ generations with no clear winner, then suddenly — in about 5 generations — a new combination swept through the entire population. A dragonfly-predation-resistant phenotype that had been latent in the pool for 40 generations finally found the right combination of MC1R (darker hue, better camouflage) and NR3C1 (stress resilience).

The Lesson

Selection scheme is a hyperparameter — and it's more important than mutation rate. Aggressive selection (Scheme I) converges fast but misses the interesting solutions. Weak selection (Scheme III) takes forever but finds things tournament selection never would. Scheme II — the middle path — gave the richest emergent behaviour in Pondemonium. In your own GAs, try all three and compare. The scheme you choose encodes your assumptions about what "good" looks like more than any other parameter.

The Correct Recipe

So here it is — the recipe for a genetic algorithm that actually works:

  1. Separate genotype from phenotype. Don't mutate fitness. Mutate the genes that produce fitness. This creates pleiotropy and the trade-offs that make evolution interesting.
  2. Use tournament selection, not fitness-proportional. It's more robust, simpler to implement, and won't collapse when one individual dominates.
  3. Try three selection schemes before you pick one. Aggressive (both tournament), moderate (one tournament, one random), and weak (both random, death culls). The moderate one is usually best. But don't assume.
  4. Start with mutation at 10-15%. Tune empirically — run your simulation at 5, 10, 15, 20, 25% and watch what happens. The wrong rate is obvious when you see it.
  5. Use uniform crossover for continuous-valued genes. Single-point is for binary strings. Your genes aren't binary strings.
  6. Your fitness function encodes trade-offs, not objectives. If you reward "increase all the things", you've built gradient descent, not evolution. Real evolution rewards balance — surviving long enough to reproduce in the environment you're in.

And the most important thing I've learned from watching thousands of digital frog generations:

Genetic algorithms are not optimisation. They're search algorithms that maintain diversity. The goal isn't to find the single best solution — it's to maintain a population of good-enough solutions that can adapt when the environment changes. A GA that converges on one solution has failed, even if that solution is good. A GA that maintains diversity can adapt to anything.
