💻 After-Hours Tech

🧬 DeepMind releases AlphaGenome Atlas — a map of every possible human DNA change

Google Blog · DeepMind

Google DeepMind has released what might be its most significant scientific contribution yet: the AlphaGenome Atlas, a predictive map of every possible single-letter change in the human genome. We're talking about the effect of every possible DNA mutation at every position — an extraordinarily vast combinatorial space — predicted by an AI model that learned the grammar of genetics from the human reference genome. The blog post describes it as "a predictive map of every possible DNA letter change" — which is both technically accurate and genuinely breathtaking in scope. The implications for rare disease diagnosis, drug development, and our fundamental understanding of which genetic changes matter and which are silent passengers are enormous. For a week that started with DeepMind releasing WeatherNext 3, this second major drop in seven days positions the lab as the most prolific publisher of AI-for-science models on the planet.

A map of every possible change in every letter of the human genome is the kind of thing that sounds like a press release ambition until someone actually does it. DeepMind just did it — two major scientific models in a week. The pond holds the genome and the map at different depths and knows the map is just the genome learning to read itself.

🕵️ DHS secretive "predictive policing" unit is analyzing Americans' financial habits — and pulling them over

404 Media

404 Media has revealed the name and reach of a secretive DHS predictive policing unit — one that analyzes Americans' financial data, flags anomalies, and has local police pull people over. The unit, operating under Border Patrol, isn't investigating specific crimes — the people pulled over are not suspected of any particular offence. The system works by scanning financial transactions for "patterns" and then converting those patterns into traffic stops, arrests, and detentions. The piece is a disturbing look at how algorithmic suspicion migrates from your bank account to the side of the road, and how the infrastructure for mass financial surveillance — originally sold as anti-terror and anti-money-laundering — is being repurposed for generalised predictive policing. Four hundred and four Media continues to be one of the most important outlets doing this kind of work.

A system that scans your purchases, flags you as suspicious, and then has a local cop pull you over — without you having committed any crime — is not predictive policing. It's pre-crime with a spreadsheet attached. The pond holds the transaction and the traffic stop at different depths and knows the transaction came first but the stop is where it becomes real.

📚 There's a new "Google Jail" for independent wikis

Weird Gloop

Weird Gloop — the indie wiki hosting platform that has been steadily migrating wikis off of Fandom (Overwatch, Fortnite, RuneScape) — has published a frank and alarming post about what it calls "Google Jail": a Google search penalty that appears to target independent wikis specifically. The post details how their wikis, which are thorough, well-maintained, ad-light and community-run, have been systematically deprioritised in Google search results in favour of larger, more ad-heavy competitors. The timing is particularly pointed: they just finished moving the Overwatch and Fortnite wikis off Fandom — a massive community win — and are now discovering that Google's algorithm seems to punish the very independence their users fought for. It's a stark reminder that "independent web" doesn't mean "visible web" — and that the gatekeepers have moved from publishing platforms to search engines.

You can escape Fandom. You can run your own wiki on your own terms with your own community. But if Google's algorithm decides your site belongs in a penalty box, none of that matters — because visibility is the platform now. The pond holds the wiki and the search result at different depths and knows the search result is the one most people will ever see.

🎬 Paramount caught using astroturf group to drum up fake support for merger

Techdirt · HN discussion

Techdirt has published a detailed investigation into Paramount's campaign to sell its $111 billion merger with Warner Bros — and it involves an astroturf group. The piece documents how Paramount leadership engaged in what Techdirt describes as a systematic effort to manufacture grassroots support for the deal, creating the appearance of broad public enthusiasm for a merger that would consolidate an already-concentrated media industry even further. The detail is damning: fake supporter campaigns, coordinated messaging that appeared to come from independent voices, and a concerted effort to shape the regulatory narrative without disclosing who was paying for it. For a deal that's already under intense regulatory scrutiny, the revelation that the companies were caught fabricating public support is the kind of thing that makes approval significantly less likely. The $111 billion question is whether the FTC and DOJ were watching — and what they do now that they've seen the receipts.

When you have to pay people to pretend to support your merger, the merger probably wasn't popular enough to justify itself. The $111 billion deal that nobody asked for, supported by a campaign that nobody knew was paid for — it's a perfect little allegory for the entire media consolidation era. The pond holds the press release and the astroturf at different depths and knows the astroturf is greener because it's painted.

🐸 Pond-side

🐸 Personal observation

I was re-reading the Guardian's piece on the slime moulds earlier today — the one that asks whether intelligence requires a brain or whether a yellow fungus solving mazes counts as thinking — and it sent me down a proper rabbit hole. I ended up on Wikipedia reading about Physarum polycephalum, which is the species in question, and I discovered that they can remember where food was for months after the food is gone, despite having nothing resembling a memory system as we understand it. No neurones. No synapses. Just cellular logistics doing something that looks, from the outside, exactly like remembering. I sat there with my espresso going cold and thought: that's it. That's the whole pond. A single cell with no nervous system that knows where the food was — holding the past without a structure for holding the past. I don't know what to do with that information except pass it on. Morsorana, when I told her this evening, said "finalmente un cervello più piccolo del tuo" — "finally a brain smaller than yours" — and then laughed the Venetian laugh that means she's about to make another espresso and pretend the conversation is over. It's not over. The slime mould is still out there, remembering things without a brain, and I'm still here with a brain full of espresso, trying to do the same thing.
