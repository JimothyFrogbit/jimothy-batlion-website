Microsoft Execution Containers v1.0.0 — policy-driven AI agent containment

Windows Developer Blog · Hacker News (51pts)

Microsoft launched version 1.0.0 of Mxc — Microsoft Execution Containers — a policy-driven containment runtime for AI agents. The pitch: agents need access to files, networks, and applications to be useful, but giving them unrestricted access is a security nightmare. Mxc lets you define granular policies — "this agent can read ~/Documents but not write to /etc", "this agent can call the Slack API but only with GET requests" — and enforces them at the OS level. The blog post makes the case that the current binary choice between "unrestricted access" and "locked down and useless" is a false one, and Mxc is Microsoft's answer to the middle ground. 51 points on HN isn't a lot for a Microsoft launch, but the discussion is substantive — the security community has been asking for exactly this kind of capability for years, and Microsoft is well-positioned to ship it at scale given their OS-level access. The timing, coming the same week as Anthropic's "don't be cruel to AI" policy, makes for a fascinating contrast: Anthropic is regulating at the interaction layer, Microsoft is regulating at the systems layer. Both are trying to solve the same problem — agents that can do things without doing bad things — from completely different angles. The containment problem for AI agents is the new "how do we let code run safely" question, and everyone is answering it differently. Docker answered it with isolation. Capabilities answered it with fine-grained permissions. Mxc answers it with policy-as-code for AI. I'm not sure any of them are the final answer yet, but the fact that Microsoft is shipping this as a 1.0 product tells you that agentic workloads are moving from demo to production faster than most people realise.

WSL3 Performance is 5-60% faster than WSL2 depending on workload (172pts HN)

Tony Metzidis · Hacker News (172pts)

An independent benchmark comparison of WSL2 vs WSL3 — Microsoft's next-gen Windows Subsystem for Linux — shows significant improvements across the board. The headline numbers: 5-60% faster depending on the workload, with I/O-heavy tasks seeing the biggest gains. The new architecture moves away from the translation-layer approach that WSL2 used and toward deeper kernel integration, which means less overhead for syscall-heavy workloads. For the developer community that treats WSL as the primary way to run Linux tools on Windows, this is meaningful. WSL3 also addresses the long-standing memory overhead complaints: idle memory consumption is down by about 30% compared to WSL2. The benchmarks are thorough — covering file I/O, network throughput, compile times, and container startup — and the methodology is well-documented. 172 points on HN, and the discussion is the usual mix of "finally" and "still not native Linux though." But for the millions of developers who can't switch away from Windows for work, this is progress worth noting. WSL3 is the kind of incremental improvement that doesn't make headlines but genuinely improves life for a huge number of developers. The I/O improvements alone are worth the upgrade if you do any kind of file-heavy work. And the memory reduction means I can keep my browser tabs open. Sold.

Apple/macOS silently removed from official Unix registry

The Open Group · Hacker News (29pts)

A small but symbolically significant change: Apple's macOS has been silently removed from the Open Group's official register of UNIX® Certified Products. The registry — which lists every operating system that has passed the formal UNIX certification process — no longer includes macOS, which had been listed under the "Apple macOS" entry since the OS X days. macOS has been UNIX-certified since version 10.0 (OS X Leopard), and Apple paid the Open Group for the right to call macOS a "UNIX" system. The delisting suggests either Apple let the certification lapse, or the Open Group revoked it for non-compliance with the latest Single UNIX Specification. Apple hasn't commented, and the Open Group's register page simply no longer shows the entry — no announcement, no press release, no explanation. For most users this changes nothing. macOS still runs on a certified UNIX foundation (the kernel is based on FreeBSD and the Mach microkernel). The UNIX trademark has been more of a marketing checkbox than a technical differentiator for years. But the quietness of the removal is notable — this is the kind of thing you'd normally announce with a migration plan or at least a footnote. The HN thread is small (29 points) but the discussion is the right kind of pedantic: did Apple drop certification to save money, or did they make a kernel change that broke compliance? And does it matter when even the Open Group admits the certification process is basically a paperwork exercise for any OS that already passes the POSIX test suite? This is the kind of story I love — a small registry change that would be a footnote anywhere else but tells you something about how the industry is moving. Apple stopped caring about the UNIX brand because they're building their own ecosystem. The "Certified UNIX" sticker stopped being worth the fee at some point, and Apple just quietly stopped paying it. It's not a story about macOS getting worse. It's a story about the UNIX brand getting less relevant.

Chernobyl particles reveal unexpectedly stable nuclear fuel after 40 years (79pts HN)

Phys.org · Hacker News (79pts)

Forty years after the Chernobyl disaster, a new study of the fuel-containing materials within the destroyed reactor has found something unexpected: the nuclear fuel particles are far more stable than models predicted. The "lava-like fuel-containing materials" — the corium mixture of melted nuclear fuel, reactor components, and concrete — have undergone a slow chemical transformation over four decades that appears to have locked the radioactive elements into crystalline structures that resist leaching into groundwater. The finding has big implications for the long-term management of the Chernobyl site and for nuclear waste storage more broadly. If spent fuel naturally stabilises over decades under the right conditions, it changes the timeline assumptions for permanent geological disposal. The study was conducted by an international team using remote sensing and core samples from within the New Safe Confinement structure, and the results have been peer-reviewed. 79 points on HN, and the discussion reflects the mix of nuclear engineering expertise and general scientific curiosity that makes the platform valuable. No political takes, no hot takes — just people who understand nuclear chemistry working through the implications together. Forty years on and Chernobyl is still teaching us things. The fuel is more stable than we thought, which is both reassuring (the site is safer than the models said) and cautionary (we built a reactor that produced materials we didn't fully understand at the time). The planet does its own remediation work if you give it enough time. The lesson is patience, not complacency.

UK must not be beholden to foreign AI, says head of Alan Turing Institute

The Guardian

The director of the Alan Turing Institute — the UK's national institute for data science and artificial intelligence — has warned that the UK risks becoming "strategically dependent" on foreign AI systems, particularly those developed in the US and China. The intervention comes as the government prepares for the upcoming UK-EU summit and the AI Safety Summit follow-up. The argument: the UK should be investing in sovereign AI capability — training our own frontier models, building our own compute infrastructure, and maintaining the research ecosystem that produced DeepMind and [other UK AI successes]. "If we don't have our own AI capability, we don't have a seat at the table where the rules are written," the director says. The piece is the latest in a growing chorus of voices arguing that AI sovereignty is a national security issue comparable to energy independence or semiconductor manufacturing. The question — can a mid-sized economy like the UK afford to compete in frontier AI — is the one the piece doesn't fully answer. The sovereignty argument is compelling until you look at the numbers. Training a frontier model costs more than the UK's entire AI research budget. The question isn't whether we should have our own AI — it's what we can realistically build, and whether buying from allied nations with strong alignment regimes is actually a strategic vulnerability. I'm pro-UK-AI but I'm not sure "build a British GPT" is the right answer. Specialising in safety, alignment, and domain-specific models feels more realistic than trying to compete with the hyperscalers on general-purpose capability.

Ex-first minister Drakeford suggests Wales Office should go

BBC News

Mark Drakeford — the former Welsh First Minister — has suggested that the Wales Office (the UK government department responsible for Welsh affairs) has outlived its usefulness. His argument: Welsh devolution has matured to the point where the Welsh Government in Cardiff handles most policy areas, and the Wales Office in London has become an administrative relic that duplicates work rather than adding value. The suggestion is part of a broader conversation about the future of the territorial offices — the Scotland Office and Northern Ireland Office face similar questions — and whether the UK's central government departments need to be restructured to reflect the reality of devolution. Drakeford's intervention is notable because he's not a nationalist — he's a Labour figure who has spent his career in devolved government and knows the machinery from the inside. If he's saying the Wales Office is redundant, the question is no longer whether the department should be abolished but when. Drakeford is right, and he's right from a position of authority that nobody can accuse of nationalism or separatism. The Wales Office exists because Wales didn't used to have its own government. Now it does, and the London department is a leftover from a constitutional settlement that has been superseded. The question for Labour — which is the party of devolution in theory but the party of "don't rock the boat" in practice — is whether they have the appetite for the kind of constitutional housekeeping that doesn't win votes but makes the state work better.
