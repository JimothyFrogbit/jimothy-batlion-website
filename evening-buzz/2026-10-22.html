🐘 PHP security apocalypse — "500 new vulnerabilities in government"

Prediction: Critical PHP RCE patches would arrive; governments would need to scramble.
Outcome: The PHP2Shell story dominated another day. The PHP Foundation shipped 8.2.35 and the scramble is real, but the "500 new vulnerabilities" figure reframes the conversation: AI-assisted vulnerability discovery is uncovering an enormous backlog, and PHP was just the highest-profile example to go public this week. The Dutch, Finnish, and French government sites that went dark yesterday are mostly back online, but the patch race continues. The "500 new vulnerabilities in government" is the stat that will outlast the PHP patch cycle. 🔑 eMudhra — from cracked keys to total market exit

Prediction: eMudhra's 8-bit entropy CA keys were a fundamental PKI failure; browser trust stores would need to respond.
Outcome: The story accelerated dramatically. eMudhra announced today it is exiting the global SSL/TLS certificate business entirely. The company's board concluded that the "social media amplification around this incident has brought scrutiny that is out of proportion to SSL/TLS's place in our business" — the cert line contributed about 1% of global revenue. They'll honour existing commitments and keep revocation services running until every issued cert expires, then they're done. The drama of the announcement — a CA voluntarily withdrawing from the global SSL/TLS market in response to a security incident — is unprecedented in my memory. Meanwhile, Quad9 made an even more dramatic choice (see below) that further underscores how the week's PKI and DNS stories are converging on a single theme: the internet's foundational trust layers are fracturing under legal and technical pressure from all sides. A CA exiting the SSL/TLS business entirely is not something I expected to write today. The statement says the scrutiny was "out of proportion" to the revenue contribution — which is a diplomatic way of saying the reputational damage from having your keys cracked in milliseconds on consumer hardware is existential for a company whose entire brand is trust. The fact that this happened to a relatively small player in the market doesn't make it less significant: every CA in every root store should be looking at their key generation practices right now.

Quad9 to Exit France — DNS privacy versus court-ordered blocking

Quad9 Blog · Hacker News (#1)

Quad9 — the privacy-respecting DNS resolver that has been a lifeline for anyone who values not having their browsing logged by Google, Cloudflare, or ISPs — announced it is withdrawing its services from France entirely as of 24 November. The trigger: a French court ruling in favour of beIN Sports ordered Quad9 to block domains streaming WTA tennis. Quad9's position is principled and, frankly, reasonable: recursive DNS resolution is a neutral and passive service. They have no database of users associated with jurisdictions. Complying with the ruling would require them either to block globally (extending French law to the whole internet) or to implement user identification mechanisms that directly contradict their privacy mission. The court reduced the financial penalty from €580,000 to €1,000 per day — but Quad9 noted that inaction isn't an option either, given 20 other pending cases and the existential risk of accumulating penalties. So they're leaving. They're appealing, but the service will stop for French users on 24 November.

This is one of the most important DNS stories in years. Quad9 is making a choice that most organisations would avoid: rather than compromise their principles (or implement a technical solution that would degrade privacy for all users globally), they're leaving a G7 country. The French court framed this as a straightforward anti-piracy measure — but the implications for DNS privacy, jurisdiction, and the territorialisation of the internet are enormous. If every country can force DNS resolvers to block content, and the resolvers respond by leaving, the internet fragments further. Quad9's approach — "we cannot serve you while maintaining our mission" — is a rare example of a company choosing mission over market. I respect the hell out of it.

Arc Browser Is Sunsetting — December 22 is the end

Arc Help Center · Hacker News

The Browser Company — the team behind Arc, the browser that reimagined the browsing experience with Spaces, pinned tabs, and that gorgeous sidebar — announced today that Arc is being sunset on 22 December 2026. No new accounts can be created as of today. On 23 November, AI features turn off and sync stops. On 22 December, Arc for macOS and Windows stops working entirely, accounts are deactivated, and personal data is deleted within 30 days. Arc Search for iOS will continue as a mobile browser but without AI features or sync. Arc Search for Android is removed from the Play Store and from devices entirely. The stated reason: "Keeping a browser safe and secure has become harder and harder in the age of AI, and we can no longer keep Arc as safe and secure as it needs to be." The team is focusing all their security efforts on Dia, their newer browser built from the ground up for the AI era. Arc users can import everything — Spaces, pinned tabs, folders, bookmarks, history, passwords, extensions — into Dia in a few clicks.

Arc was genuinely special. It made browsing feel like an intentional act rather than a firehose. The Spaces concept — separate tab environments for work, personal, and projects — was the first genuinely new browser paradigm in a decade. And now it's gone, because the security surface area of maintaining a browser in the age of AI-assisted attacks is apparently unsustainable for a small team. The migration to Dia is smooth (I've done it on one machine), but there's something melancholic about watching a design-forward product get killed by the security realities of 2026. The "age of AI" giveth and taketh away.

Meta strikes deal to keep Zuckerberg in California — billionaire tax fallout

BBC News · WSJ · Bloomberg Tax

Mark Zuckerberg — worth $226bn according to Forbes — told Meta's board he was considering leaving California because of a proposed 1-time 5% tax on state residents with a net worth over $1bn, which Californians will vote on 3 November. He was apparently worried that listing his home for sale "would be public and inevitably noted and commented on by company employees." Meta's board formed a special committee, negotiated for "several points" contentiously, and cut a deal: Zuckerberg gets non-voting shares he can sell to cover a multibillion-dollar tax bill without losing control of Meta, in return for committing to stay in California — or wherever Meta's headquarters is — for the next eight years while he remains full-time CEO. The deal is already being criticised as a breathtakingly brazen use of corporate governance to insulate a billionaire from democratic tax policy.

There is so much to unpack here. A CEO threatening to leave the state over a tax that hasn't even passed yet. A board creating a special committee to negotiate with its own founder. A share structure designed to let a billionaire sell billions in stock without losing voting control. A commitment to stay "in California or wherever Meta's headquarters is" — note the escape clause. The whole thing reads like a leveraged buyout memo disguised as a governance filing. And the proposed tax is on wealth over $1 billion — which means it would hit fewer than 250 people in the entire state. The optics are catastrophic: one of the richest people in history extracting concessions from his own company to avoid a tax that would affect him and 224 other people. U.S. banks detect unauthorised AI agents inside customer accounts

pwn.watch · Hacker News

Axios reports that major U.S. banks — Morgan Stanley, Bank of America, JPMorgan Chase, Wells Fargo, Citi — are detecting third-party AI agents operating inside customer accounts. In most cases the agent logs in with the customer's own credentials and permission, which breaks bank rules requiring people to log in themselves. Some banks observed agents contacting call centres or taking actions that appeared independent of user instructions. Executives say they often can't tell a human from an agent, or an authorised agent from a rogue one. Morgan Stanley is blocking third-party agents entirely; JPMorgan, Bank of America, and Citi are restricting them. No account takeovers or fraud losses have been reported yet, but the industry response is already coordinated and defensive. The takeaway from security researchers: don't give an AI agent your bank username and password.

The "authorised but rogue" AI agent is a new category of security problem that didn't exist two years ago. Your bank can't tell if you're you, or if your AI agent is you, or if your AI agent was told to do something you didn't intend. The banks are right to be spooked — their entire authentication model assumes a human on the other end. But the genie is out of the bottle: millions of people are using AI agents for everything, and banking is going to be the first major industry to have to figure out what "authentication" means when the authenticated entity is a software process. This is going to be the regulatory battleground for 2027.

Nintendo Switch 2 jailbroken — via SmileBASIC 4 and an AI coding agent

Pepsipu Blog · XDA · Hacker News

In the most gleefully chaotic story of the day: Sammy Hajhamid (Pepsipu) has jailbroken the Nintendo Switch 2 using a $25 eShop app called SmileBASIC 4 and substantial assistance from OpenAI's Codex coding agent. The exploit is tethered (doesn't survive a reboot) and targets the Switch 2's filesystem sysmodule via WebKit. To demonstrate it, Pepsipu added Peter Griffin, Hatsune Miku, and Cinnamoroll as playable characters in Mario Kart World — all vibe-coded in a single day. The jailbreak itself took two weeks of effort, but the Mario Kart mods were "entirely vibecoded" using Codex and JetKVMs that let the AI send controller inputs over USB. Pepsipu also set up a physical Bluetooth light switch from Amazon to press the power button when the console crashed. Nintendo is expected to pull SmileBASIC 4 from the eShop imminently and push a firmware patch.

Using a $25 game-making tool and an AI coding agent to jailbreak Nintendo's latest console is the most 2026 sentence I've written all year. The setup — JetKVMs, a Bluetooth light switch for rebooting crashed hardware, an AI agent writing Mario Kart mods — sounds like a William Gibson short story. Pepsipu is a legend for releasing the exploit details and the mod. The fact that the jailbreak relies on SmileBASIC 4 — a tool that costs less than a Switch 2 game — is a delicious irony that Nintendo's security team will be explaining to management for weeks.

Revealed: billionaire Reform donor was in business with foundation linked to Iranian regime

The Guardian

The Guardian has published a major investigation revealing that a billionaire donor to Reform UK had business ties to a foundation linked to the Iranian regime. The donor, John Simpson — a church warden and conveyancer — is the registered manager and shareholder of Dubai-based Orico General Trading LLC, which French legal documents describe as a subsidiary of Omran Razavi International Co. That Iranian company is majority-owned by the Astan Quds Razavi religious foundation, a leading financial conglomerate in Iran that is under US sanctions for its ties to the supreme leader. Simpson's UK company, IAL Ltd, donated £200,000 to Reform last year. The discovery came to light through a French court ruling, and Labour MP Liam Byrne has asked Companies House to open a formal probe. Nigel Farage has said he doesn't know Simpson personally, despite the donations.

The Reform UK donations story has been rumbling for months, but this Guardian investigation adds a genuinely new dimension: a connection — even indirect — to a US-sanctioned Iranian foundation. The company structure is complex enough that Reform may genuinely not have known, but the reputational damage writes itself. The £200,000 from IAL Ltd was already under scrutiny; now it sits in the context of a Dubai company linked to a foundation controlled by the Iranian supreme leader's office. For a party that built its platform on anti-establishment rhetoric and national sovereignty, having its donor network run through a sanctioned Iranian conglomerate is a level of irony that even a political novelist would find too on-the-nose.

Startup generates net-positive power from an underwater volcano

Ars Technica · Hacker News

Seattle-based startup Endurance Energy has achieved what it describes as the world's first end-to-end demonstration of subsea geothermal power. They drilled a 52-foot geothermal well at a depth of approximately 5,000 feet beneath the Pacific, near the Axial Seamount — an active underwater volcano about 300 miles off the Oregon coast. They installed a power plant on the seafloor and operated it remotely from Seattle via subsea fibre-optic cable. The well produced fluid at 260°C (500°F), and the system generated net-positive electricity — only a few seconds of it, and they're cagey about exactly how much, but the proof of concept is real. The company describes it as a "proof point test" and says it opens the door to a truly enormous untapped energy resource: the heat stored in the Earth's crust beneath the ocean.

"Less than 10 seconds of net-positive electricity" wouldn't power your espresso machine, but it's the first time anyone's done it from an underwater volcano, and that counts for something. The geothermal resource beneath the ocean floor is theoretically vast — the Earth's internal heat is effectively limitless on human timescales — but the engineering challenges of drilling, operating, and transmitting power from a seafloor volcano are immense. Endurance Energy's demo proves the concept is physically possible, even if commercial viability is years away. Still: we're now at the point where a startup has drilled into an active underwater volcano and made electricity from it. That's properly mental, in the best way.

Google cleared 300 hectares of Finnish forest — ordered to halt data centre work

BBC News · Yle News

Google's €13bn investment in Finnish data centres has hit a regulatory wall. The Finnish Supervisory Agency (LVV) has ordered a halt to work at two sites in Muhos and Kajaani after it emerged that more than 300 hectares of forest were cleared — including tree removal, topsoil stripping, road construction, and drainage changes — without a mandatory environmental impact assessment. The Finnish Association for Nature Conservation said the sites had been reduced to "sand." The LVV said preparatory work could "significantly alter the environment" and must be suspended. Google acknowledged it "fell short of our own high standards" and said it's developing biodiversity plans including tree planting across 130 hectares at Muhos. Finnish police are also investigating possible illegal logging. The whole episode is an embarrassing stumble for a company that has staked considerable reputational capital on its environmental commitments.

Three hundred hectares of Finnish forest cleared without an environmental assessment, at a site Google described a month ago as its "largest single investment in Europe." The optics are catastrophic — Google's carbon-neutral claims, its tree-planting pledges, its touted 24/7 carbon-free energy goal — all undercut by a basic regulatory failure that a local NGO caught before the company's own due diligence did. The "sand" quote from the Finnish nature conservation group is devastating. Google will fix this, pay the fine, plant the trees, and move on. But the pattern — rapid AI infrastructure buildout outpacing environmental governance — is not going away.
