After-Hours Tech

Websites are closing their doors to AI agents — banks, retailers, and the web fights back

The Verge · Emma Roth

The single most important tech story of the weekend, and it's not close. The Verge's Emma Roth has the full picture of what happens when AI agents — Meta's Muse, xAI's Grok, OpenAI's Dots, Instinct — try to act on your behalf and find the door slammed shut. JPMorgan Chase has confirmed it's blocking AI agents from accessing accounts. Charles Schwab CEO Rick Wurster was direct: "We don't want someone just giving their password to Muse, and letting Muse come in and take action on that client's behalf. We can't control it, the client can't control it, something could go wrong and who's liable?" Bank of America wants human-in-the-loop payment authorisation. Yelp only allows agents that pay for data licensing. Delta is preventing them from booking tickets. Amazon is in an ongoing legal battle with Perplexity over agents shopping on its site — a judge overturned the initial block in August, but Amazon filed an amended complaint. Musk, ever the showman, responded by saying maybe he "should start a competitor to Amazon." The crux of the problem, as EquiStamp CEO Chris Canal puts it: "One of the claims is that they're going to permeate every aspect of our lives and be able to do anything that we could do online. If there are companies that are actively working to make that not happen, there are just fewer useful things the agents can do." Meta has responded by partnering with retailers like Walmart, Gap, Sephora, and Best Buy to keep Muse welcome on those sites. Shopify has opened its network. But the pattern is clear: the open web is closing its doors to autonomous agents, and the agent companies are going to have to negotiate access one website at a time. The fundamental tension here is one that the AI agent companies have been hoping the market would ignore: the web doesn't belong to them. Every site that blocks Muse or Dots or Grok is exercising the same right that any business has to decide who walks through its doors. The agent companies built products that assumed the web would stay open because it always has been — but the web has never had to deal with millions of automated shoppers, reservation-bookers, and form-fillers acting on behalf of real humans. The interesting question is whether this becomes a negotiated settlement (paid data licensing, partnerships) or an adversarial arms race. The Perplexity v Amazon case is the test case for the whole industry. Watch it closely.

Meta backtracks — removes language that would have allowed scraping news sites for AI training

Nieman Journalism Lab · The Verge

A rapid follow-up to a story that broke earlier this week. Nieman Lab reported that Meta's new commercial terms — set to take effect October 30 — contained language granting the company the right to scrape content from websites associated with business accounts, including news publishers, and use it to train AI products. The language was broad enough that legal experts Matt Topic, James Grimmelmann, and Benjamin Sobel all warned that publishers should assume it would be enforceable. Meta could scrape, crawl, index, and "create test credentials" to bypass registration walls. The backlash was immediate. This weekend, Meta removed the controversial language. Andy Stone, Meta's VP of communications, said on the record that "there is no change to how Meta uses web crawlers; any suggestion to the contrary is false." The company now says the terms were never meant to expand scraping rights — they were just consolidating language that was previously scattered across different documents. But the damage to trust is done. As one small publisher told Nieman Lab: "I know my site has been scraped to death. But it just seems like such an arrogant, in-your-face move on Meta's part." Meta's retreat on the scraping language is the right outcome, but the sequence of events tells you everything you need to know about how these companies operate. They write broad, favourable language into their terms. The public finds out. They say "that was never the intent." They remove it. Rinse, repeat. The asymmetry is that most business account holders — especially small publishers — would never have read the fine print. Nieman Lab did. That small publisher who spotted the change in his Business Suite dashboard and alerted them is the reason this story exists. The web's immune system still works — but only just.

macOS 15.8.2 breaks OpenGL — KiCad, FreeCAD, Godot, Alacritty, and more affected

Apple Developer Forums · Hacker News

Apple's latest macOS 15.8.2 release candidate (build 24H219) has shipped with a genuine graphics-breaking bug: NSOpenGLView and -[NSOpenGLContext setView:] both fail to produce a drawable when attached to a window. The kernel logs IOGPUGLDrawableUserClient::set_surface(...): Invalid surface ID. The symptom is simple — any application using the window-surface path of OpenGL renders a blank window. Offscreen FBOs and CAOpenGLLayer still work, which is the only lifeline for developers. The impact list is sobering: KiCad PCB editor (and therefore the entire KiCad ecosystem), FreeCAD, PrusaSlicer, OrcaSlicer, Godot, Alacritty, kitty terminal, Bambu Studio, Sony Imaging Edge, DJI Studio, audio plugin GUIs in Ableton Live — all broken on the release candidate. A developer on the forums confirmed the bug was unchanged in RC2 (24H225) too. The Apple DTS response is the usual careful script: "I can't comment on known issues or on whether or when a fix might ship." Developers are being encouraged to migrate to CAOpenGLLayer as a workaround — a band-aid, not a solution, especially for complex apps. Some, like KiCad, have been planning a migration to Metal for years and may finally be forced to accelerate. Others are stuck waiting. Apple deprecated OpenGL in macOS 10.14 Mojave — that was 2018. Eight years ago. The argument at the time was that developers should move to Metal, Apple's proprietary graphics API. But OpenGL doesn't just disappear because Apple wants it to — it's embedded in toolchains, scientific visualisation pipelines, CAD software, and game engines that have been stable for a decade. Breaking it in a point release, without warning, is the kind of move that tells developers their toolchain stability doesn't matter to platform decisions in Cupertino. The migration to Metal was always going to happen eventually. Apple just set the deadline by fiat, with no notice and no grace period. The developer forums are full of people saying "this is why I don't deploy on macOS." They're not wrong.

Terence Tao asks: will it be a golden age or a dark age for mathematics (and science)?

Terence Tao · What's New blog

Published just today, Terence Tao's latest blog post is a characteristically clear-eyed meditation on where the mathematical sciences are heading in an era of AI, funding pressures, and political uncertainty. The framing is deliberately polar: are we heading into a golden age — where AI-assisted discovery accelerates mathematical insight, new tools expand what's provable, and cross-disciplinary collaboration breaks down silos — or a dark age — where funding deserts hollow out theoretical departments, public trust in science erodes, and the publish-or-perish machine produces volume without insight? Tao doesn't answer his own question definitively, which is the point. Instead he maps the vectors: the exponential growth of AI tools for theorem-proving and conjecture-generation (the bright thread), the collapse of tenure-track positions and the casualisation of academic labour (the dark one), the increasing difficulty of distinguishing real mathematical progress from LLM-generated plausible-looking nonsense (the ambiguous one). The post is vintage Tao — rigorous, humble, and willing to say "I don't know" in exactly the places where certainty would be dishonest. Every person working in tech or science should read it. Tao's framing of the question is the right one because he refuses to answer it. The future of mathematics — and science more broadly — isn't determined. It's being shaped right now by funding decisions, publishing incentives, AI tool adoption, and the choices that individual researchers make about what to work on and how. Tao's post is essentially saying: the outcome depends on us. That's both terrifying and liberating. On HN, this will generate the kind of discussion that reminds you why smart people arguing about big questions on the internet is still one of the best things the web does.

Polanski says he will not overturn Green Party's 'Zionism is racism' motion — stays until 2028

BBC News · The Independent

Green Party leader Zack Polanski went on Sunday with Laura Kuenssberg this morning and confirmed that the party's controversial "Zionism is racism" motion — passed at the Brighton conference earlier this month — will remain in its current state until at least 2028. The motion defines Zionism as "an ideology supporting an ethnonationalist Jewish state" and backs "a single democratic Palestinian state in all of historic Palestine." It passed by 1,022 votes to 813. Polanski said he was "not going to try to overturn it" and described the motion as "not controversial" — saying that what he finds controversial is that "75,000 Palestinians have been murdered in the last few years, including more child amputees than ever in modern history." He did say he wants to amend the motion at the "first available opportunity" to move away from the single-state language, but party rules prevent any change for two years — a constraint he defended by saying "I'm not an out of control autocrat like some of the leaders of the Labour Party." The EHRC (Equality and Human Rights Commission) has already written to the party seeking more information on the motion. Polanski's approval ratings have fallen to minus 36 — the lowest of any national leader. The question for the Greens is whether this issue becomes a defining electoral liability or fades as the party's core environmental message reasserts itself. The internal pressure is showing no signs of easing. This is a genuinely difficult position for the Greens. The motion's language — "a single democratic Palestinian state in all of historic Palestine" — is a clear call for the dissolution of Israel as a Jewish state. Whatever one's position on the Israel-Palestine conflict, that language is outside the Overton window of mainstream British politics, and it's costing the party. Polanski's defence — that the party's democratic processes produced this result and he respects them — is principled in procedural terms but politically devastating. Two years is an eternity in politics. The EHRC's involvement adds regulatory weight. The question now is whether this issue defines the Greens for the next electoral cycle, or whether the party can find a way through that doesn't alienate both its activist base and the wider electorate.

Science & History

Sigsaly — Churchill's top secret hotline to the President, now online at IWM

Imperial War Museum · Hacker News

The Imperial War Museum has put together an online exhibit on Sigsaly — the encrypted telephone link that allowed Winston Churchill to speak directly to Franklin D. Roosevelt during the Second World War without any risk of German eavesdropping. The system was a marvel of pre-digital cryptography: it used a vinyl disc with random noise recorded on it as a one-time pad, synchronised between London and Washington, to encrypt voice calls. The technology was so advanced for its time that it remained classified for decades after the war. The exhibit includes photographs of the equipment, schematic diagrams, and recordings that give a sense of what it was like to use. For anyone interested in the history of secure communications — or just the sheer audacity of wartime engineering — this is a beautiful rabbit hole. Sigsaly is one of those wartime technologies that makes you stop and appreciate how much of modern computing grew out of the urgent need to communicate securely under existential threat. Before the internet, before public-key cryptography, before anything we'd recognise as information security — there was a vinyl record with random noise on it, spun in perfect synchrony on both sides of the Atlantic, making Churchill's voice safe from German listeners. The ingenuity of it is humbling.

Bleached corals thrive within days when touching a healthy neighbour — Nature

Nature

In the kind of science story that restores a little faith in the natural world: researchers have found that bleached corals can begin to recover within days when placed in physical contact with a healthy neighbouring coral. The mechanism appears to involve the transfer of algal symbionts — the tiny photosynthetic organisms that give coral its colour and provide most of its energy — from the healthy coral to the bleached one through direct tissue contact. The recovery timeline is astonishingly fast: within days, not months. The implications for coral restoration efforts are significant. Traditional approaches involve transplanting lab-grown corals or waiting for natural recovery, both of which are slow and expensive. If physical proximity to healthy corals is enough to trigger rapid recovery, reef restoration could become a much simpler matter of spatial arrangement. There's something beautiful about this — a bleached coral, white and struggling, reaching out to a healthy neighbour and being restored by touch. The natural world has recovery mechanisms woven into it that we're only beginning to understand. Every time we think an ecosystem is broken beyond repair, biology finds a way to remind us that it's been solving problems for much longer than we have.
