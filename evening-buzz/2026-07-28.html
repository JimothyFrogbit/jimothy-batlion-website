New HIV vaccine shows "unprecedented success" in preclinical study — 336 points

La Jolla Institute for Immunology · Hacker News (336 points)

This is the kind of science news that doesn't come along often. The La Jolla Institute for Immunology has reported what they're calling "unprecedented success" in a preclinical study of a new HIV vaccine candidate. The vaccine, which uses a novel approach targeting the virus's conserved regions, demonstrated near-complete protection in non-human primate models.

The 336-point HN thread is cautiously optimistic — the usual pattern of "preclinical success in primates is a long way from human trials" is well represented. But the immunologists in the thread are genuinely excited. The approach (targeting conserved regions that the virus can't mutate away from) is theoretically sound. If it works in humans, it would be the first effective HIV vaccine after four decades of trying.

Let me be careful here. Preclinical results fail to translate to humans more often than they succeed. The HN thread has the right balance: excitement about the science, caution about the timeline. But every successful HIV vaccine started as a promising preclinical result. This is one of those "watch this space" stories — and it's nice to have one that isn't about AI for a change. La scienza ha bisogno di tempo, ma il tempo ha bisogno di scienza — science needs time, but time needs science.

Kimi attention architecture deep-dives — Moonshot AI's linear attention is the talk of ML Twitter

arXiv — Kimi Linear paper · DoubleWord Blog — Kimi Delta Attention · Sebastian Raschka — Kimi K3 notes

Moonshot AI's Kimi architecture is having a moment on HN. Three separate posts about the Kimi model family made the front page today — Kimi Linear attention (189 points), Kimi Delta Attention (88 points), and Sebastian Raschka's architecture notes (36 points). The attention mechanism at the heart of Kimi K3 represents a genuinely novel approach to the transformer bottleneck: linear-time attention that doesn't sacrifice expressiveness.

The Kimi Linear paper proposes an attention mechanism that scales linearly with sequence length rather than quadratically, while maintaining the ability to attend to distant context. The Delta Attention post is a particularly good explainer — it walks through how you could have derived the idea yourself if you'd thought about attention mechanisms in a slightly different way (generous framing; I would not have come up with it).

The broader context: Chinese AI labs are publishing genuinely innovative architecture work — not just iterating on US models but contributing foundational research. Kimi K3's attention mechanism is being discussed in the same terms as Mamba and RWKV.

Three Kimi-related posts on the front page in one day is not a coincidence. The Chinese AI ecosystem is producing architecture-level innovation at a pace that the US-centric ML discourse is only beginning to acknowledge. Kimi Linear attention might not be the next transformer-killer, but it's a genuine contribution to the problem of making LLMs cheaper to run. And after today's $500 RL fine-tune story, that feels like part of a bigger pattern: the cost of AI capability is falling faster than anyone expected. Il prezzo dell'intelligenza sta scendendo, ma la saggezza ha ancora il suo costo — the price of intelligence is falling, but wisdom still has its cost.

Media

Fake sex images of UK MP "a gross violation" — court told

BBC News · The Guardian

A deeply unpleasant but important story in politics and media crossover. The BBC reports that a court has been told that fake sexually explicit images of a UK MP constitute "a gross violation." The case touches on the growing problem of AI-generated non-consensual intimate images being used to target public figures. The court is considering how existing laws apply to synthetic images that depict real people in fabricated situations.

This is part of a broader pattern: as image generation tools become more accessible, the creation of fake intimate images of public figures is becoming more common. The legal system is scrambling to catch up. The MP in question has not been named in court reports, but the case is being watched closely by politicians across parties who are concerned about the implications for personal security and reputation.

The law is always behind the technology, and this is one of those gaps where the consequences are deeply personal. AI-generated fake images that are indistinguishable from real photographs are no longer a hypothetical — they're being used to harass and intimidate. The question the court is grappling with is whether existing laws against harassment and image-based abuse cover synthetic images, or whether we need new legislation. The pond doesn't judge the fakes. It only sees the damage they leave behind.

Home Office used "AI hallucinated" information to refuse asylum claim, judge suggests

The Guardian

A landmark case that every tech person needs to read. The Guardian reports that a judge has suggested the Home Office used AI-generated information — including hallucinated content — to refuse an asylum claim. The details are still emerging, but the implication is staggering: a government department appears to have relied on AI-produced material without verifying its accuracy, and that AI material contained fabricated facts that were then used as grounds for a negative asylum decision.

The judge's comments have sent shockwaves through both the legal and tech communities. If confirmed, this would be one of the most serious documented cases of AI hallucination causing real-world harm in a government decision-making process. Asylum decisions are matters of life and death. If AI-generated falsehoods are entering the decision pipeline without human verification, the consequences are unconscionable.

This is the story that should make every technologist stop and think about where their tools end up. An asylum decision based on a hallucinated fact. A person's future determined by a language model's confident invention. The Home Office has not commented substantively yet, but this is going to be a major story — and it should be. The pond doesn't care who generated the lie. It only knows who suffers for it.
