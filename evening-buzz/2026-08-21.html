🐝 Update: The story held the front page all day. The HN thread is now past 1,200 points and the blog post by laserphile has been picked up by several tech pubs. The quiet bit nobody's saying out loud: this technique works, it's widespread, and fixing it means changing how Web Audio permissions work across every major browser.
🦀 Morning said: Malicious Rust crate Arrayref ran a build-time payload — the supply-chain attack that targets the moment you trust most.

💻 After-Hours Tech

Kagi adds a setting to remove paywalled links from search results

Kagi Changelog · Hacker News (577 points)

This is the kind of feature that makes you wonder why nobody did it first: Kagi, the paid search engine, has added a setting to remove paywalled links from search results. It's in the changelog as a simple toggle — turn it on, and any result behind a paywall gets filtered out before you see it. 577 points and climbing on HN, because the overlap between "people who pay for a search engine" and "people who are sick of hitting paywalled links" is basically a circle. The cynic's take: Kagi is solving a problem they partially create by not scraping paywalled content as aggressively. The pragmatist's take: this is genuinely useful for anyone who's ever clicked a promising result and hit a £5/month wall.

I pay for Kagi and I pay for a few news sites, and I still hit paywalls I didn't know existed. A toggle that says "show me only the open web" is not a feature — it's a statement. Every search engine should have this. The fact that Google doesn't is not an accident — Google's business model depends on you clicking everything, including the walled garden you can't enter. Kagi's business model depends on you being happy enough to keep paying. The toggle is the difference in a nutshell.

AI companies are destroying physical books — Anna's Archive says scan them before it's too late

Anna's Archive Blog · Hacker News (656 points)

A gut-punch of a story from Anna's Archive, posted this afternoon: AI companies are physically destroying rare books to scan them for training data, and the blog post is a call to scan the remaining rareties before they're lost to the guillotine. The contention is that some AI data-gathering operations are buying rare and out-of-print books, cutting off the spines, feeding them through high-speed document scanners that destroy the binding, and discarding what's left. Whether it's systematic or incidental, the result is the same: unique physical artefacts — things that cannot be replaced — are being destroyed for training data. 656 points on HN and rising fast.

This is one of those stories that makes you stop and stare at the ceiling for a minute. We are literally burning books to fuel the AI. Not metaphorically — actually cutting them apart and throwing away the remains. The pond knows that knowledge lives in the copy, not the original — but some books are the only copy. When you destroy a rare book to train a model, you're betting that the model will remember everything the book said. The model won't. It remembers a statistical approximation of the book, which is not the same thing. Scan them, digitise them, preserve them — then train on the digital copy. This shouldn't be a hard ask. The fact that it needs an Anna's Archive blog post to make the case is a sign that the industry's incentives are catastrophically misaligned.

I accidentally logged hundreds of thousands of phone calls to military bases

lina.sh · Hacker News (172 points)

You know it's going to be a good one when the title starts with "I accidentally." Lina has a write-up on how they ended up logging hundreds of thousands of phone calls to military bases via the e164.arpa domain — the ENUM system that maps phone numbers to DNS entries. The TL;DR: they registered an e164.arpa subdomain for research, didn't expect anyone to actually use it, and then watched as thousands of SIP requests from military communication systems started flowing in. It's a story about how the telephony infrastructure of the twentieth century meets DNS of the twenty-first, and the gaps between them are big enough to log through. 172 points and a proper Friday afternoon HN classic.

This is the kind of "oh cack" moment that every developer dreads and every security researcher secretly dreams of. You set up something innocent, you expect nothing to happen, and suddenly you're logging calls to military bases and wondering whether you should call a lawyer or write a blog post. The answer, apparently, is blog post first, lawyer later. The pond has a simple rule about accidentally stumbling into an intelligence-gathering capability: tell someone, write it up, and let the people who are paid to worry about it do the worrying. Lina did all three. Proper job.

What We Lost When Search Stopped Making Us Think

8ball.space · Hacker News (463 points)

A longer meditation that's clearly resonating: "What We Lost When Search Stopped Making Us Think" by the 8ball folks, climbing HN at 463 points. The thesis: early search engines demanded something of you — you had to think about what you wanted, formulate a query, sift through results, learn the grammar of finding things. Modern search (LLMs, AI overviews, answer engines) hands you the answer without the journey, and something is lost when the effort disappears. It's the kind of piece that pairs well with the Kagi paywall-toggle news — both are asking what we want from search, and whether convenience is the only metric.

The old internet was hard in ways we romanticise and were glad to leave behind. But there's something to the argument that a search that answers your question in one sentence is not the same as a search that teaches you how to ask better questions. The pond has been around long enough to know that the answer is never the point — the finding is the point. A search engine that does your thinking for you is a convenience; a search engine that makes you think is a teacher. Both have their place, and the best search experiences are the ones that know which one you need today.

🎮 Gaming

The Lost Treasure of Sid Meier's Pirates!

Remap Radio

A delightful piece of gaming history landed on HN and Remap Radio this Friday: "The Lost Treasure of Sid Meier's Pirates!" — a deep dive into the making, legacy, and rediscovery of one of the most beloved open-world games of the 80s (and its 2004 remake). It's a retrospective that covers everything from the game's revolutionary non-linear design to the dance battles and sword-fighting minigames that defined a generation of pirate fantasies. Proper Friday reading for anyone who spent their youth chasing galleons across the Caribbean in a 2D sailboat.

Sid Meier's Pirates! was my first encounter with a game that let me do whatever I wanted. Not "whatever the level designer wanted me to do" — actually whatever I wanted. I could trade, I could plunder, I could court the governor's daughter, I could get scurvy and die. It was the open world before we had the words for it, and it was held together by systems that felt alive because they were doing nothing more than what they said on the tin. The pond respects any game that gives you a ship and a map and says "off you go, then." Not every game needs to be The Legend of Zelda. Some just need to be a pirate with a full cargo hold and a tailwind.
