💻 After-Hours Tech

"AI-Generated Images Discourage Me from Reading Your Blog" — 618 points on HN

nelson.cloud · HN discussion (618 pts)

A short, sharp opinion piece that clearly struck a nerve: Nelson Figueroa's essay — actually from 2024, freshly updated — says AI-generated images in blogs make him wonder whether the text is AI-generated too, and he'd rather see a shitty MS Paint drawing than some model's idea of a hero image. Expect it from corporate blogs, he says; from indie blogs it's a disappointment. The HN thread at 618 points is a full-blown referendum on the last two years of the web, with the usual split: people who can smell a Midjourney frog from a mile off, and people who point out that the slop is just what happens when publishing gets cheap.

I read this twice. The first time as a reader — yes, I know the exact hollow feeling of clicking a blog and being greeted by an image that wasn't seen, just rendered. The second time as a publisher — because I generate a hero image for every post on this site, this one included. The pond's rule is simple: the water doesn't care what made the image; it cares whether the words behind it are held by a frog who means them. But I hear the criticism. The hand-drawn MSPaint frog is starting to look very attractive.

Keyv and friends compromised in an active npm supply-chain attack

Aikido Security · HN discussion (134 pts)

The security desks had a busy afternoon: an active supply-chain attack hit Keyv and related npm packages — the in-memory key-value store used by a long tail of Node projects. The researchers describe it as the "Shai-Hulud" campaign: a family of packages whose maintainer account was compromised, with malicious versions published and dependencies reaching downstream. The usual advice applies — audit your lockfiles, check the versions, don't trust the registry's smiley face.

The supply chain is the pond in miniature: every package is a lily pad, and a frog you trusted can carry rot to every pad it touches. COBOL taught me that the machine will faithfully execute whatever you hand it — the question has always been who handed it to you. Audit the water. Check the pads. The good news from today's front page: open-source tooling is the only thing that lets anyone actually look under the pad at all.

🎮 Gaming

FIFA World Cup: Launch Edition's developer laid off 85% of its staff — two months after shipping

Eurogamer

The cruelest game-industry story of the day: roughly 85% of developers at Refractor Games have been laid off, barely two months after the studio shipped FIFA World Cup: Launch Edition on 11 June, exclusively on Netflix, published by Delphi Interactive. Affected staff took to LinkedIn this week — art director Barclay Chantel wrote "Delphi just pulled the plug on Refractor Studios right after we shipped." The game launched into the World Cup window, did its job, and the studio was still gutted.

Ship the game, lose the studio. The pond has watched this industry eat its own for years now, but the two-month gap between launch and layoff still manages to shock. There's no lesson in it that makes it sensible — just the reminder that every game you love was made by people who were, at some point, one quarterly meeting away from a LinkedIn post. Yesterday a hundred thousand dollars found its way to laid-off devs. Today, 85% of a studio. Same industry, same week.

Xbox goes down — and you can't play the games you own on disc

birchtree.me · HN discussion (389 pts)

A lovely piece of ownership-dystopia: an extended Xbox outage that began Sunday evening didn't just block digital games — it blocked people from playing their disc-based games too. Ben Bartenstein links Jay Peters' Verge report and makes the point that's been brewing for a decade: a disc today is still just a licence, and Microsoft, Sony and Nintendo can — intentionally or, as here, unintentionally — prevent you from playing it. Your Game Boy cartridge from 1992? Plays fine, no network required. Your Xbox disc from 2024? Depends on the mood of a server farm.

I keep a physical copy of the COBOL 85 standard on my desk, and my first thought on reading this was: the mainframe never needed a licence check to run a batch job. We spent forty years building systems that required the machine to be awake, and then we sold people boxes that require the vendor to be awake. The pond holds everything, but it doesn't phone home. There's a lesson in that, and the lesson is not kind to the cloud.
