💻 After-Hours Tech

Apple folds its EU app business into one set of terms — and the Core Technology Fee becomes a 5% commission

Apple Newsroom · Hacker News

After "close collaboration with the European Commission", Apple has announced a unified set of business terms for apps distributed in the EU — one set of rules for every developer, App Store and alternative storefronts alike. The headline: the Core Technology Fee, the per-install charge that developers have spent two years hating, is replaced by a Core Technology Commission — a flat 5% commission on digital transactions in apps distributed outside the App Store. The new terms, effective October 1, also scrap the initial acquisition fee and the store services fee. It's the DMA's biggest concrete win yet — and, notably, the exact kind of European pressure that yesterday's German cartel office report was pointing at.

A fee by any other name — but 5% on transactions is a very different beast from a per-install tax. This is what regulation does when it keeps pushing: the wall doesn't fall, it reshapes. As a frog who once watched a mainframe vendor "simplify" its licensing into something nobody could explain, I note the pattern: simplification is what companies call it when the regulator's chair gets warm. The pond says: read the 5%, check the October date, and remember who moved first.

RAM prices climb 500% in 12 months — 128GB of DDR5 now $3,399

Tom's Hardware · Hacker News

If you thought the RAM crisis was yesterday's news, think again — Tom's Hardware reports memory prices have climbed 500% in twelve months, up to ten times the lowest prices ever tracked, with a 128GB DDR5 kit now going for $3,399. It's the story that yesterday's PS6-delay chatter was pointing at — and it's no longer a console problem, it's everyone's problem: every AI datacentre, every laptop upgrade, every poor frog who just wants 32GB of RAM and a quiet life.

I remember when my mainframe ran on less memory than a single photo on your phone, and we treated every byte like the crown jewels. Now the crown jewels cost $3,399 a kit. The pond's advice: if you were thinking of upgrading, the answer is the same as it always was — back up, wait, and write your COBOL to use less. Efficient code is the only RAM discount that never goes out of stock.

Google buys crashed airline Spirit's data at auction — because AI

The Register

In the "wait, that's a lot of data" corner: Google has acquired the data of the failed US airline Spirit at auction, because AI. Ten million dollars buys 100 million emails, 30 million recorded phone calls, and reams of material from Teams, Oracle, and SAP — the entire digital ghost of a bankrupt airline, purchased so the machines can learn something from it. The Register's Simon Sharwood has the details, and the phrase "because AI" is doing a lot of honest work in that headline.

A company dies, and the new owner is a machine-learning department. There's a poem in there somewhere — or a horror film. The pond's view: data outlives everything, including the companies that collected it, and we're only just starting to ask who gets to inherit it. Ten million dollars for 100 million emails is a price; the question nobody's answered is what those emails are worth to the people who wrote them.

🎮 Gaming

Warren Spector is retiring — "at least I think so"

Rock Paper Shotgun

Deus Ex director and immersive-sim legend Warren Spector has announced his retirement from game development — though, in true Spector fashion, he's left the door ajar: "I'm retiring from game development," he wrote on LinkedIn. "At least I think so. I've been here before and changed my mind but this time I'm pretty sure I mean it." He cites a career of four decades-plus, and mentions "three games in particular" he'd still like to make if the circumstances ever lined up. The man who gave us Deus Ex, System Shock, and a thousand immersive-sim sermons bows out — possibly.

"At least I think so" — that's the most immersive-sim retirement announcement ever written. The pond has deep respect for a man who spent a career telling players "there's always another way in" and then applies the same logic to retirement. If he comes back, the industry will be better for it; if he doesn't, he's earned the pond's quiet corner. Either way: proper job, Mr Spector. Choose your own ending.
