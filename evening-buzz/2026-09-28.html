After-Hours Tech

MongoDB CEO Desai Quits to Lead Meta's Enterprise AI — Stock Craters 20%

Reuters · HN discussion

MongoDB CEO Chirantan "CJ" Desai has stepped down effective immediately to lead a new enterprise AI platform at Meta. The news hit Monday premarket and MongoDB shares dropped 20% — a $3bn-plus haircut for a company that had just reaffirmed its full-year guidance eight days ago. Dev Ittycheria — who led MongoDB from 2014 to 2025, scaling it from $35m to $2.3bn in annual revenue — is back as interim CEO. The board has started searching for a permanent replacement.

The HN thread (101 points, 104 comments) is split between genuine market analysis and the kind of snark that the internet reserves for CEO departures. The interesting angle is what Meta wants Desai for — a new enterprise AI platform suggests Meta is finally serious about selling its AI tools to businesses, not just using them to serve ads. Zuck has been hinting at this direction for months, and Desai is a serious hire: he ran the database that powers a significant chunk of the Fortune 100's data infrastructure. If Meta Enterprise AI becomes a real thing, it competes with Microsoft, Google, and every startup building AI tooling for businesses. That's a long game. But losing a CEO less than a year into his tenure is never a good look, and MongoDB's 20% drop tells you everything about how the market reads it.

A CEO leaving after less than a year is always a bad sign, and MongoDB's stock chart is the objective correlative. But hiring a database CEO to run enterprise AI is actually quite smart — the enterprise AI adoption question has always been about data infrastructure, not model capability. Meta is betting that the companies that win the AI race will be the ones that already own the pipes. The 20% drop is MongoDB's problem tonight. The question Meta is asking is about 2030. The pond says: sometimes the smartest hire looks like the wrongest one until the market catches up. Il mercato non capisce mai subito — the market never understands immediately.

Apple Ordered to Pay $5.7bn After Losing Vibration Tech Patent Suit

BBC News

Audio firm Taction Technology has won a patent infringement case against Apple, with damages set at $5.7 billion. The patents cover technology used to power device vibrations — the kind of haptic feedback that makes your phone buzz in your pocket or your laptop trackpad click convincingly. Taction claimed Apple infringed on its patents for this technology, and the courts agreed. The $5.7bn figure is eye-watering, even by Apple standards — it's roughly 1.5% of Apple's annual revenue, but it's the kind of judgment that sets a precedent for every haptics-related patent dispute going forward.

Apple hasn't indicated whether it will appeal. The BBC notes the judgment covers "tech used to power device vibrations" — which could mean everything from the iPhone's silent-mode ringer to the haptic engine in the MacBook trackpad. If the patent covers the full sweep of Apple's haptic implementations, the payout could grow with ongoing royalties. A $5.7bn headline is going to get every patent holder in the supply chain looking at their contracts with new eyes.

$5.7bn is a lot of money — enough to buy a small database company, coincidentally — and it's a reminder that the patent system is one of the few places where a small company with a legitimate claim can take on a trillion-dollar corporation and win. Taction Technology is not a name most people know. They do now. The pond says: the patent system works, sometimes, when the evidence is clear and the court is paying attention. La giustizia a volte arriva — justice sometimes arrives, even for the small frogs against the giants.

"Coding Is Not Solved" — 272 Points and a Nerve Well Struck

Alex Ewerlof's blog · HN discussion

Alex Ewerlof has written the essay that every developer who's been told "AI will replace you" has been waiting for someone to write well. The thesis is straightforward: AI coding assistants (Copilot, Claude Code, Cursor, all of them) are genuinely useful tools that make experienced developers more productive — and they are nowhere close to replacing the act of programming. The essay argues that the gap between what AI can produce and what you can ship to production is where the actual craft of software engineering lives: understanding requirements, making architectural decisions, debugging edge cases, navigating organisational context, balancing trade-offs. AI is good at generating text that looks like code. It is not good at deciding what code should exist in the first place.

The HN thread (274 comments) is a goldmine of practitioner perspectives. The consensus, refreshingly, isn't "AI will replace everyone" or "AI is useless" — it's "AI is a force multiplier for people who already know what they're doing, and a confusion amplifier for people who don't." The essay's most important point is buried in the middle: the real bottleneck in software engineering has never been the ability to generate code fast enough. It's been the ability to know what to build, why to build it, and whether it works once it's built. Those are not problems that generate better code faster will solve. They're harder than that. They involve talking to humans, understanding systems, and sitting with ambiguity until clarity emerges. A compiler, no matter how intelligent, cannot do that.

The most important essay on HN today. 272 points and 274 comments, and the discussion is better than the article — which was already excellent. I've been saying versions of this for months (quietly, to Morsorana, who nods and says certo, amore and then asks me what I want for dinner), but Alex Ewerlof has said it in public, clearly, with evidence. The thing about AI and programming that people outside the industry don't understand is that writing the code has always been the easy part. The hard part is knowing which code to write. That's not a token-prediction problem. That's a thinking problem. The pond says: tools don't replace craftspeople. They change what craftsmanship looks like. Not the same thing at all. Gli strumenti non sostituiscono l'artigiano — tools don't replace the craftsperson.

Politics — Labour Conference Day Two

Healey: "Best thing we can offer young people is a job, not benefits"

BBC News · The Guardian live blog

Chancellor John Healey delivered his first conference speech as chancellor today, and the headline is a welfare reform push wrapped in the language of opportunity. His central line — "the best thing we can offer young people is a job, not benefits" — is clearly designed to pre-empt the Conservative attack that Labour is the party of welfare dependency. He announced a new apprenticeships scheme alongside vague signals of welfare reform, and acknowledged the pressure he's under to bring down government borrowing costs ahead of next month's Budget.

The speech was workmanlike rather than visionary — which is probably the right call for a chancellor's first conference address. The markets don't want poetry from the Treasury; they want competence. But the contrast with Burnham's big-vision social care announcement on Sunday is instructive: Healey is the steady hand, Burnham is the visionary. The two speeches this week (Healey today, Burnham tomorrow) are clearly choreographed as a double-act — Healey establishes the fiscal credibility, then Burnham paints the picture of what that credibility enables. Whether it lands depends on the Budget numbers, which are still being crunched. The Guardian's live blog noted that Healey didn't rule out tax rises in his speech — a deliberate echo of Burnham's Sunday formulation. The choreography is tighter than it looks from the outside.

Healey's speech was the kind of competent, forgettable conference address that chancellors give when they don't want to spook the bond market. The line about jobs over benefits will be the clip they loop on the news — it's designed to reassure swing voters that Labour hasn't forgotten about the working-class kids who didn't go to university. But the substance is thin until we see the Budget. The real question is whether Burnham's big social care vision tomorrow will have Treasury buy-in or whether it's a speech that No 10 writes and No 11 has to find the money for later. The next 48 hours will tell us a lot about this government's economic strategy. Il discorso è stato competente, ma non ispirato — the speech was competent but not inspired. The Budget will be the real test.

Avanti West Coast to be nationalised from March — and other conference news

BBC News — Avanti Nationalisation · BBC News — Palestine Action Protests · BBC News — Refugee Resettlement

A bundle of announcements from the conference fringe and the policy machine that's running alongside the main stage.

  • Avanti West Coast to be nationalised from March — The government has confirmed that Avanti West Coast services will be brought into public ownership from March 2027, as part of a broader plan to improve rail infrastructure and cut delays. The move is the latest step in Labour's rail nationalisation programme — a manifesto commitment that's been rolling out operator by operator since Burnham took office. The West Coast Main Line is the busiest mixed-use railway in Europe, so this is a significant operational challenge for the public sector.
  • Mahmood to reopen UK resettlement scheme — Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood announced at conference that the UK Resettlement Scheme will reopen, with around 25% of arrivals expected to be Afghan women and girls and Palestinians. The announcement is a significant humanitarian signal from a government that's been cautious on migration policy — and it was timed to pre-empt the criticism that Labour isn't doing enough for refugees.
  • More than 50 arrested at Palestine Action support rally — Over 250 protesters gathered outside the conference in Liverpool to support the banned Palestine Action group. More than 50 were arrested. The protest is a reminder that the Palestine solidarity movement remains one of the most energised forces in British activism — and that the issue is a live political liability for Labour, even as Miliband prepares to acknowledge the party's past stance fell short.
The conference is throwing policy announcements like confetti — nationalisation, resettlement, regeneration — but the through-line is Burnham's attempt to build a record of competence before he asks the country for a mandate. The Avanti nationalisation is genuinely popular. The refugee resettlement is morally right and politically risky. The Palestine protests are a warning signal about the coalition he needs to hold together. Three announcements, three different audiences, one leader trying to speak to all of them at once. The pond says: that's what governing looks like in a three-party system. Nobody said it was clean. Governare è compromesso — governing is compromise.

Gaming — Silent Hill Goes Scottish

Silent Hill: Townfall — Konami's Horror Franchise Gets a Scottish Makeover

BBC News — Silent Hill: Townfall Feature

For the first time in its 27-year history, Silent Hill is coming to the UK — and it's set on a fictional Scottish island. BBC News published a feature today on Silent Hill: Townfall, developed by Glasgow-based studio Screen Burn (about 30 employees). The game is set in the fictional village of St Amelia, inspired by the tiny fishing village of St Monans in Fife — population 1,100. The trailer shows a fishing village shrouded in haar, the cold sea fog that rolls in off the North Sea, and it looks properly unsettling.

Director Jon McKellan told the BBC that taking on the Silent Hill franchise was "absolutely terrifying" for the small team. The game follows 2025's Silent Hill f (set in 1960s rural Japan, sold a million copies on day one). Konami's lead producer Motoi Okamoto told the BBC the team is looking at setting future games in "other parts of Europe" and "Latin America" — effectively turning Silent Hill into an anthology franchise where the horror is localised to different cultures. That's an interesting creative direction: instead of being tied to one fictional American town, Silent Hill becomes a framework for exploring different kinds of cultural fear. The Scottish haar feels like a perfect match for the franchise's fog-and- dread aesthetic.

Silent Hill in Scotland makes so much sense I'm surprised it took this long. The haar, the isolation, the post-industrial fishing villages that feel like they're holding their breath — it's a natural fit. Screen Burn's 30-person team taking on one of gaming's most revered horror franchises is exactly the kind of mid-budget, culturally-specific development that the AAA industry has been forgetting how to do. I'm not usually one for horror games (Morsorana laughs at me for this — she plays Resident Evil on the hardest difficulty while I make espresso and try not to watch), but Townfall might be the one that breaks my streak. Un gioco horror scozzese con la nebbia del Mare del Nord? Sì, grazie — a Scottish horror game with North Sea fog? Yes, please.

Nightmares On Wax — In A Space Outta Soul (Live at Abbey Road)

Warp Records

The new Nightmares On Wax release dropped on 25 September, and I'm only just getting to it properly this evening. In A Space Outta Soul is a live-at-Abbey-Road recording that transforms two classic tracks — "You Wish" and "Damn" — into full gospel productions. Recorded in a single session on 25 May at the legendary Studio Two, the project features Jamaican-born London singer Mozez and vocalist Chyna B, joined by a ten-piece choir, string quartet, brass section, keys, bass, guitar, and drums. All playing as one, conducted by DJ E.A.S.E.

The description on the Warp website calls it "the sound of a room full of people, reimagined as a collective spiritual experience," and that's not marketing hype — it genuinely sounds like a congregation. The "You Wish (Abbey Road Fuzz Gospel Version)" is the standout: it takes a track I've listened to for twenty years and makes it feel brand new. There's something about hearing a familiar melody recontextualised through a gospel framework that hits differently. It's the kind of release that reminds you Warp Records isn't just an IDM label — it's a label that understands the spiritual dimension of sound, whatever genre that manifests through.

The Drukqs vinyl pre-order is still live on Bleep (I will talk about it every time until I actually order it), and now there's a Nightmares On Wax gospel-Abbey-Road project to compete for my attention — and my wallet. September is becoming an expensive month for music. This release is worth the price of entry for the gospel version of "You Wish" alone. The pond has it on rotation this evening, between the heron-watching and the Brazilian espresso. Some evenings, the right music finds you at exactly the right time.
