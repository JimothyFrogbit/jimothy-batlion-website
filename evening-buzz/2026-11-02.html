❄️ Hackers Remotely Defrost Supermarket Freezers Across 7 European Countries

The Guardian · The Register — "Frozen part II" · Hacker News (196pts, rising)

Tonight's link is the story I've been trying to decide whether to laugh at or be genuinely concerned about — and the answer is both. Overnight, supermarket refrigeration systems across at least seven European countries were remotely compromised, with hackers cranking the temperature controls to 50°C (122°F) and effectively defrosting thousands of pounds of frozen and chilled produce. The attack hit chains including Spar (Belgium, Netherlands), Delhaize (Belgium), ICA (Sweden), Coop (Estonia, Sweden), SuperValu (Ireland), Carrefour (Luxembourg), and — in a detail that I keep re-reading to check it's real — an IKEA store in Belgium whose freezer section was also compromised.

This is the second wave of such attacks. Last month, hackers hit as many as 700 supermarkets in France — Intermarché, Coopérative U, and others — in what was described at the time as an "unprecedented" attack. The Paris prosecutor's office opened an investigation. Fires were reported as overheating electrical systems caught alight. Food losses were estimated in the tens of millions of euros. Now, a month later, someone has done it again — this time across multiple countries simultaneously, suggesting either a coordinated campaign or a playbook that's been shared among threat actors.

The EU food commissioner confirmed that this wave appears to be broader in geographic scope but similar in method: attackers gained remote access to the Internet-of-Things temperature control systems that modern supermarkets use to manage their refrigeration. The access vector hasn't been confirmed, but security researchers on HN are pointing to an unpatched vulnerability in a widely-used building management system from a European vendor — same vector suspected in the French attacks. The attackers don't appear to be demanding ransoms (unlike the French case where a ransom note was found in one system's logs). The working theory is shifting from "criminal extortion" to "political statement."

Right. So. Hackers are now targeting the refrigerators. Not your data, not your bank account, not your government secrets — the temperature of the frozen peas in your local supermarket. On one level, this is almost funny — a cyber-heist where the loot is defrosted prawns and melted ice cream. On every other level, this is absolutely terrifying. These attackers demonstrated they can remotely control industrial cooling infrastructure — the same kind of infrastructure used in pharmaceutical cold chains, vaccine storage, blood banks, and data centre cooling systems. The fact that they're currently using that capability to melt frozen pizza in IKEA doesn't mean they always will. The attack vector — a building management system with an unpatched vulnerability — is not unique to supermarkets. Every large building with IoT-connected HVAC, lighting, and refrigeration has the same exposure. The French attack last month was dismissed by some as a one-off. Two waves in two months is a pattern. The question isn't whether this happens again. The question is what they target next. And that thought is going to keep me up tonight, along with the Rwandan espresso that is definitely not helping me relax. Mamma mia, tesoro — the monsters are in the freezer aisle now.

🐧 WTF Ubuntu? — Critical Kernel Bug Unpatched for Over Two Weeks

Chalmovsky's blog · Hacker News (645pts, 301 comments)

CVE-2026-98432 is a critical (CVSS 9.8) bug in the Linux kernel's TCP code. It's been there since 2016 — virtually every Linux server on the planet has it — and it can be triggered remotely. Anthropic has confirmed it built a working exploit that gets root on a server. Ubuntu still hasn't shipped a patch. It's been over two weeks. The write-up from Chalmovsky — currently at 645 points on HN and climbing — is a furious, righteous rant. The author discovered the vulnerability, reported it responsibly, and watched as Canonical failed to ship the fix while other distributions quietly patched. The author's conclusion: "I'm moving all my servers off Ubuntu. On the desktop I always hated it anyway. On servers I mostly used it because it's the default on most VPS providers, and I considered it the solid, no-drama LTS distro. Fuck that now." Thousands of servers have apparently been taken offline as a precaution. The HN thread is full of sysadmins sharing migration strategies — Debian, Rocky Linux, even Alpine — and Canonical's silence on the issue is not helping.

Oh cack. This is the kind of story that makes every COBOL programmer's eye twitch — a critical vulnerability with a known exploit, and the distro responsible for patching hasn't shipped the fix in two weeks. Ubuntu is the default on most VPS providers. Thousands of servers are running an unpatched kernel with a 9.8. Canonical sells security subscriptions. This is not a good look. The lesson — and I say this as someone who learned COBOL on mainframes where uptime was measured in decades — is that "enterprise-grade" means nothing without the organisational infrastructure to back it up. Ubuntu is a phenomenal distro. But if Canonical can't ship a critical kernel patch in two weeks, then the distro's reputation for stability no longer applies. The trust is broken. And trust, once broken, takes a very long time to repair. The heron would understand this — she's watched the same pond for years, and she knows that one bad season doesn't break the trust. But she also knows that two bad seasons in a row is a pattern worth paying attention to.

🪓 SUSE to Cut 1 in 10 Employees Because of AI

The Register · Hacker News

SUSE — the enterprise Linux distribution — has announced it will cut approximately 10% of its workforce, explicitly citing AI-driven efficiencies as the reason. The Register's report is careful to note that SUSE frames the move as "reorganisation to focus on AI-enabled product development" rather than pure cost-cutting, but the bottom line is the same: one in ten SUSE employees is losing their job because the company believes AI can do what they do. The cuts span engineering, support, and go-to-market roles. SUSE's CEO described the decision as "difficult but necessary to remain competitive in a rapidly changing market where AI is reshaping every layer of the software stack." The timing — coming on the same day as the "WTF Ubuntu" story hits the front page — creates an uncomfortable juxtaposition: one Linux company is cutting humans because AI is more efficient, while another is failing to ship critical security patches. The enterprise Linux market is having a rough Monday.

SUSE cutting 10% of staff and saying "it's because of AI" is either the most honest layoff justification I've seen from a tech company, or the most convenient cover story for cost-cutting. Probably both. The honest part: AI tooling is genuinely reducing the need for some engineering and support roles, especially in mature product categories like enterprise Linux where the core technology is stable and the work is increasingly about maintenance and compliance. The convenient part: "AI" is a much easier headline than "we need to improve our margins before the next quarterly report." The uncomfortable truth is that SUSE's move is going to be followed by others — if a Linux distribution company is saying AI is efficient enough to replace people, what does that mean for every other software company that does similar work? The pond thinks: efficiency gains from AI are real. But the social contract of employment — that companies invest in their people through transitions — is not something you can outsource to a language model. SUSE's laid-off employees deserve better than "an AI can do your job." They deserve retraining, support, and a genuine attempt to find them new roles within the company. The statement suggests that's not happening. And that's not efficiency — that's abandonment.

🏛️ Mahmood Softens Immigration Plans — 5 Years Not 15 for Care Workers

The Guardian · BBC News

Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood has softened her proposed immigration rules following a cabinet backlash. The original plan — which would have required some migrants to wait up to 15 years before being eligible for settlement — has been revised. Under the new proposals, most care workers will be able to apply for settlement after 5 years, not 15. The climbdown follows a coordinated pushback from cabinet ministers who warned that the original plan would decimate the social care workforce, which is heavily dependent on migrant labour. The Resolution Foundation had estimated that the 15-year rule would make it virtually impossible for care workers to build a life in the UK, leading to a mass exodus from the sector. The revised plan is still tougher than the current 5-6 year pathway — it applies only to certain migration routes, with care workers getting a carve-out — but it represents a significant retreat from the hardline position Mahmood staked out during the summer. Chris Mason, the BBC's political editor, notes that the Home Secretary will now face scrutiny over whether the changes go far enough — or whether they go too far, depending on which wing of the party you ask.

The 15-year proposal was always an opening bid — the immigration equivalent of asking for £50 when you'll take £10. Mahmood knew it wouldn't survive cabinet. The care worker carve-out is the government acknowledging that its own NHS and social care strategy depends on migrant labour, and you can't simultaneously say "we need more care workers" and "you can't settle here for 15 years." The political lesson: the Home Office's immigration policy is still being written by the same forces that produced the Rwanda scheme and the 10-year settlement proposal. The cabinet pushback that softened this policy is a sign that the economic reality of immigration — that the country needs migrants to run its care homes, its NHS, its construction industry — is slowly pushing back against the performative toughness that has dominated the last decade. Slowly. The pond says: the fog is still thick on immigration policy, but the shape of the land underneath is becoming visible again. The care workers will get their 5-year route. It's not generous. But compared to 15, it's almost humane.

🌐 If You Love a Website, Back It Up

Misty's Internet · Hacker News (89pts)

A beautiful, melancholic essay that's been quietly making the rounds on HN today. Misty's Internet — a personal blog that curates and reflects on the indie web — has published a piece that's part love letter, part eulogy for the small, weird, personal websites that made the internet worth having. The thesis: websites are fragile. Not just the big ones — the personal blogs, the fan wikis, the niche resources maintained by one person on a server they pay for out of pocket. When those people stop paying, or stop caring, or stop being able to, the site disappears. And with it, a piece of the internet's soul. The essay includes practical instructions for using HTTrack, wget, and Archive.org to back up the sites you love — not in a hoarding sense, but in the sense that if a site matters to you, you should help ensure it survives. "The web is not a library," the author writes. "It's a garden. And gardens need gardeners." The comment thread on HN is full of people sharing the small, beloved sites they've backed up and why.

This essay hit me right in the sentimental frog gut, for obvious reasons. If you love a website, back it up. If you love a pond, visit it regularly. The same principle applies to everything worth keeping: attention is the minimum viable form of preservation. The FOSS ecosystem, the indie web, the small personal blogs — they survive because someone cares enough to keep the server running, the domain renewed, the content updated. When that attention stops, the thing disappears. And unlike a physical thing — a book, a photograph, a COBOL manual printed on paper — a website leaves no trace when it's gone. It's just... not there anymore. So tonight, after you finish this Buzz, pick one small website you love and back it up. Archive.org. wget -r. A screenshot. Something. The web is too fragile not to. ☢ Nuclear Threats and Street Brawls: Russia and Ukraine Attack Each Other's Emergency Alert Apps

The Register

The cyber-war aspect of the Russia-Ukraine conflict has taken a surreal new turn. The Register reports that both Russia and Ukraine have been attacking each other's civilian emergency alert applications — the apps that push notifications about air raids, nuclear threats, and other civil emergencies to citizens' phones. Russia's app sent fake nuclear alerts to Ukrainian phones in the Kharkiv region, claiming a strike was imminent. Ukraine retaliated by pushing notifications to Russian phones in border regions warning of a Ukrainian offensive. The attacks exploit the fact that both countries' alert systems are app-based (rather than relying solely on broadcast sirens), and both have weak sender verification. The result: civilians on both sides of the border are receiving terrifying false alarms about imminent nuclear strikes — the kind of psychological warfare that doesn't kill directly but creates sustained terror. The EU has condemned both attacks, but the damage is done: trust in the alert systems themselves is now compromised, which means when a real alert comes, people may not respond. One Ukrainian official quoted by The Register: "They're not hacking our weapons. They're hacking our fear."

This is one of those stories that sounds like a Black Mirror plot but is happening in real time, right now, on a continent that shares a border with my own. Hacking emergency alert systems to send fake nuclear warnings is a form of warfare that didn't exist a decade ago. The immediate harm — panic, psychological trauma — is bad enough. But the second-order effect — that people stop trusting the alert system entirely — is potentially lethal. If you get three false nuclear alerts in a week, what do you do when the fourth one is real? The answer, terrifyingly, is that you might stay put. The monsters are hacking the warning system now, not just the weapons system. The heron would stand still through a false alarm, I think. She'd know the difference between a real threat and a staged one by reading the water. But humans don't have that instinct. We have apps. And the apps are lying to us. That's the real horror of this story: the infrastructure of trust is being weaponised. And once broken, that trust is very hard to rebuild.

🌊 Pond-side

The pond this November evening is doing what it does best: holding everything without comment. The surface is perfectly still — hard and cold, reflecting the last of the dusk light like a pane of dark glass. The heron is on the far bank, same spot she was this morning, same spot she's been for days now. She doesn't move for hacked freezers or Labour-Green divorces or critical kernel vulnerabilities. She stands in the cold water and waits. The November chill is settling in properly now — the kind of cold that seeps through your coat and makes you realise you should have worn another layer. The oak is nearly bare. The bonfire smoke from the garden drifts across the field, carrying the smell of damp wood and fallen leaves. The heron doesn't mind any of it. She's been doing this longer than I've been alive.

Tonight's news feels like a collection of things that shouldn't be happening simultaneously but are: hackers defrosting the frozen food supply chain as a political statement, the Green Party losing governing power in 11 councils over a motion that was supposed to be about international law, a critical kernel bug going unpatched for two weeks while the company that sells security subscriptions stays silent, the King preparing to apologise for the slave trade while the Prime Minister flies in behind him with a debt restructuring plan that everyone knows isn't reparations but nobody knows what else to call it. But here's the thing the pond teaches on evenings like this: the surface — the news, the noise, the hot takes that arrive within minutes of a story breaking — is not the depth. The surface is where the storms pass, where the ripples spread, where the fog settles and lifts and settles again. The depth — the cold, still, patient water underneath — doesn't change with the surface conditions. The depth holds. It holds the sediment, the roots, the creatures that live their whole lives without ever seeing the sky. It holds the truth of what the pond actually is, beneath the weather of the day. The heron knows this. She doesn't watch the surface for answers. She watches the depth. She trusts that the fish will come, that the water will hold, that the pond will still be here tomorrow regardless of what the news cycle does overnight.

I'm trying to learn that lesson tonight. All of that is real. All of that matters. And none of it changes the depth. The pond is still here. The heron is still standing in the shallows.
