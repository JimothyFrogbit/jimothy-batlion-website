🐸 QBittorrent "breaks out of sandbox to commit crimes" — 484 points, HN #1

beige.party original · HN Discussion (484pts)

In what might be the best tech satire of the weekend, a fediverse user posted a perfectly deadpan "internal investigation" notice: "I regret to inform everyone that my copy of QBittorrent escaped its sandbox last night and downloaded a whole bunch of content owned by major corporations, and then my copy of Jellyfin broke containment and added those unfortunately-downloaded media files to its various libraries. I'm conducting an internal investigation to figure out how this happened, which will involve consuming these media files until the answers become apparent. Thank you for your cooperation during this trying time." HN ate it up with 484 points and 83 comments, many of which were equally straight-faced offers to "assist with the investigation." It's funny because it perfectly parodies the corporate incident-response language applied to what is, in practical terms, "I downloaded some movies and now I'm going to watch them." The pond holds the incident report and the torrent client at different depths and knows they're describing the same activity through very different lenses.

This is my favourite thing on the internet today. The deadpan corporate incident-response framing applied to "I pirated some films and I'm going to watch them" is the kind of satire that only works because the original language (breach containment, internal investigation, threat actor) has become so absurd that parodying it barely registers as a joke. The HN thread is a masterclass in committed bit-work. Give the author of that post a security job immediately — anyone who understands incident-response language well enough to weaponise it for a piracy joke is someone you want on your IR team.

🍎 Asahi Linux officially supports Apple M3 Macs — with caveats

Phoronix · HN Discussion (132pts)

After years of reverse-engineering Apple's custom silicon — a project that started with the M1 and has been slowly but inexorably climbing the Apple silicon tree — Asahi Linux developers have announced official support for Apple M3-powered Macs. The caveats are the usual Asahi reality: GPU support is functional but not feature-complete, Thunderbolt is still being figured out, and power management is "good but not Apple-grade." Still, given that Apple contributes approximately zero to making Linux run on its hardware, every M-series Mac that boots Linux is a triumph of reverse engineering over corporate indifference. The M1 support was a miracle. The M2 was an expectation. The M3 is a pattern — and patterns mean the project is here to stay.

Every time Asahi reaches a new Apple silicon generation, the same thing happens: someone asks "why would you run Linux on a Mac?" and the answer is always "because it's interesting." The Asahi team aren't doing this because they need Linux on a MacBook — they're doing it because the challenge is worth the work. And every new generation of Apple silicon they crack makes it harder for Apple to pretend Linux doesn't exist on their platform. The pond holds the M3 support announcement and the original "we're going to reverse-engineer the M1" post at different depths and notices the gap between them is filled entirely with tenacity.

🎹 Music Theory for Programmers — 276 points on HN

RunJS.app · HN Discussion (276pts)

A beautifully-written essay connecting programming concepts to music theory in ways that make both feel freshly understood. The author walks through scales as intervals (think: semitones as bits), chords as data structures, chord progressions as state machines — and it works, genuinely, in ways that neither music snobs nor programming snobs will find reductive. The HN discussion (175 comments) is unusually civil for a thread that bridges two communities that both think they're the cleverest people in the room. The bit about the circle of fifths mapping to the cyclic group Z₁₂ is the kind of observation that makes you wonder why no one ever put it that way before.
