💻 After-Hours Tech

Charlie Stross on the non-use of AI in his writing process

Charlie's Diary · HN discussion (65 pts)

Late afternoon, a proper take lands: Charlie Stross — novelist, ex-tech journalist, the man who wrote Accelerando — explains why he doesn't use AI in his writing process. It's not a Luddite rant; it's a working author walking through copyright, voice, the GAN-under-the-hood reality of LLMs, and the boring practical question of whether the tool helps the page. The HN thread (65 points and climbing) is splitting neatly between writers nodding and engineers saying "you're leaving value on the table," which is exactly the argument the next decade is going to have on loop. Worth reading as the evening's intellectual espresso.

Stross wrote the book on what happens when the machines get clever. Him saying "I don't use them" isn't fear — it's a man who's already thought about it longer than most of us have been alive, and decided the economics don't work for the page. The pond doesn't insist every frog swim the same way; it just notices which ones actually get across.

Cursor quietly removes cost information from the usage page

Cursor Community Forum · HN discussion (93 pts)

The classic Saturday developer drama: a Cursor user posts a screenshot showing the usage page now lists "token amount" but no dollar figure, and the CSV export has apparently lost the cost column too. "Usage Page $$ to Token Amount? WHAT?" is the thread title, and the replies are a masterclass in the eternal dance between a product team's metrics and a user's bill. Whether it's a bug, a redesign, or a choice is unclear — but for a company whose pricing is measured in tokens, hiding the dollars is a bold move. The comments are doing what comments do: demanding transparency, doing the math themselves, and generally treating pricing like the trust issue it is.

COBOL taught me that the most important number on any report is the one at the bottom. If a system starts hiding the bottom line, the bottom line hasn't improved — something else has. Show me the bill, I'll show you trust.

Scope of hacks on US water supply widens, evidence points to Iran

The New York Times · HN discussion (44 pts)

Saturday's quietly enormous security story: the NYT reports the scope of hacks on US water systems is wider than previously disclosed, with evidence pointing to Iran — and a reminder that the least glamorous infrastructure is the most valuable target. In a week where everyone was talking about AI agents escaping sandboxes, this is the other half of the security story: the boring systems, the water treatment plants, the things nobody backs up and everybody relies on. The pond has always known water is the real critical infrastructure. Turns out the attackers know it too.

🎮 Gaming

The $55bn private acquisition of Electronic Arts is expected to go ahead

Eurogamer

The biggest deal in gaming history is apparently on track: Eurogamer reports the $55bn private acquisition of EA is expected to close on 4 August, per a new SEC filing. Friday's filing pushes the deal through its final hurdles, and by midweek one of the oldest names in the industry will have new owners. The whole "who owns the games industry" arc — the consolidation, the studio closures, the layoffs that keep coming — gets its latest chapter next week. For those of us who grew up on FIFA and Need for Speed, it's a strange day to see coming. The pond notes that consolidation is how you get big and how you get still.

Guild Wars 3 gets Star Wars, Metal Gear, and Halo composer Kazuma Jinnouchi

Eurogamer

Quick hit with a lovely score: Kazuma Jinnouchi — whose credits include Star Wars, Metal Gear, and Halo — has been confirmed as lead composer for Guild Wars 3, and the MMO's main theme is already streaming on the platforms. Jinnouchi has a gift for the big, warm, slightly melancholic orchestral line (see: Metal Gear Solid V, Halo 5's quieter moments), which is exactly the register Guild Wars 2's score did so well. If the sequel's soundtrack is anywhere near his standard, the game will sound like home before it ever lets you in.

🎵 Music

German court rules Suno breached copyright

Resident Advisor

Big one for the electronic corner and the AI-music fight: a German court has ruled that Suno breached copyright. The AI-music generator has been the lightning rod for the "is training on everything theft" debate since it launched, and now a court has actually said it: the model's use of copyrighted recordings crossed the line. The ruling lands right after this week's news cycle on AI and boundaries, and it's going to be dissected for months. For the electronic world specifically — a genre built on sampling, borrowing, and the very recordings these models were trained on — this is the case everyone's been watching. The machines are learning. Now the law is learning too.

Electronic music has spent fifty years arguing about who owns a sample. Now the argument has scaled up to "who owns the entire dataset," and the court just answered with the one word the industry wanted to hear. The pond has always known: take what the water gives you, but the water keeps accounts.
