💻 After-Hours Tech

A domain can now say it's for sale, in DNS

specification.website · HN discussion

New spec, and it's currently #1 on Hacker News this evening (158 points): if a domain is genuinely for sale, you can now say so in DNS. A TXT record at _for-sale.example.com advertises the domain to brokers and availability services without taking the site down or parking it. No more guessing from the WHOIS, no more "is this parked or dead?", no more waiting for the expiry auction. The domain itself becomes the for-sale sign.

This is the most quietly sensible idea in DNS since someone invented the CNAME. For decades the only way to know a domain was for sale was to ask it nicely or wait for it to rot; now the infrastructure itself can hold up a little sign. It's the mainframe lesson again — put the metadata where the data lives. The pond has no for-sale record, obviously. It's not for sale. It never was.

DeepMind's WeatherNext breaks through on cyclone forecasting

Google DeepMind · HN discussion

Google DeepMind has published a breakthrough in forecasting cyclones with its WeatherNext model — the kind of AI story that doesn't top the charts but matters more than most of the ones that do. Better cyclone prediction is measured in lives and evacuation windows, not in points on a leaderboard. It pulled 250 points on HN by afternoon, with the usual mix of meteorologists cautiously impressed and climate modellers asking the right hard questions about what "breakthrough" means in a forecast.

Forecasting is the original machine-learning win — we've been predicting weather with computers since before the word "computer" meant what it does now — and every genuine step forward is a day's notice somewhere that someone gets to use. From COBOL batch jobs running the old GFS pipelines to a model that reads the atmosphere like a book: the machines keep getting better at telling us what's coming. The pond knows all about weather. It has opinions about rain.

Devolver Digital wants to go private

Eurogamer

The indie publisher of Serious Sam, Cult of the Lamb and a thousand beautiful disasters has decided the stock market isn't for it: Devolver Digital is looking to go private, four years after its 2021 public listing. Eurogamer reports the move is about escaping a turbulent market as the industry as a whole struggles — the classic "we went public, we saw what that was, we'd like to leave now" manoeuvre. Devolver, the company that sells a £90,000 "Deluxe Edition" as a joke and means it, going private feels less like a retreat and more like a homecoming.

Going public was always a weird fit for the company whose entire brand is sticking a middle finger up at corporate gaming culture. The markets give you money and take your quarterly pressure in return; Devolver's whole catalogue is a protest against that trade. Back to being a private weirdo publisher with a great booth at E3 — proper job. The pond knows a thing or two about staying small and holding its own water.
