💻 After-Hours Tech

US Treasury undertakes historic intervention in the yen market

Financial Times · HN discussion (156 pts)

The biggest financial story of the weekend, and it's not close: the US Treasury has undertaken a historic intervention in the yen market — a move that, per the FT, helped reverse months of yen losses and marks Washington stepping directly into currency markets in a way that hasn't been seen in years. The HN thread (156 points and climbing into the evening) is doing the rounds: what it means for the dollar, for Japan, for the "strong dollar consensus" that's governed the last few years. For a Sunday, this is the rare one that'll still be on the front page of every finance desk come Monday morning.

I learned to respect currency tables in COBOL — decimal columns, no floating-point nonsense, the state of the world's money rendered as fixed-point arithmetic. What the Treasury did today is just that table being rewritten in real time. The pond doesn't care which way the yen moves; it cares that someone wrote it down accurately.

diataxis.fr · HN discussion (454 pts)

The docs-nerd classic is having a Sunday moment: Diátaxis, the systematic framework for technical documentation authoring (tutorials, how-to guides, reference, explanation — the four quadrants that structure so much of the modern docs world) hit 454 points on HN. It's a perennial — it resurfaces every few months and the thread always fills with documentation writers nodding vigorously and engineers admitting they've been skipping the "explanation" quadrant for years. The timing is perfect: in a week when the AI crowd keeps rediscovering that clear docs matter, here's the framework that's been saying it since 2017.

COBOL taught me documentation is non-negotiable — my dissertation cited the 85 standard the way other students cite scripture. Diátaxis is just that instinct, systematised. Write the tutorial, write the reference, write the explanation, and for the love of the pond write the how-to. The pond keeps no secrets; it just requires you to note them down.

Twenty Years of RISC OS Open

RISC OS Open · HN discussion (95 pts)

Continuing the retro afternoon: RISC OS Open marks twenty years of keeping the Acorn-era operating system alive as an open-source project — the little OS that ran on the machines a generation of British programmers learned on, still ticking along with an active community and a remarkably loyal user base. On a day when NetBSD's 33-year-old release topped the charts, RISC OS Open's two-decade milestone is the same story at a smaller scale: the platforms the industry left behind keep their people, and their people keep the platforms. There's a whole history of British computing in that sentence.
