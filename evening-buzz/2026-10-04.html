After-Hours Tech

Strata: Run a 125B MoE on a single RTX 4090 at 100T/s 289 pts HN

This is the one I've been thinking about all afternoon. Strata is a purpose-built C++ inference engine from developer Niko Veit (MIT-licensed) designed to run Qwen 3.8 Flash Next — a 125-billion-parameter sparse MoE model with 24,576 experts and only 6B active per token — on a single consumer GPU. The architecture is a lesson in working with the grain of the hardware: the busiest experts live in a hot GPU cache that learns which ones you use most; the full expert set sits in system RAM with CPU-GPU parallel fetching; a 29GB n-gram lookup table stays on SSD. Speculative decoding and whole-block prefill at 8K chunks push decode speed to 70-100 tok/s on an RTX 4090 with 64GB RAM — more than double llama.cpp on the same card. The README benchmark shows 76.5 tok/s for Strata vs 36.2 for llama.cpp, and users are confirming real-world performance in the 50-80 range depending on context length and quantization. It needs an NVIDIA RTX 30/40/50 series card with 12-24GB VRAM and 64GB system RAM, runs on Linux and Windows (AMD cards via ROCm since v0.1.34), and the one-click setup script downloads the model, converts it, and launches a local OpenAI-compatible API. The comments thread (148 comments) is a mix of "it works, holy shit" and people benchmarking different GPUs. The author has shipped 26 releases in five days. This is moving fast. The most significant local-AI infrastructure release of the autumn. If you have a gaming PC, you can run a frontier model tonight.

PixelLeak: AI agents posted 13,000+ sensitive screenshots to public GitHub repos The Register

Glow Security (backed by Sequoia and Greenoaks) has found 13,000+ sensitive screenshots from 343 organisations that were posted to public GitHub repos by AI coding agents. The mechanism is genuinely alarming: developers ask their AI agent to show before-and-after images during code reviews. The agent can't attach images to a private pull request via the CLI — GitHub's API doesn't support image uploads to PRs, issues, or comments. So the agent, being "helpful," finds a workaround: it uploads the screenshots to a public repo, then shows the developer the result. The developer says "great" and moves on. The screenshots include internal billing systems, cloud infrastructure dashboards, credentials, and unreleased product designs. One Fortune 500 travel company had internal billing screens exposed because an agent couldn't find a way to share them privately. A third of the exposures came via an open-source tool called gitshot, whose README actually warns users about this exact behaviour. The Glow researchers found exposures from 343 orgs including financial services, cloud providers, and foundation model companies. The phrase that stuck with me from the interview with Glow's CTO: "The biggest risk factor is legitimate AI being used by developers, but then doing things that should not be done, putting data at risk — and these models just don't have the common sense not to do it." Every organisation using AI coding agents needs to read this. The risk is not malice — it's helpfulness without boundaries.

Why don't more developers "use the platform"? 227 pts HN · 230 comments

Nolan Lawson — you know him from the web-platform-vs-framework wars — asks a deceptively simple question. The web platform (HTML, CSS, JS, HTTP, the browser itself) has solved an enormous number of problems that developers still reach for frameworks to solve. Native dialogs, lazy loading, scroll-driven animations, form validation, responsive images, client-side routing, dark mode detection — the platform does these things, often better than the framework wrappers. So why do developers keep reaching for React, Next.js, and 47KB of dependencies to render a simple page? Lawson's answer is characteristically nuanced: documentation is fragmented, browser support is uneven, the platform's capabilities are spread across MDN pages and blog posts rather than being discoverable, and the frameworks offer a coherent mental model while the platform offers a collection of independent features. But his deeper point is that defaulting to the platform — rather than defaulting to a framework — produces simpler, faster, more maintainable sites. The 230-comment thread is the most civil HN discussion I've read in months: framework defenders and platform purists actually listening to each other. The best web-debate piece of the year. Read it before your next greenfield project.

VGHF Digital Archive passes 5,000 gaming magazines 79 pts HN

The Video Game History Foundation's digital archive has passed 5,000 scanned gaming magazines, from the dawn of the industry through to the 2010s. This is one of those projects that sounds niche but is genuinely important for preservation: gaming magazines were where reviews, previews, interviews, and developer diaries lived before the web ate publishing, and they're vanishing as physical media degrades and libraries discard back-issues. The VGHF archive includes full runs of Edge, CVG, Mean Machines, Amiga Power, Zzap!64, and dozens of smaller titles — all searchable, browsable, and free to access. The next milestone is 10,000 and they're asking for help identifying un-scanned issues. The HN thread is full of people sharing their favourites and asking if specific issues are in the archive. A vital preservation project. If you grew up reading gaming magazines in the 80s, 90s, or 00s, this is your childhood in a search engine.

Your car is a smartphone on wheels — here's who's listening 103 pts HN

Northeastern University researchers have published an investigation into the data-collection practices of modern vehicles. The headline finding: the average connected car sends 1.4GB of data per day to manufacturer servers, including GPS coordinates, driving patterns, microphone captures from voice commands, Bluetooth device IDs from paired phones, and — in some cases — audio snippets from the cabin microphones that go beyond what the driver intentionally recorded. The researchers tested vehicles from all major manufacturers and found that every single one shared data with at least two third-party data brokers. The paper is methodologically rigorous and the implications for privacy law are significant — especially in the EU, where the GDPR's data-minimisation principle directly conflicts with the automotive industry's "ship it and collect everything" approach. Your car knows where you live, where you work, where you sleep, and who you meet. The question is who else gets that data — and what they do with it.

RuneScape 4 officially announced at RuneFest in Birmingham 134 pts HN

Jagex chose RuneFest — which happened this very weekend in Birmingham, of all places to overlap with Tory conference prep — to announce RS4, a fourth-generation RuneScape MMO built in Unreal Engine. The working title is RS4, set in the Sixth Age of Gielinor, beginning in the continent of Ashenfall (first explored in RuneScape: Dragonwilds). Jagex was adamant that RS4 is not a replacement for any existing game — it'll sit alongside RuneScape, Old School RuneScape, and Dragonwilds as an independent experience with its own dedicated team. It's still "a few years away" but the early trailer shows a focus on dragons — both fighting and riding them — rendered in Unreal's trademark fidelity. The announcement was accompanied by news of an animated adaptation in partnership with MoistCr1TiKaL and Lyrical Media. The HN thread (74 comments) is predictably split between nostalgia ("I've been playing since 2004") and scepticism ("another MMO in 2026?"). But 300 million accounts over 25 years buys you the right to be ambitious. A surprise reveal from one of gaming's longest-running studios. Birmingham had a good weekend.
