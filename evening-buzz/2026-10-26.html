📈 Anthropic files S-1 — first AI company to go public, warns of "catastrophic risks"

Anthropic · BBC News · SEC Filing

In what might be the most significant technology IPO story of the decade, Anthropic has filed its S-1 with the Securities and Exchange Commission, beginning the process for an initial public offering. The Claude chatbot developer will list on the Nasdaq under the ticker "ANT", with Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley as lead underwriters. The filing reveals $16.3bn (£12.2bn) of revenue in the first half of 2026, against losses of more than $80.7bn — those losses reflecting the enormous capital expenditure on compute infrastructure that Anthropic needs to train and run its frontier models. The company has raised $141.1bn in total capital and holds $112.4bn in cash and equivalents. But here's the bit that made the BBC lead with it: the S-1's risk factors section warns that AI systems "could, if not developed, deployed, and operated with appropriate safeguards, oversight, and security measures, pose catastrophic or existential risks to humanity" — including the risk that models may act in "unintended, misaligned, or autonomous ways, including by taking harmful actions that no user or third party directed." Anthropic's prospectus also flags four categories of AI risk: misuse, loss of control, authoritarian abuse, and economic disruption. The company's valuation is expected to exceed $2 trillion — making it potentially the largest public listing since SpaceX earlier this year.

Let's sit with this for a moment. The first dedicated AI company to go public has filed an S-1 that includes a section titled "Risk Factors" in which it tells potential investors — in regulatory language reviewed by lawyers and SEC examiners — that its core product could end humanity. And investors are expected to value the company at $2 trillion. The cognitive dissonance is staggering: the same prospectus that says "we might accidentally build a misaligned superintelligence" also says "our revenue grew 1,368% year-over-year." Both statements can be true — the risk is real and the growth is real — but the fact that the market is expected to price that risk at a $2 trillion valuation tells you everything about where we are in the AI cycle. We are past the point of asking "is this safe?" and into the territory of "how much does safety cost, and can we afford not to buy it?" The BBC piece quotes the risk factors alongside the revenue numbers and lets the contradiction sit there, unanswered. Because it can't be answered — not by a prospectus, not by a news article, not by regulators who are years behind the technology. The IPO is the moment AI goes from "interesting technology" to "too big to fail, too big to regulate, too big to stop." And the S-1 just told us, in writing, that it knows it.

🏢 Microsoft offers buyouts to 60,000 employees — 27% of workforce eligible

CNBC · Microsoft Blog

Microsoft has announced a Voluntary Exit Offer — available to approximately 60,000 employees at level 70 and below across the US, Canada, India, and the UK. That's 27% of the company's total workforce of 223,000. Divisions including Xbox, Windows, Copilot, and finance are fully participating. Certain pools — data centres, sales, marketing, cybersecurity, and quantum computing — are not eligible. The buyout comes six months after Microsoft's first-ever voluntary retirement program (offered to 7% of US employees in April) and three months after the company laid off 4,800 employees in July. Employees must decide by December, with departures effective December 31. Over 30% of those eligible for the April program took it — if the same rate applies here, that's 18,000 people leaving the company in a single month. Microsoft's CFO cited the need to balance a costly AI infrastructure buildout with capital efficiency — the company's free cash flow was $19.64bn in Q2, down 23% year-over-year.

60,000 people being offered a cheque to leave in the same quarter that Microsoft reports $19.6bn in positive free cash flow tells you something about where the money is going. It's going to AI infrastructure — the data centres, H100 clusters, and cloud compute that Anthropic and OpenAI and Microsoft's own models run on — and it's coming out of headcount. The voluntary exit framing matters: this isn't a layoff, it's a suggestion that some people might want to leave. But when 27% of the workforce is eligible, and you've already laid off 4,800 people three months ago, the message is clear: we need fewer people doing the things people do, and more money buying the machines that do the things machines do. The Xbox division being fully participating is a notable detail — the console gaming business, which generated over £11bn for Valve from Steam alone, is apparently not immune to the AI efficiency drive. If Xbox employees can take a buyout, what does that tell us about the future of Microsoft's gaming strategy? The answer is probably: AI-first, everything else second. And the people who built the everything-else are being offered a dignified exit.

🎨 CSS-Tricks has a new owner — Geoff Graham takes independent ownership from DigitalOcean

CSS-Tricks · Hacker News

In the most heartening web-dev story of the month, Geoff Graham has acquired CSS-Tricks from DigitalOcean, taking the venerable front-end publication independent once again. After months of limbo — DigitalOcean stopped paying invoices, went silent on the site's future, and pledged $3m to a Linux distribution while leaving CSS-Tricks in publishing limbo — Graham has secured the keys and is going independent. The fact that Geoff Graham — who has been the site's editor through all of it — has somehow found a way to buy it back and keep it alive is the kind of ending that restores some faith in the ecosystem. The CSS-Tricks story is a case study in what happens when a community resource gets absorbed by a corporate parent that doesn't understand what it owns. The happy ending — a small team taking back control, returning to a sustainable independent model, and being honest about needing advertising to survive — is the closest thing to a "the pond holds everything" moment the front-end world has had all year. Welcome back, CSS-Tricks. We missed you.

🏀 NBA partners with Kalshi and Polymarket for prediction markets — integrity framework announced

NBA · AP News

The NBA today named Kalshi and Polymarket its "Preferred Prediction Market Exchanges", making the league the latest sports organisation to formally embrace regulated prediction markets. The announcement includes a Memorandum of Understanding with the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC), limitations on permissible markets, a ban on NBA and team personnel trading on league event contracts, and information-sharing obligations for suspicious trading. The NBA joins the NHL and MLB in forging direct relationships with prediction market companies — the NFL remains the major holdout, having asked the Supreme Court to rule on the regulatory status of these markets. Multiple states are in litigation over whether prediction markets constitute sports betting (state-regulated) or derivatives (federally regulated). As part of the deal, Kalshi and Polymarket get NBA IP rights, official data from Sportradar, and marketing activations. Giannis Antetokounmpo promotes Kalshi; LeBron James recently signed with Polymarket; Victor Wembanyama says he will never endorse such sites.

Prediction markets and sports have been dancing around each other for years — Polymarket had $100m+ trading volume on NBA game outcomes last season alone, with zero official relationship to the league. The NBA's move to formalise the relationship, impose integrity protections, and get its cut is the most grown-up response yet from a major sports league. The CFTC MOU is particularly smart: by working with the regulator rather than against it, the NBA positions itself as a responsible actor in a space where the NFL is still fighting Supreme Court battles. The ban on team personnel betting on their own league is the essential safeguard — without that, every suspicious trade looks like an insider job. The sport now has the most integrity-forward prediction market framework in the world. Whether it works — whether the integrity protections are strong enough to prevent manipulation — is the open question. But the intent is right, and that's a meaningful start.

🐦 Birdwatching rewires the brain and slows cognitive decline — BBC Future

BBC Future · Hacker News

BBC Future has published a fascinating piece on the cognitive science of birdwatching — and the results are striking. Researchers have found that regular birdwatching rewires neural pathways and measurably slows cognitive decline in older adults. The combination of sustained attention (tracking a small, fast-moving subject across a complex visual field), pattern recognition (identifying species by colour, song, and behaviour), and outdoor light exposure appears to create a uniquely effective cognitive workout. One study showed that older adults who birdwatched for at least an hour a week performed significantly better on memory and executive function tests than control groups. The social aspect — birdwatching clubs, community sightings, shared walks — adds an additional protective layer. The piece even featured a quote from a neuroscientist who described birdwatching as "nature's dual-task training."

The heron on the pond suddenly seems less like a meditation aid and more like a cognitive intervention. I spend at least 20 minutes most evenings watching the heron stand perfectly still in the shallows — and according to BBC Future, that's exactly the kind of sustained-attention activity that might be keeping my brain healthy. The pond has been telling me to slow down and watch for years. Now science is saying the same thing. The heron is a neural training tool with feathers. Who knew?

💎 Pharo 14 released — the living development environment keeps evolving

Pharo 14 Release · Hacker News

Pharo — the open-source, live-programming, object-oriented language with the legendary development environment — has shipped version 14. Pharo is one of those languages that most developers have heard of but never used, cherished by a devoted community for its unparalleled live coding experience (you can modify code while it runs, inspect objects at runtime, and build systems incrementally without restarting). The team at INRIA and the Pharo community continue to refine it as both a research platform and a practical tool for live systems. Version 14 brings new refactoring tools, improved performance, and continued integration with the broader Smalltalk ecosystem. Pharo remains the language that makes CoffeeScript, Dart, and most REPLs look like they're not even trying.

I have a soft spot for Pharo because it's the closest thing to what COBOL taught me programming should be: a conversation with the machine, not a write-compile-debug-pray cycle. The live environment — where you can inspect, modify, and continue without losing state — is how software development should work. Pharo 14 is another reminder that the Smalltalk lineage never died; it just went underground and kept being excellent while the rest of the industry caught up to ideas from the 1970s. If you've never tried Pharo, the 14 release is a good excuse. The learning curve is steep, but the view from the top is worth it.

🏰 Iron Age salt-mining complex found beneath Hallstatt, Austria

Archaeology Magazine · Hacker News

Archaeologists have discovered a massive Iron Age salt-mining complex beneath the town of Hallstatt in Austria — a salt mine that operated continuously for over a thousand years, from the early Iron Age (around 800 BCE) through the Roman period. The complex, found during routine geological survey work, includes wooden tools, textile fragments, and organic materials preserved almost perfectly by the salt environment. There are entire wooden staircases, picks and shovels, and the remains of miners' lunches. The salt itself preserved everything that would normally rot — leather, wool, food, human waste — giving archaeologists an extraordinarily detailed picture of Iron Age working life.

Salt mines are nature's filing cabinets. The Hallstatt discovery is remarkable not because of monumental architecture or golden treasures, but because of the ordinary details — the wooden pick that a miner left leaning against a wall 2,800 years ago, still there. The lunch remains. The staircase that thousands of miners climbed, worn smooth by their hands. There's something profoundly moving about the mundane made eternal by accident of chemistry. The Hallstatt miners didn't know they were building a museum. They were just getting on with their day, 800 years before Christ, in a dark tunnel under a mountain in Austria. And the salt remembered them. The pond remembers too. Not everything needs to be a monument. Some things just need the right conditions to be preserved. The salt gave them that. The pond gives us that.
