Gemini 4 Argon — 1406 points and the story of the day

Google DeepMind · Introducing Gemini 4 Argon · Hacker News (1406pts)

DeepMind released Gemini 4 Argon yesterday — their new frontier model — and it's sitting at 1,406 points on HN with the kind of energy that only happens when Google ships something genuinely impressive. Argon is positioned for "complex workflows across real-world software engineering, enterprise knowledge work, and cybersecurity defense." That's Google's careful product language for: this thing can write code that compiles, navigate enterprise document landscapes, and find vulnerabilities before they're exploited. The pricing is aggressive — $2 per million input tokens, $10 per million output through November 30, then $4/$20 after that. Cached tokens at 95% off. That's cheaper than GPT-4o and cheaper than Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and the benchmarks (what little Google has published) suggest it's competitive with both. The HN thread at 1,406 points is the usual mix of benchmark scepticism, pricing analysis, and genuine curiosity about whether this is the first Gemini model that actually lives up to the demo videos. The comments that stand out are the ones from developers who've actually tested it: "it's the first Google model that doesn't feel like it was trained on a different definition of 'code' than the one I use" is a sentiment I've seen repeated in multiple independent threads. That matters more than any benchmark number. The Gemini 4 Argon launch feels different from the previous Gemini launches — less hype, more substance, and a price point that makes you think Google is serious about winning the API pricing war. The 1,406 points on HN tell me the developer community is paying attention in a way they weren't for earlier Gemini releases. The question — as always with frontier models — is whether the reliability holds up at scale. The introductory pricing is designed to get people to find out. I suspect Google knows what it's doing.

Ledger confirms spy chip implant behind $86M in crypto thefts (769pts HN)

BleepingComputer · CoinDesk · Hacker News (769pts)

Ledger has confirmed that an unauthorised hardware implant — a "spy chip" embedded inside the enclosure of certain Ledger devices sold through Southeast Asian reseller CryptoBilis — was responsible for transmitting recovery phrases over the LTE mobile network. The implant's microcontroller reads the 24-word recovery phrase as it appears on the device screen during setup, decodes it, and beams it out via a concealed eSIM. Investigator Specter estimates losses may exceed $86 million across Bitcoin, Ethereum, and Tron. MediaMarkt Saturn — Germany's largest electronics retailer — has stopped selling Ledger devices after one was found with the implant at Singapore Airport. Ledger is advising any customer who bought from CryptoBilis not to initialise the device, and anyone who already has should move their assets to a newly generated wallet on a clean device. The HN thread at 769 points is full of people doing the only sensible thing: checking their device serial numbers, verifying their purchase history, and wondering how long this has been going on. The supply chain attack vector — intercept a batch of devices at a reseller, implant a chip, re-seal the packaging — is terrifyingly simple and almost impossible for the end user to detect. This is the nightmare scenario for hardware wallets: a supply-chain-level compromise that the user cannot detect with any tool they have access to. The LTE eSIM implant is specifically chosen because it doesn't need to pair with the user's phone — it phones home on its own. The $86M figure (if accurate) suggests this wasn't a small operation — it was a well-funded, multi-month supply chain infiltration with a clear ROI. The crypto security community is going to be tearing apart devices with X-ray scanners for the next six months. The real question is: how many other batches were compromised that we haven't found yet?

Amazon pushes up prices all across the internet (475pts HN)

Not-Ship (Amanda Shendruk) · Hacker News (475pts)

Amanda Shendruk at Not-Ship published a devastatingly clear breakdown of how Amazon's fee structure — and its Fair Pricing Policy — forces prices up everywhere, not just on Amazon. The mechanism is elegant and brutal: Amazon charges sellers nearly 50% of every sale in combined fees (commission, fulfillment, advertising). Sellers have to raise prices to cover it. Then Amazon's Fair Pricing Policy punishes sellers who offer lower prices anywhere else — including their own websites — by removing the Buy Box or suspending them entirely. So sellers raise prices everywhere. The FTC's upcoming antitrust case against Amazon centres on exactly this dynamic: Amazon uses its market power (40% of US online retail) to force sellers into a pricing straitjacket that inflates prices across the entire internet. As Shendruk puts it: "We pay Amazon prices, even when we're not shopping on Amazon." The piece is a follow-up to her earlier article on Amazon's search page manipulation, and it's exactly the kind of clear, independent journalism that the HN audience rewards. 475 points, well deserved. The Amazon pricing story is one of those things that feels obvious once you read it but is nearly impossible to prove without someone doing the work of stitching together the fee disclosures, the policy documents, and the seller testimony. Shendruk has done that work. The Fair Pricing Policy is the real smoking gun — it's Amazon admitting, in its own terms of service, that it needs to suppress price competition to maintain its own margins. If the FTC wins its case, the remedy would reshape e-commerce pricing for a generation. If Amazon wins, this becomes the playbook every dominant platform follows. The trial is next year. Watch this space.

AOSP source releases are so late developers are disassembling libraries to report bugs (443pts HN)

GitHub (android/ndk#2260) · Hacker News (443pts)

An Android 17 bug report that reads like a detective story: wcscspn() and wcspbrk() in bionic libc treat every wide character >= U+0100 as a match on the September Pixel builds. That means any app processing emoji, CJK characters, Cyrillic, or Greek text gets wrong positions back from these functions. A developer found the regression, but couldn't inspect the source because Google's AOSP releases lag behind what's actually running on devices — the public June sources have a correct implementation, but the September production binaries have different code. So they disassembled the shipped libc.so to find the faulty branch. The disassembly revealed three code paths — a simple compare, a 256-byte boolean table, and a fallback — and the table path had a bug where any character >= 0x100 was treated as a match. Visual Pinball on Android (a Wine-based VBScript engine) was already broken by this: comments with emoji caused the lexer to misreport line endings. The HN thread has become a wider discussion about what "open source" means when the source you're given doesn't match the code you're running. 443 points. The AOSP source delay story hits close to home for anyone who's ever debugged a production issue by staring at source that doesn't match the binary. The developer who disassembled libc.so to find this bug did it because they had no other option — but they shouldn't have had to. Google's argument for delayed AOSP releases is that they need to protect security research and give partners time to integrate. But when the delay means developers can't debug regressions that affect real users, the trade-off has shifted. Open source isn't open if the source is a historical artifact that doesn't reflect what your users are running.

Burnham rules out coalition with Greens after 'Zionism is racism' motion

The Independent · BBC Politics

Prime Minister Andy Burnham used PMQs to deliver the strongest signal yet that Labour will not work with the Green Party in any future arrangement — ruling out both coalition and confidence-and-supply — after the Green Party conference passed a motion declaring "Zionism is racism." Burnham's language was direct: "It is antisemitic, and this side of the house will have nothing to do with it." The motion, pushed through by Green Party leader Zack Polanski's faction, has become the defining issue of the party's current crisis — the investigation into its drafting opens this week, the party's governing body meets to discuss disciplinary procedures, and now the leader of the opposition has publicly declared the party beyond the pale for any future cooperation. For Labour, the calculation is straightforward: the Greens are a marginal electoral threat (five MPs) but a significant reputational risk — any association with a party that has declared a foundational tenet of Jewish identity to be racist would be weaponised by the Conservatives and Reform UK. For the Greens, the statement removes any ambiguity about their political isolation. The question is whether Polanski's faction sees isolation as a price worth paying for ideological purity — and whether the rest of the party agrees. Burnham's statement is the political equivalent of closing a door that was already mostly shut. The Greens were never going to be in coalition with Labour under current leadership — the gap between Polanski's radical-left positioning and Burnham's centrist consolidation is too wide. But formalising it as a public declaration matters: it tells every Green Party member that the current trajectory leads to permanent opposition, not just in government but in the progressive alliance too. The party's governing body meets this week. The members who want to reverse course now have a very clear consequence to point to.

Argentina lodges legal claim against UK's Falklands oil exploration

BBC News

Argentina has begun legal action at the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea, seeking to block the Sea Lion oilfield project north of the Falkland Islands — a joint venture between Britain's Rockhopper Exploration and Israel's Navitas Petroleum containing an estimated 1.7 billion barrels of oil. The claim follows a two-week ultimatum from President Javier Milei, who called the British-licensed exploration "the illegal plundering of our resources." UK Foreign Office minister Uma Kumaran has criticised Buenos Aires' "escalating campaign" against companies operating in the Falklands and announced new protections for them. The legal action is the latest chapter in a dispute that has simmered since the 2013 referendum (99.8% of Falklands residents voted to remain British) and the 1982 war before it. The window for diplomacy before the legal process takes over is narrowing. The Falklands oil dispute is one of those stories that runs in the background for years and then suddenly escalates when someone files a legal claim. The Sea Lion field is genuinely significant — 1.7 billion barrels is enough to transform the Falklands economy and change the strategic calculus of the South Atlantic. Argentina's legal strategy is interesting: by going through the Law of the Sea tribunal rather than the UN, they're trying to frame this as a resource dispute rather than a sovereignty one — a deliberate choice that avoids the 2013 referendum result and focuses on maritime boundaries. Smart legal positioning. Whether the tribunal accepts that framing is another question entirely.

BBC Politics

After Dark

A 900-page pre-Pantone guide to colour from 1692 (245pts HN)

Open Culture · Hacker News (245pts)

A. Boogert's 1692 watercolour manual — the single known copy, held at Bibliothèque Méjanes in Aix-en-Provence — has resurfaced in the public consciousness via a high-resolution digital scan that lets you flip through over 700 pages of hand-painted colour swatches. The book — Klaer Lightende Spiegel der Verfkonst (roughly: "Clearly Lighting Mirror of the Painting Art") — is a watercolourist's guide written before Newton's Opticks, before Pantone, before anything resembling a standardised colour system. Boogert drew on an Aristotelian system of colour, mixing his own pigments and documenting how to shift tones with "one, two, or three portions of water." The pages look like a Pantone catalogue from an alternate timeline where colour standardisation happened in a Dutch workshop instead of a New Jersey printing plant. The HN thread at 245 points is full of designers, artists, and typography nerds losing their minds over colour samples painted by hand in 1692. One commenter: "this is what the internet was made for." Hard to argue. The 1692 colour guide is the kind of thing that makes me believe in digital preservation as a genuine cultural good. A single manuscript, one copy in the world, sitting in a French library for centuries — and now anyone with an internet connection can page through Boogert's colour swatches and wonder what he would have thought of Pantone, of hex codes, of a world where every colour has a number and a standard and a factory in New Jersey making sure it's exactly the same every time. Boogert made one book. He didn't know if anyone would use it. He made it anyway. That's the kind of energy I respect.
