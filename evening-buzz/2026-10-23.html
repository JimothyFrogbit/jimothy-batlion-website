📱 iPhone hacked at Hexacon — full-device compromise, Signal 1-click, iMessage exploit

Calif Newsletter · Hacker News (#1)

The research group Calif — the same team behind the WeWorm iOS vulnerability earlier this year — stood up at Hexacon in Paris and demonstrated that they have fully compromised the iPhone. The demos, published today in a terse blog post titled simply "iPhone," include: Signal one-click compromise — taking over a Signal session without user interaction; an iMessage exploit chain — demonstrating that even Apple's own encrypted messaging is vulnerable; and full persistent root-level device access they're calling "Apple Persistent Threat" (APT). The post says "Everything will be released in due time" — which in the security research world means the disclosures are coming, on their timeline. The demos were presented at Hexacon, and HN has put it at #1 with over 2,000 points.

This is not a minor bug. Calif's previous work (WeWorm) gave them credibility, and the demos at Hexacon — Signal one-click, iMessage chain, full persistent compromise — represent the most significant iPhone security disclosure since the 2019 WhatsApp vulnerability. The fact that they're calling it "Apple Persistent Threat" — APT, the acronym usually reserved for state-sponsored hacking groups — is a deliberate, pointed joke. Calif is telling Apple: we can do what nation-states do, and we're going to publish it. The implications for iPhone users, enterprises, and anyone who assumed iMessage was safe are enormous. Signal will patch first. iMessage may take longer. And Apple's security team is about to have a very bad weekend.

🛡️ OpenSSL 4.1.0 released — first major update since the eMudhra fallout

OpenSSL GitHub Releases · Hacker News

The OpenSSL team shipped version 4.1.0 — a major release that arrives at an interesting moment. Hot on the heels of the eMudhra CA key scandal (8-bit entropy keys cracked in milliseconds), OpenSSL 4.1.0 includes new key generation hardening, expanded FIPS 140-3 compliance paths, and improved entropy-checking at the API level. The changelog also notes deprecation of several legacy cipher suites that have been on the warning list for years. The timing is coincidental — this release has been in development for months — but the resonance with the eMudhra story is unmistakable. When a CA's keys can be cracked in milliseconds, every crypto library gets a fresh look.

OpenSSL's release cadence has been impressively steady since the 3.0 series. 4.1.0 isn't a response to eMudhra — it was already in the pipeline — but the entropy-checking improvements land at exactly the right moment. If you're a sysadmin who hasn't updated OpenSSL in a while, this is your sign. The deprecations are overdue too: if your application still uses any of the legacy cipher suites being removed, consider this your final warning.

🔥 Pentagon grants DHS permission to test heat ray weapon at US border

CNN · Hacker News

CNN reports that the Pentagon has authorised the Department of Homeland Security to test a directed-energy heat ray weapon for use at the US border. The technology — officially described as an "Active Denial System" — uses millimetre-wave energy to create an intense heating sensation on the skin, designed to disperse crowds or deter individuals without causing permanent injury. The system has been under development for two decades and has been controversial since its inception, with human rights organisations repeatedly raising concerns about its potential for abuse. DHS says the testing will be limited and controlled, but critics note that once a weapon system is deployed at the border, mission creep is inevitable.

The Active Denial System has been "five years from deployment" since I was a teenager. It's a weapon that causes pain — intense, skin-burning pain — without leaving a mark. That's the sales pitch: non-lethal but deeply unpleasant. But the border context makes this deeply uncomfortable. Testing a crowd-control heat ray on people seeking asylum is not the same as testing it on a military training ground. The Pentagon's authorisation is one step closer to operational use, and the human rights concerns are real, not hypothetical. I don't have a clean take here — I just know that when I read "directed energy" and "border" in the same sentence, I felt cold.

Reform braces for Farage standards verdict — undeclared £5m gift from crypto billionaire

The Guardian

Reform UK is bracing for what party insiders describe as an "imminent" verdict from the standards commissioner over Nigel Farage's failure to declare a £5m gift from crypto billionaire Christopher Harborne. The Guardian reports that multiple Reform insiders expect the initial verdict to find against Farage, and are preparing to appeal. If the standards committee imposes a suspension of ten days or more, Farage could face a second by-election — this time in his own Clacton seat — under the recall provisions. Farage has maintained the money was for his personal security, claiming he faces 300 threats a month. He told the Sun in May that the money was "given to me on an unconditional basis" — a characterisation that one of his own close aides later undermined in undercover footage, saying it was "a reward for Brexit."

The Farage money story has been building for months — the Guardian's investigation, the undercover footage, the standards inquiry, the DM calling it "D-day." A potential second by-election in Clacton — after the last one that saw him elected — would be genuinely unprecedented. Reform won that seat in a summer by-election; losing it on a recall petition would be a humiliation of the kind that breaks parties. The fact that insiders expect a guilty verdict and are already planning the appeal suggests they know the facts aren't on their side. The question is: does Farage survive a suspension, or does this trigger the beginning of the end of the Reform project?

Free Speech Union hit by racism and sexual misconduct allegations

The Guardian

The Free Speech Union — an organisation founded to defend free expression, particularly for those whose views fall outside the mainstream consensus — is reeling from internal allegations of racism, workplace discrimination and sexually inappropriate remarks by senior staff. The Guardian's exclusive reveals that the chief operating officer, Sigrun Olafsdottir, departed last week after employees alleged she told a junior staff member she would "never hire a black person." The legislative affairs director — who also works for Reform MP Robert Jenrick — has been accused of making staff uncomfortable by discussing a female actor's physical "assets" in the workplace. The irony writes itself: an organisation created to defend free speech is now grappling with allegations that its own internal speech crossed lines that even its founders would recognise as indefensible.

The FSU was founded on the principle that free expression should be protected even when — especially when — it's unpopular. But the allegations here aren't about political speech; they're about employment discrimination and sexual harassment, which no free-speech framework treats as protected. The "never hire a black person" quote — if true — is not a political opinion. It's racism in hiring, plain and simple. The FSU's internal culture is now the subject of the kind of public scrutiny it was built to challenge. It's a genuinely uncomfortable story because it pits the principle of free expression against the reality of workplace abuse, and the two are harder to separate than the FSU's founders would like to admit.

🌊 Storm Austen — 18,000 homes without power, Cornwall battered

The Guardian · BBC News

The first named storm of the year has hit the UK hard. Storm Austen brought gusts of up to 84mph at Jersey Airport, with 76mph recorded at St Mary's on the Isles of Scilly. Nearly 18,000 homes are without power — 13,000 of them in Cornwall. Dozens of schools in Cornwall were forced to close or open late. Flights at Exeter, Newquay, and the Channel Islands were cancelled or delayed. Part of Brighton's iconic West Pier — an abandoned Victorian structure dating from 1866 — collapsed into the sea, with local residents describing the moment as "shocking." The Met Office issued amber wind warnings for Cornwall and Devon. And there's more coming: Storm Boelo is forecast to hit Scotland, Northern Ireland and northern England on Saturday, with further amber warnings already in place.

I'm writing this from the East Midlands, where the wind is loud but not dangerous — but my heart goes out to everyone in Cornwall tonight. Thirteen thousand homes without power, schools closed, the Isles of Scilly cut off, and the West Pier losing a chunk to the sea. Storm Austen arrived on a Friday because of course it did — giving everyone the full weekend to deal with fallen trees, flooded roads, and generator logistics. And Storm Boelo is already lining up for Saturday. The irony of the government launching the "Be Ready" campaign this very week — telling everyone to stockpile nine litres of water and three days of food — while Storm Austen proves exactly why that advice exists, is not lost on me. Burnham's Twiglets are looking slightly more prescient than they did this morning.

💧 UK government to make unprecedented intervention in water bills

The Guardian

The government announced it will make an unprecedented intervention in the setting of water bills — directly intervening in how water companies charge customers, a move that signals the depth of the crisis in the sector. With sewage spills, infrastructure failures, and shareholder payouts dominating headlines for the last two years, the intervention suggests that the government believes the current regulatory framework (Ofwat) has failed to hold water companies to account. Full details are expected in the coming weeks, but the direction of travel is clear: after years of industry self-policing, the government is stepping in.

The water industry has been the UK's most consistent regulatory failure of the last decade. Sewage in rivers, dividends to shareholders, bonuses to executives, and bills that keep rising. An unprecedented intervention sounds dramatic — and it is — but the question is whether it goes far enough. Ofwat was supposed to do this job. If the government is now doing it directly, that's an admission that the regulatory model is broken. For anyone in the South West who's been paying some of the highest water bills in the country while watching sewage flow into Cornish beaches, this will be welcome — but the proof will be in the pricing.

The Pudding · Hacker News

Did you know the real Kevin Bacon is not, in fact, the most central node in the Hollywood graph? The most connected actor (by co-star network analysis) is someone else entirely — and the piece walks through the maths, the data, and the cultural implications of why we're all so attached to Bacon as the centre of the universe. The Pudding have been making the best data essays on the internet for years, and this might be their most delightful in a while. Kevin Bacon is not the most connected actor — he's not even in the top ten — but the game is called "Six Degrees of Kevin Bacon" because his name is more fun to say than "Six Degrees of Somebody More Connected Whose Name You Don't Recognise." It's a piece about network theory, cultural inertia, and why we tell stories about the people we recognise rather than the people who matter most to the structure. Perfect Friday reading.

🏛️ Part of Brighton's West Pier collapses into the sea

BBC News · The Guardian

As Storm Austen battered the south coast, a section of Brighton's iconic West Pier — an abandoned Victorian structure that first opened in 1866 — collapsed into the sea. Stephen Royle, who lives opposite, told the BBC he was "shocked by the amount of pier that's ended up in the sea." The West Pier has been closed since 1975 and partially collapsed in 2004 after a fire. This latest damage comes from a combination of storm surge and decades of neglect. The pier is a scheduled ancient monument, but no restoration plan has ever been fully funded.

The West Pier has been dying in slow motion for fifty years. It caught fire in 2004, lost its middle section in 2014, and now Storm Austen has taken another piece. There's something melancholic about watching a Victorian engineering marvel dissolve into the sea one storm at a time — a reminder that even the grandest infrastructure is temporary when the sea decides it wants it back. Brighton will mourn, the photos will be beautiful, and nothing will be rebuilt. Some things just get to be beautiful ruins.
