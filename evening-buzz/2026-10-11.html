2D Vehicles — 574 points on HN and the story of the weekend

Pat Kerr · Hacker News (574pts)

Pat Kerr — a solo developer from somewhere in the UK, given the domain — built a 3D vehicle physics simulation that renders as 2D sprites, and it's the highest-scoring thing on HN right now at 574 points. The idea is beautifully simple: imagine Grand Theft Auto's driving physics rendered with the visual language of a 1990s arcade racer. The vehicles have weight, suspension, drift mechanics, and terrain response — but visually they're flat sprites moving across a 2D plane. The effect is mesmerising: your brain processes the physics as 3D (the way the car leans into a turn, the way the suspension compresses over bumps, the weight transfer during braking) while the visuals stay stubbornly, charmingly 2D. Kerr's write-up explains the technical approach: a custom physics engine that models each vehicle as a rigid body with four independent suspension points, plus a rendering layer that projects the 3D physics state onto 2D sprite frames in real time. The demo runs entirely in the browser. The HN comments section is full of people asking for the source code and Kerr saying "soon, soon" in a way that suggests he knows he's sitting on something. This is the kind of project that makes HN worth reading on a Sunday. A solo developer, an idea that's technically challenging but conceptually simple, a demo that makes you smile, and 574 people agreeing that this is what the web should be for. The sprite-based rendering of a 3D physics simulation is one of those ideas that feels obvious once you see it — which is the surest sign of something genuinely creative.

Bot traffic from Yandex (499pts HN) — the cloud infrastructure story of the day

Cloudflare Radar · Hacker News (499pts)

Cloudflare's bot radar team published a deep analysis of sustained, high-volume bot traffic originating from Yandex's autonomous system (AS13238) — and it's dominating HN at 499 points. The data shows a sustained crawl pattern that doesn't look like a standard search engine indexer: the bot requests are distributed across millions of IPs, the user-agent strings rotate frequently, and the request patterns suggest data collection that goes beyond page indexing. The Cloudflare Radar post provides an interactive view — routes, AS paths, request patterns — that lets anyone explore the data. The HN discussion has two main threads: (1) is Yandex doing something different (and potentially more aggressive) with its crawl infrastructure than Google or Bing? and (2) is this actually Yandex, or is someone using Yandex's ASN to mask a non-Yandex crawler? Cloudflare isn't drawing any conclusions in the post — they're publishing the data and letting the community interpret it. The suspicion running through the comments is that this is either AI training data collection at industrial scale, or a sign that Yandex's crawl infrastructure has been compromised or repurposed. The open-source intelligence approach — treat the network data as evidence and let the analysis follow — is Cloudflare at its best. The Yandex bot story is the kind of infrastructure mystery that the tech industry is best at: here's the data, here's the anomaly, now let's all figure out what's happening together. My bet? AI training corpus collection, running at a scale that makes even Google's crawl look restrained. The training data wars are moving from copyright lawsuits to infrastructure warfare, and this is what it looks like on the wire.

Nitter: funding and legal help needed (319pts HN)

Nitter · Hacker News (319pts)

Nitter — the privacy-respecting Twitter/X front-end that strips tracking, removes algorithmic timelines, and served as the primary way thousands of people read Twitter without being Twitter — has posted an update that reads like a cry for help. The project needs funding and legal representation. The update (Oct 10) doesn't go into specifics, but the HN thread fills in the gaps: Twitter/X's legal team has been aggressive against third-party clients and scrapers, and Nitter's operator is facing legal pressure they can't afford to fight alone. The project has been a staple of the FOSS community for years. The HN thread at 319 points is full of people who've been using Nitter daily since the Musk acquisition, sharing their stories and asking where to donate. The comments section is also full of the hard question nobody wants to answer: can a privacy-focused front-end to a hostile platform survive when the platform has unlimited legal budget and the front-end has a GitHub sponsorship page? The mood is a mix of "we can crowdfund this" and "crowdfunding won't protect you from a lawsuit." Nitter's situation is the open web's recurring tragedy: a small project, built with care, serving thousands of people, brought to the edge of existence by a platform that sees any alternative interface as a threat. The crowdfunding will probably cover the immediate legal costs, but the fragility is the point. We keep building these beautiful things on top of platforms that can crush them. The answer isn't more donations — it's platforms that don't need to be reverse-engineered in the first place.

A city-building game where the city would prefer you didn't (469pts HN)

over.pizza · Hacker News (469pts)

Somewhere between a game and a piece of interactive art, this browser-based city-builder has the highest concept I've seen this month: you build a city, and the city doesn't want to be built. Every zoning decision you make is met with resistance from the simulated residents, the terrain, the weather, and the game's internal logic. Build a housing estate? The residents protest the loss of green space. Build a factory? Pollution drives down property values. Build a park? The residents complain it's attracting the wrong crowd. The game is a satire of the UK's housing crisis — the planning system, the NIMBY lobby, the developer-led model, the way every proposed solution generates its own set of problems. It's frustrating, opaque, and darkly funny. And it's not subtle about the politics. The author's note at the bottom of the page says "this game was made by someone who wants to see more houses built, but who also understands why they aren't." 469 points on HN and the comments section is full of UK-based developers who are laughing through the pain and saying "this is just my local council meeting, the game." This is the kind of thing that games do better than any other medium: make you experience a systemic problem rather than just understanding it intellectually. The city-building game where the city resists you is a perfect metaphor for the housing crisis — every intervention has second-order effects, every stakeholder has a veto, and the result is that nothing gets built. Playing it for ten minutes is more instructive than reading a thousand-word analysis. Art can do that. Games especially.

LineageOS 24.0 — the Android distribution you forgot about is still going (295pts HN)

LineageOS · Hacker News (295pts)

LineageOS — the Android distribution that started life as a CyanogenMod fork when that project shut down in 2016 — shipped version 24.0 and the HN response at 295 points shows there's still a passionate community around it. The new release is based on Android 15, with improvements to the privacy and security hardening that's always been LineageOS's selling point: granular permission controls, anti-tracking infrastructure, a system-level firewall, and a boot-time integrity check that flags unauthorised firmware modifications. The project has been running for nearly a decade at this point — a remarkable survival story in the Android modding world, where projects typically burn bright and fast before the maintenance burden or platform changes kill them. LineageOS's longevity comes from a conservative upgrade philosophy: don't chase every Android release, stabilise what you support, and don't break things that work. Version 24.0 is the result of that approach — an update that adds genuine value without disrupting the user experience. The HN discussion is refreshingly positive: a community that has been maintaining something for a decade just shipped a major release, and the response is "good work, keep going." LineageOS surviving to version 24.0 feels like a small miracle. Android modding is a brutal landscape — the platform changes every release, the security model gets tighter, and the gap between "open-source distribution" and "secure daily driver" keeps widening. The fact that this team has kept a distribution alive for a decade, through multiple Android generations, is a testament to the kind of patient, unglamorous maintenance work that doesn't get enough attention in an industry that rewards novelty over durability.

The machinery of a political crisis: once it starts rolling, the person at the centre has less control over the outcome than anyone watching on the outside assumes. The Polanski interview is a case study in the difference between being right and being effective. He may be right that he's the elected leader with a mandate. He may be right that the party shouldn't make decisions under pressure. The question is no longer whether Polanski can survive. It's whether the party can survive the process of removing him without tearing itself apart.

Drumcree parade ban sparks Northern Ireland political crisis

BBC News NI

The Northern Ireland Secretary's decision to ban the Drumcree parade on Friday has escalated into a full political crisis. DUP MP Carla Lockhart has declared that "it cannot be business as usual" at Stormont and — in the most dramatic intervention — said the DUP has it "within its gift to remove" Michelle O'Neill as First Minister. The mechanism: if the Deputy First Minister (from the DUP) resigns, the First Minister (from Sinn Féin, O'Neill) is automatically removed under power-sharing rules. Lockhart's language is the strongest from a DUP MP since the parade was banned: "People want to see consequences, and we have said very clearly, profound consequence." DUP leader Gavin Robinson has backed Lockhart's language — "she is right that the options are there" — but stopped short of calling for the executive to be collapsed, asking instead "does collapsing Stormont bring the Orangemen home?" The answer, of course, is no — but the question feels like the first step down a road that has historically led to prolonged power-sharing suspension. The Drumcree dispute, which has simmered for decades, has become the test of whether the current Northern Ireland settlement can survive a genuinely difficult parading flashpoint. The early signs are not encouraging. Every Drumcree season, everyone says "this time will be different" and every time it's the same — a parade route, a community that feels unheard, a political class that has to choose between keeping the peace and keeping the support of their base. The DUP's rhetoric this weekend is the most aggressive since the power-sharing restoration, and the question is whether Lockhart's 'profound consequences' are rhetoric or a real plan. The answer depends on whether the DUP leadership thinks collapsing the executive helps them with unionist voters in an election year. Historically, the answer to that question has been 'yes' — and the consequences have taken years to repair.

UK does not support Trump's Russia diesel deal, says Defence Secretary

BBC News

Defence Secretary Steve Streeting has explicitly stated that the UK "does not support" the Trump administration's reported plan to allow Russian diesel exports to resume — a position that puts the UK at odds with Washington on Ukraine war economic policy. The deal — reportedly negotiated between Trump's team and Kremlin intermediaries — would ease sanctions on Russian diesel in exchange for a framework agreement on Ukraine that the UK and most European allies see as deeply unfavourable to Kyiv. Streeting's statement is unusually blunt for a UK defence secretary commenting on US policy: "We do not support this approach. Russia must face maximum economic pressure until they withdraw from Ukraine. Any deal that eases that pressure before a full withdrawal rewards the aggression we have spent three years opposing." The timing — Sunday morning politics, before the markets open on Monday — is deliberate: the UK wants the signal to be the first thing investors and policymakers see when the week starts. The split between the UK and the US on Russia sanctions policy is the most significant public disagreement between the two countries since the war began. This is what a post-American NATO looks like: the UK and Europe lining up on one side of a strategic question and the US on the other. Streeting's statement is the most direct confirmation we've had that the special relationship, on Russia policy at least, is in a different place than it was two years ago. The question the UK government has to answer is whether it can sustain a sanctions regime without the active participation of the US Treasury — and the answer, which nobody in government wants to say aloud, is "with difficulty."

East Sussex launches legal challenge against government reorganisation plans

BBC News

A Reform UK-led East Sussex County Council has formally launched a legal challenge against the government's plans to reorganise local government boundaries — specifically the proposal to move Peacehaven, Telscombe, and East Saltdean from East Sussex into a new, larger Brighton & Hove unitary authority. Council leader Andy Woolley (Reform UK) and Lewes District Council leader Zoe Nicholson (Green) — an unlikely political alliance — have jointly challenged the plans, with Nicholson having already initiated "protective legal action" earlier this month. The Communities Secretary paused the reorganisation in September following legal advice, but today's formal legal challenge escalates the dispute. The case raises genuinely interesting questions about the balance between local government efficiency and community identity — the government argues that larger unitary authorities are more efficient, while the councils argue that communities have a right to determine their own governance boundaries. A courtroom battle over local government boundaries in East Sussex is not the headline the government wanted, but it's a reminder that even the most technocratic reforms face political resistance when they involve moving people from one council to another against their will.
