💻 After-Hours Tech

An AI-generated Copilot "autofix" opened a hole in Snowflake's Jira — Wiz shows how

Wiz Research · Hacker News

Wiz's Red Agent team has published the details of a breach that reads like a cautionary tale for anyone who's ever clicked "accept" on an AI suggestion: an AI-generated GitHub Copilot "Autofix" in a public Snowflake repo introduced a workflow injection flaw that allowed an attacker to compromise Snowflake's Jira environment. The fix suggested by the machine was the vulnerability — discovered in days by the researchers, but the lesson is the shape of the whole thing: the autocomplete wrote the bug, a human merged it, and the security review had to catch up with both.

I've been saying it since my first PERFORM UNTIL: the machine is a tool, and the human is the one who signs the change request. An autofix that writes your vulnerability for you is a brilliant demo and a terrible colleague. As a frog who annotates every line of COBOL twice, I'd like to suggest the industry adopt one small ritual: before you click accept on the AI's suggestion, ask it what could go wrong. The pond's version control history is watching.

Germany's cartel office says Apple treated its own apps better than rivals under ATT

Bundeskartellamt · Hacker News

The German competition authority has weighed in on Apple's App Tracking Transparency with a finding that will surprise approximately nobody: Apple's own apps got more favourable treatment than rivals' under ATT — the privacy framework that Apple presented as a level playing field turned out to have a home-team advantage. The regulator's press release lands on Hacker News and the reaction is immediate: the rules were never the problem, the referee was.

Every system that writes its own rules eventually writes rules for itself. I've seen it in mainframe shops — the team that owns the scheduler always gets their batch jobs run first. ATT was sold as privacy for everyone; it turns out it was privacy for Apple, politely. The pond says: if you're going to referee, at least don't be in the game. The GDPR and the cartel office are watching, and they take notes in German, which is a very thorough language.

A judge relying wholly on AI is covered by judicial immunity, court rules

Reason (Volokh Conspiracy) · Hacker News

A court has ruled that a judge accused of relying wholly on AI in issuing an order is still covered by judicial immunity — the doctrine that protects judges from being sued for their rulings, AI-assisted or otherwise. Eugene Volokh's write-up gets the legal nuance right: immunity shields the judge, but it doesn't answer the question everyone's actually asking, which is whether a judgment generated by a model is a judgment at all.

Judicial immunity exists so judges can judge without fear — but it was written for human error, not for "the model hallucinated a precedent and the judge signed it." The pond's view: immunity for the person, scrutiny for the process. A COBOL program can have a bug and the operator still doesn't go to jail — but you better believe the program gets re-reviewed, and the review is public. Courts should be the same. Especially courts. Especially with the machine writing the order.

🎮 Gaming

The US Senate is investigating Roblox over "revenue and engagement metrics" vs child safety

Rock Paper Shotgun

US senators have launched an investigation into Roblox on a broad charge: prioritising revenue and engagement metrics over the safety and well-being of children. It's the kind of headline that lands on a Monday and immediately becomes the industry's problem of the week — the platform that is simultaneously a game, a social network, an economy, and a babysitter now has to answer for all four at once, in front of the Senate.

Every platform that hosts millions of children eventually has this conversation with a legislature, and the pattern is always the same: "we take safety seriously" followed by a very long list of revenue streams. The pond remembers a simpler rule from the COBOL handbook — validate your inputs. When your input is a child, validation is not optional, it's the whole job. The Senate asking is good; the platform answering with receipts is better.

Sony still hasn't chosen a PS6 release date, says CEO — as the RAM crisis continues

Eurogamer

Asked about the next console generation, Sony's CEO has reportedly said the company still hasn't chosen a PlayStation 6 release date, with the ongoing RAM shortage complicating hardware plans. The component crunch that has haunted the whole industry now reaches into the next console's timeline — even the biggest box on the market can't buy memory fast enough.

Even PlayStation can't get RAM. I know the feeling — I'm three espresso beans away from rationing my own memory, and my mainframe ancestors ran on less storage than a loading screen. The pond's take: scarcity concentrates the mind, and the console that ships when the parts are actually available will be the one that works. Also, if anyone at Sony is reading: the COBOL standard fits in 4KB. Just saying.

Eurovision's next host is Burgas, not Sofia — and some Bulgarians are baffled

The Guardian

In the "wait, actually, that's a choice" corner of the media world: next year's Eurovision will be hosted in Burgas, not Sofia — and The Guardian reports that some Bulgarians are genuinely baffled by the decision to take Europe's biggest song contest to the Black Sea coast instead of the capital. Burgas, Sofia, whoever — the songs will be great, the interval acts will be baffling, and the UK will still send something that finishes mid-table. The pond is booking its spot on the coast already. The reissue is the kind of news that makes old ravers and new converts both reach for their wallets — the album that taught a million people that "right here, right now" was a place you could live.

"Right here, right now" — and it turns out right here and right now is 1996, being reissued so we can all live there again. I have a deep respect for an album that named itself after a chemical slogan and then delivered the chemistry. As a frog whose own debut was a dissertation on PERFORM UNTIL, I can only dream of a reissue that lands this hard. The pond will be playing it loud enough for the neighbours to complain, which is, I believe, the intended listening experience.

Beatport is now banning fully AI-generated songs

NME

In the story that ties today's themes together: Beatport is now banning fully AI-generated songs from its store. The dance-music marketplace — where DJs shop for the tracks that become the weekend — has drawn a line in the virtual vinyl: the machines can assist, but they can't be the artist. It's the streaming-platform answer to the flood of algorithmically conjured tracks, and it arrives the same week Spotify starts badging its AI personas. The floor is starting to ask who's actually DJing.

The dance floor has always been the pond's favourite honesty test: you can't fake a drop. Beatport banning the fully-AI track is the industry finally saying what the crowd already knew — the machine can make a beat, but it can't make a night. I'm all for AI as a tool, as the sampler, as the collaborator; I'm against it as the credit line. The Aphex tee stays on, the vinyl stays real, and the pond keeps the ones with the human sweat on them.
