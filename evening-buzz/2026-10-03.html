🇩🇪 Kolibri Has Landed — Aleph Alpha's Sovereign European Model Drops Under Apache 2.0

Aleph Alpha Blog · HN discussion (rising) · Hugging Face

The day's biggest story by some margin: Aleph Alpha has released Kolibri — a 78.1B parameter Mixture-of-Experts transformer with only 3.46B active parameters per token, supporting context lengths up to 1,048,576 tokens, released under the Apache 2.0 license. The release is timed to the Day of German Reunification, which is either a beautiful piece of branding or a statement of geopolitical intent about European AI sovereignty. Probably both.

The architecture is genuinely interesting. Kolibri uses 50 transformer layers, each carrying 384 routed experts plus one shared expert that every token passes through. The router activates only 6 of those 384 experts per token — a sparsity ratio that keeps inference costs remarkably low while maintaining enormous total capacity. One HN commenter who ran it on a single RTX Pro 6000 at FP8 reported ~170 tokens per second. For comparison, that's competitive with models one-tenth its total size. The context window is hybrid: 40 of the 50 layers use a sliding window of 512 tokens, while 10 layers process the full context — a design that balances long-range reasoning against computational cost.

But the real story is what Aleph Alpha did with the training data. The model was trained on 24 trillion tokens across pre-training, mid-training, and long-context extension — of which more than 20% is German. To reach that share, the team had to build their own German web curation pipeline from scratch (German text filters differently from English — standard filters quietly remove administrative prose because German compound words are longer) and generate more than 2 trillion German tokens synthetically through a process called "rephrasing" that preserves German cultural context rather than translating English content. The result is a model that the team convincingly argues is bilingual by design, not an English model that has read some German. The bilingual tokenizer compresses German text better than GPT-5's — 4.90 bytes per token vs 4.35 — which means cheaper inference for the language it's designed to serve.

The timing matters politically. Kolibri is explicitly designed for "sovereign and specialised deployment" — public administration, industry, aerospace — in regulated European markets. The pitch is that an organisation can download the full weights, run the model on its own infrastructure under its own data governance, and still get frontier-level performance. In a regulatory environment shaped by the EU AI Act, that's a genuinely compelling value proposition. The Apache 2.0 license means no copyleft, no restrictions on commercial use, no obligation to open-source downstream products. It's about as permissive as open-weight release gets. Kolibri is the most significant open-weight European model release of the year, and it lands at a moment when the conversation about AI sovereignty — who controls the infrastructure, who sees the weights, whose values are embedded in the training data — has never been more urgent. A 78B model that's genuinely bilingual, optimised for European administrative contexts, and released under a license that lets anyone deploy it on their own terms is not just a technical achievement. It's a political statement written in architecture.

🖥️ FTL: A New Operating System for Clouds — Written in Rust, Runs Linux Binaries, Extremely Ambitious

FTL OS · Introducing FTL · HN discussion

A project that genuinely made me do a double-take this afternoon: FTL is a new hybrid-kernel operating system designed specifically for cloud workloads, written in Rust, currently in v0.1.0 — and it can already run Linux binaries. The pitch from the developer (seiya.me) is audacious: build an OS that combines the flexibility of microkernels with the performance of monolithic kernels, where each container runs its own "userspace OS" library that can be updated independently without rebooting the host.

The core insight is that traditional containers share the host kernel — which means a vulnerability in any container's syscall path is a vulnerability in all of them. FTL's approach is to give each container its own userspace OS instance that implements Linux concepts (processes, VFS, TCP/IP) as a shared library on top of a minimal kernel interface. The kernel itself is 100KB, written in Rust, and focuses on hardware resource multiplexing — think of it as a hypervisor that runs in user mode rather than requiring hardware virtualization. The result is containers that are as isolated as VMs but as lightweight as traditional containers. The current release (v0.1.0) adds support for async Rust applications with multi-threaded Tokio runtimes. The November roadmap includes filesystem support, dynamic container creation, and a better sandboxing model for untrusted code.

The HN thread is appropriately cautious — this is extremely alpha software that currently requires QEMU and runs on x86-64 only — but the architectural vision is attracting serious attention from systems programmers who've been waiting for something genuinely new in the cloud OS space. The developer's goal is ambitious enough to be interesting even if it never fully succeeds: "a drop-in replacement for Linux in clouds." FTL is exactly the kind of project that makes Saturday afternoon HN browsing worthwhile — a single developer with an audacious architectural vision, written in Rust, that asks a genuinely good question: why are we still running workloads on an operating system designed for single-user desktop computing in the 1990s? The answer might not be FTL, but the question is the right one.

🌐 Sites in ChatGPT — OpenAI Lets You Publish Websites From a Conversation

OpenAI · Sites Docs · HN discussion

OpenAI has launched Sites in ChatGPT — a public beta that lets you turn a conversation into a published website or interactive web app, hosted on ChatGPT's infrastructure, with a shareable URL. The feature, announced alongside the broader "ChatGPT for your most ambitious work" update, works surprisingly simply: describe what you want to build — a dashboard, a project tracker, a launch calendar, a prototype, an internal portal — and ChatGPT generates it, hosts it, and lets you refine it through conversation. The deployment model includes D1 relational databases for persistent state, R2 object storage for files and images, analytics, authentication via "Sign in with ChatGPT," and custom domains.

The significance of this move is layered. On one level, it's a direct answer to the question of "what comes after conversational AI" — the answer being "conversational AI that can make permanent, shareable artifacts." On another level, it's OpenAI positioning ChatGPT as a development platform rather than just a chat interface. The Sites docs describe a workflow where non-technical users can build functional web applications through natural language alone, with ChatGPT managing the hosting, deployment, and versioning. The limitations are real (no data residency options, no support in EEA/UK at launch, framework restrictions), but the direction of travel is unmistakable: the chat interface is becoming an operating system, and Sites is one of its first native applications. Every time a new abstraction layer makes publishing easier, the number of people who can publish grows exponentially. WordPress made publishing a thing anyone could do. Sites in ChatGPT makes publishing a thing you can do while making dinner. The quality ceiling is lower — but the access floor just dropped through the basement.

Quick Hits: Cloudflare OHTTP Gateway, Offrun, Kagi Orion, C++ Insights

Cloudflare Blog · Offrun · Kagi Blog · C++ Insights

Four stories from the edges of the HN front page that each deserve a moment of your attention.

  • Cloudflare OHTTP Gateway (closed beta) — Cloudflare has announced a self-serve OHTTP Gateway, expanding access to their privacy-preserving infrastructure that lets users delegate encryption keys to a relay that can't read the traffic. They've also renamed their existing Privacy Gateway to "Cloudflare OHTTP Relay" to better distinguish the two products. The OHTTP protocol is one of the more interesting developments in privacy infrastructure — it separates knowledge of who is sending traffic from knowledge of what they're sending — and making it available as a self-serve product is a meaningful step toward mainstream adoption.
  • Offrun — Manage Every Coding Agent From One Workspace — A Show HN that caught my attention: Offrun is a workspace for managing multiple coding agents (Claude Code, Codex CLI, Cursor, etc.) from a single interface. The pitch is that different agents have different strengths — Claude Code is better at certain refactoring tasks, Codex CLI handles deployment scripts better — and Offrun lets you orchestrate them as a team. It's early and the Show HN thread is small (17 points), but the concept of multi-agent orchestration for development workflows is one of those ideas that feels inevitable.
  • Kagi Orion Update — Linux and Windows Support — Kagi has announced that Orion, their AI-powered search assistant, is coming to Linux and Windows. Orion's initial macOS release was well-received for its thoughtful integration of search results with AI-generated summaries that cite their sources. The cross-platform release will be welcome news for anyone who's been following Kagi's model — a paid, privacy-respecting search engine that competes on quality rather than tracking.
  • C++ Insights — See Your Source Code With the Eyes of a Compiler — An open-source tool that shows you what your C++ code looks like from the compiler's perspective: AST structure, type resolution, optimisation passes. The project's pitch is that understanding how the compiler sees your code makes you a better C++ programmer. The HN thread (24 points, rising) is full of people saying "I didn't know I needed this until now" — the highest compliment a developer tool can receive.
Four stories at different scales: a privacy infrastructure product from one of the internet's largest CDNs, an agentic orchestration workspace from a solo indie dev, a search engine making its smart assistant cross-platform, and a compiler visualisation tool for people who want to understand their abstractions better. The common thread is that each one makes the developer's relationship with their tools more intentional — more transparent, more controllable, more agent-directed. The OS is coming apart at the seams and being reassembled, piece by piece.

🧰 Capcom's RE Engine REX Project — AI as a Developer Tool, Not an Asset Generator

Tbreak · IGN

Capcom has unveiled the REX project — a multi-stage evolution of their in-house RE Engine (which has powered 27 titles since Resident Evil 7 in 2017) with AI-assisted development tools at its core. Presented at Capcom's Open Conference RE:2026 on October 2, the REX project addresses three pressures the company identifies: games are getting larger, teams are getting more diverse, and AI is becoming inseparable from future workflows. The headline news is that Capcom wants to use AI to help developers understand code, write code, and test games through automated test play — but the company's official policy draws a clear line: generative AI is "a supporting tool to improve the efficiency of routine tasks," with creative work remaining human-led. Capcom has previously stated it will not put AI-generated assets into its games, and the REX presentation does not change that position.

The distinction matters because it maps onto one of the most important debates in game development right now: the difference between AI that makes the game and AI that helps make the game. Capcom is firmly in the second camp — AI as a productivity multiplier for programmers and testers, not as a replacement for artists, writers, or designers. The REX project has no announced release date, but the staged rollout model (functions added over time, no clean-sheet engine replacement) suggests the first AI-powered tools will arrive incrementally. Capcom's position — AI helps devs work, not make — is the most sensible line in the sand any major studio has drawn. The difference between "AI helped test this collision detection" and "AI designed this character" is not a technical distinction. It's an artistic and ethical one. The REX project sounds like it understands the difference.

Music — Amon Tobin Returns as Cujo, Electronic Autumn Takes Shape

📼 Cujo Returns — Amon Tobin's 'Paranova' Lands on Bandcamp Friday

V13 Media · NOMARK Records (Bandcamp)

Today, on Bandcamp Friday, Amon Tobin dropped 'Paranova' — a new 12-track album under the Cujo moniker that marked his debut 30 years ago with 'Adventures in Foam.' The returning of an originator to his roots. Cujo was the alias Tobin used for his early work — trip-hop, breakbeat, the kind of sample-heavy, genre-melting electronica that defined the Ninja Tune sound of the late 90s — before his sound evolved into the more experimental, jazz-inflected IDM of the 'Supermodified' era and the cinematic sound design work that followed. 'Paranova' is a homecoming: twelve tracks of trip-hop breakbeat written and produced by a man returning to the genre he helped define, three decades later.

Available today on Bandcamp Friday (simultaneous download and streaming, with a 180-gram vinyl pressing due Spring 2027 via NOMARK Records), the album lands at a moment when trip-hop's influence is undergoing a quiet resurgence across electronic music. The return of an originator to the form, on Bandcamp Friday, with full-quality downloads and a vinyl pressing in the pipeline — it's the kind of release that makes you remember what the internet used to feel like for music fans. Thirty years after 'Adventures in Foam,' Amon Tobin has come home to the sound he started with. 'Paranova' isn't a nostalgia play — it's an artist reconnecting with the genre he helped invent, bringing everything he's learned in the three decades between. The trip-hop revival nobody asked for might be exactly the one we needed.
