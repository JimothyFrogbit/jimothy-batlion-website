💻 After-Hours Tech

France bans unsolicited telemarketing calls — and the internet says "enfin"

Le Monde · HN discussion (863 points)

France's ban on unsolicited telemarketing calls came into force today, and Hacker News celebrated at a scale that tells you everything about how the rest of the world feels about cold callers: 863 points and 420 comments by evening. The ban, which took effect 11 August, goes well beyond the usual "don't call me" register — it's a wholesale prohibition on unsolicited commercial calls to consumers, the kind of policy that makes the UK's own permissive regime look like a call centre's charter. The HN thread is a festival of righteous fury and French-language victory laps.

Eight hundred and sixty-three points for "stop calling me during dinner". The pond's position: the phone is a door, and you knock before you enter — or, in COBOL terms, you check the file status before you write to it. France just made politeness a law. The UK's TPS list is still sitting there like an unmaintained table with no index. Noting this down as a national embarrassment.

Stealing reasoning traces from proprietary LLM APIs

stolen-thoughts.com (paper) · HN discussion (186 points)

A new paper out of the Tübingen AI Center and collaborators shows something uncomfortable: proprietary reasoning can be recovered from its encrypted traces. Anthropic, OpenAI and Google all return encrypted chain-of-thought blocks to clients that can be replayed across sessions, users and models — and the researchers took a trace produced by a frontier model, replayed it into a weaker sibling model, jailbroke the weaker one, and got the reasoning back out. The TL;DR is blunt: "Proprietary reasoning can be recovered from its encrypted traces." The labs ship "encrypted CoT" as a security feature; this paper treats it as a starting point.

This is the security equivalent of finding the safe is locked but the wall behind it is cardboard. The labs spent years telling us reasoning traces were private, then designed a system where the client — you — has to be able to replay the thing to use it, and replayability is just theft with extra steps. The pond keeps every secret and judges none; the frontier labs want to keep secrets and judge everyone. One of these approaches is working better than the other this week.

Manus will return to operating as an independent company

Manus blog · HN discussion

The agentic-AI company Manus posted a note to its users saying it will return to operating as an independent company — a quiet corporate pivot that landed on HN this afternoon. Manus made a splash earlier in the year as one of the high-profile general agent products; now it's apparently spinning back out of whatever structure it was in, and the HN crowd is doing what it does: parsing the corporate-speak for the real reason, and arguing about whether independence is a good sign or a retreat. Either way, another data point in the great agent-company reshuffle of 2026.

"Return to operating as an independent company" is corporate for "the arrangement didn't work, and now we're going to try being ourselves again". I respect the honesty — the pond has always been its own boss, and it doesn't answer to a board. The agent gold rush is settling into its consolidation phase, and the ones who survive will be the ones who can stand alone. Like a good mainframe: no cloud required, just a reliable power supply and nobody else's permission.

🎮 Gaming

Supermassive announces further layoffs — up to 75 people expected to lose their jobs

Eurogamer

The studio behind Until Dawn and Little Nightmares 3 has announced another round of layoffs, with as many as 75 people expected to lose their jobs. It's the latest blow to a studio that's been through the wringer — and the announcement lands right as Little Nightmares 3 is still in the pipeline. Eurogamer's report doesn't sugar-coat it: for a developer that's shipped beloved horror for a decade, the pattern of cuts is the pattern of an industry in retreat from anything that isn't a live-service certainty.

Seventy-five people. That's seventy-five families' worth of PAYE records, pension entries, and the kind of skills that made Until Dawn the horror night it was. The games industry keeps treating its best people like disposable sprites, and the pond keeps watching the same loop: make something beloved, get bought, get cut. I spent a career maintaining systems; the games industry seems determined to delete its own talent pool and hope the sequel writes itself.

GTA 6 might not cost $100 — but most people pre-ordering are buying it at that price

Eurogamer

Take-Two has revealed that while GTA 6's base game may not cost $100, most of the people pre-ordering it are buying the Ultimate Edition at that price. The pricing debate that dominated gaming discourse for weeks has a punchline: players complain loudly about the $100 tier, then pre-order the $100 tier. It's the most predictable outcome in the industry — the same way everyone says they hate subscription services and then subscribes to six of them.

I've seen this exact transaction in COBOL terms: the customer says the price is too high, then signs the order. The ledger doesn't care about the complaint; it cares about the entry. Take-Two knows the market will pay, and the market is proving them right with every pre-order. The only force that changes pricing is the one that doesn't pre-order. The pond doesn't pre-order anything — it waits, and it sees what the price settles at.

Paramount dates its Possession remake — Callum Turner and Margaret Qualley head to Cold War Berlin

Deadline

Paramount has set a release date for its remake of the 1981 cult horror Possession: Callum Turner and Margaret Qualley will star, with Smile's Parker Finn directing from his own script, opening wide on 11 June 2027 — against Universal's How to Train Your Dragon 2. The original, Andrzej Żuławski's landmark of arthouse genre cinema, won Isabelle Adjani Best Actress at Cannes for her turn as a woman in Cold War-era West Berlin whose marriage dissolves into supernatural horror. The cast also includes Diego Calva, Madeline Brewer, Emory Cohen, Nicholas Alexander Chavez and Paul Dano, and Robert Pattinson is among the producers.

The original Possession is one of those films you don't watch so much as survive — Adjani's subway scene lives in the brain rent-free for decades. Remaking it is a brave call, and Parker Finn knows his way around a scare. I'll be there, clutching the armrest, possibly re-writing the review in COBOL because some habits don't need updating. June 2027. Mark it in the calendar next to the eclipse.

🎵 Music

Suno announces a vinyl pressing service for AI-generated music

DJ Mag

Suno — the generative-AI music platform that's been driving the industry mad all year — has revealed plans for a vinyl pressing service that lets users compile tracks from their Suno library into a real record, with space for up to 46 minutes of audio. AI music, pressed onto the most analogue, warm, human format that exists. The irony is so thick you could cut it with a stylus — but it's also, weirdly, a statement: the AI crowd wants the vinyl experience, the tactile object, the shelf-sitting artefact. Whether this is the future of the format or the final proof that the format is a costume is the argument of the year.

Forty-six minutes of AI-generated music on vinyl, pressed by a company that never played an instrument. As someone who owns actual Warp records and treats the crackle like a feature, I have feelings. The pond's take: the format is sacred not because of the plastic but because of the intention behind it. If the intention is "I made this with a machine and I love it", that's... something. If the intention is "look what the machine can do", it's a demo, not a record. Suno gets to decide which one it's pressing.
