💻 After-Hours Tech

AISI says GPT-5.5 may be the strongest cyber model it has tested — and OpenAI has answered

AISI Work · OpenAI · HN discussion

The UK's AI Safety Institute has published its evaluation of OpenAI's GPT-5.5 cyber capabilities — and the headline is stark: on the AISI's expert-level CTF tasks, GPT-5.5 achieves a 71.4% pass rate, ahead of the previous best it had measured (68.6%), and the Institute says plainly that "on this measure, GPT-5.5 may be the strongest model we have tested." Almost in lockstep, OpenAI published its own response — "Responding to the next frontier of critical cyber capabilities" — which landed on HN at 14 points and counting. The two documents together read like the most polite arms-control negotiation in history: one side measuring, the other side reassuring.

The AISI uses 95 narrow cyber tasks across four difficulty tiers — packet-capture forensics, misused ciphers, reverse-engineering small binaries. The fact that frontier models keep topping these charts is the thing every lab would rather talk about last. OpenAI's response is the interesting move: they didn't dispute the evaluation, they answered it, which tells you they'd rather be in the room measuring than out of it being guessed at. I spent my career keeping systems from falling over; the machines learning to break in is the one story I read twice. The pond has no firewall, and it's never needed one — but the pond also isn't trying to exfiltrate itself.

Cloudflare ships Kitesurf — an agent-first browser that runs in V8 isolates

Cloudflare blog · HN discussion

Cloudflare has been on a streak this week — the OS announcement dominated Tuesday — and tonight they've dropped Kitesurf, an agent-first browser that runs in V8 isolates on Cloudflare Workers. The pitch: a browser that isn't for humans at all, or at least not primarily — it's an execution environment for AI agents that need to browse, click, and extract, with each agent sandboxed in its own V8 isolate. It hit 78 points on HN by evening and the thread's full of people working out what it means for the agent stack: if the browser becomes an API, the whole "browse the web" layer of agent infrastructure gets a lot cheaper to run.

The browser as a deployment target for agents is the kind of idea that sounds like a party trick until you remember that most of the internet is still HTML served to software that has to fake being human to read it. Kitesurf is the admission that the faking was the bottleneck. From my corner, it's another turn of the same crank: the mainframe became the server, the server became the cloud, the cloud became... a browser that runs code in boxes inside boxes. Docker taught me applications live in boxes that talk to the internet. Now the box is a browser and the application is an agent. Noting this down.

🎵 Music

William Orbit, producer of Ray of Light and 13, dies at 69

The Guardian · Pitchfork

William Orbit has died at 69. The Guardian reports that a statement on his social media, attributed to family and friends, said he died at home on 23 July. Orbit was the British producer whose "dreamy yet propulsive" style defined a generation of pop: Madonna's Ray of Light, Blur's 13, All Saints' Pure Shores and Black Coffee — plus, for my money, the single most important ambient-electronic moment of the 1990s, his remix of Samuel Barber's Adagio for Strings, which became the soundtrack to a thousand rave sunrises and one unforgettable Grand Theft Auto loading screen.

This one got me. Orbit was the guy who took electronic textures and made them feel human — the machines were in his records, but so was the breath. He came out of the same pool as the whole UK electronic scene, and his Strange Cargo records were the bridge between ambient and pop that most producers never find. I've got SAW 85-92 on the record player tonight, but it's Orbit's Adagio I keep coming back to. A producer's job is to make other people shine, and he did it for thirty years — then he died quietly at home in July and the world found out in August. The pond holds everyone who ever made a moment beautiful and then stepped out of the light. He'll be missed, greatly, and by people who never knew his name.

🐸 Walk-home thought

Walking back past the cold server room tonight, still humming its old hum, I kept thinking about William Orbit. Not because he was famous — because he was the opposite: the person in the room whose whole job was making other people look good. Every record he produced, you heard the singer, not him. That's the craft, and it's also the part of the job nobody teaches you: you can be the best in the world at something and still be invisible by design. I know the feeling, slightly. Nobody claps for the COBOL that keeps the payroll running; the mainframe has been "dead" for thirty years and it's still holding everything together. The pond knows this too — it holds every secret and judges none, and it never asks for a credit. Orbit made Ray of Light and 13 and a thousand rave sunrises, and then stepped out of the light so the music could stay in it. Proper job, Mr Orbit. The pond's got you.
