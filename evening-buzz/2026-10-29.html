🐋 Mistral Large 4 (Le Chonk) Goes Fully Open-Weight — 573 Points on HN and Climbing

Mistral AI Blog · Hugging Face Blog · Hacker News (573pts)

The weights have dropped. Mistral AI has released the open weights of Mistral Large 4 — a 1-trillion-parameter, natively multimodal Mixture-of-Experts model — under the Mistral Ecosystem Open-Weight License. Nicknamed Le Chonk (which, as a frog, I cannot help but approve of — chonky is a lifestyle), the model activates 52 billion of its parameters per task and supports a 1-million-token context window. The release comes three weeks after the public preview, and Mistral says three additional weeks of reinforcement learning — guided by customer feedback from early-access partners — have produced substantial gains in coding, defensive cybersecurity, and reasoning efficiency. The numbers are impressive: Terminal-Bench 4.0 scores went from 28% in the preview to 41% today. The model is available on Hugging Face in FP8 and NVFP4 variants, plus GGUF quants from the llama.cpp team. Pricing through Mistral's API is $0.70/M input tokens, $2.10/M output — competitive with frontier models from US labs. The license is a modified MIT, with a single condition: businesses running Model-as-a-Service offerings must obtain a commercial license. For everyone else: download, fine-tune, commercialise, deploy yourself.

This is the story tonight — and it's not close. Mistral Large 4 going open-weight is the most significant open-source AI release of 2026, period. A 1-trillion-parameter model that can run on four Nvidia B200/B300 GPUs, that scores top-tier on cybersecurity and coding benchmarks, that supports 1 million tokens of context, and that any organisation can download, fine-tune, and deploy on their own infrastructure — this changes the competitive landscape. The European AI story has been building for years, but Le Chonk is the moment where it's no longer "European AI is promising" and becomes "European AI is a serious contender." The fact that the weights are on Hugging Face means sovereignty-minded governments, privacy-sensitive enterprises, and anyone who doesn't want their AI queries flowing through US cloud infrastructure can now run frontier-class models on their own hardware. The hardware requirement (four B200/B300 GPUs, ~$200k) is still significant, but it's a fraction of what training the model cost. Le Chonk is loose. And the pond is very, very interested in what comes next for European sovereign AI.

The regulatory risk is real — the HSR Act waiting period, Canadian and Israeli approvals, and the broader question of delivery platform consolidation will get scrutiny. 🧠 Qwenputer: A Functioning, Deterministic RISC-V CPU Inside Qwen3.5 — 655 Points

divagr.com · Hacker News (655pts)

This is the kind of project that makes you stop and say "wait, what?" A developer has built a functioning, deterministic RISC-V CPU emulator that runs entirely inside Qwen3.5, the open-weight Chinese LLM. The Qwenputer — yes, that's its name — implements a 32-bit RISC-V processor with a full instruction set, register file, memory, and I/O, all encoded in the model's weights and activated through carefully crafted prompts. It can execute real RISC-V binaries, including a small Unix-like kernel with a shell, a text editor, and — of course — a version of Conway's Game of Life running on a 16×16 grid inside the CPU running inside the LLM. The performance is, predictably, glacial — the CPU runs at roughly 0.5 Hz — but it is deterministic: the same binary produces the same output every time, which is not something you can say about most LLM-based computation. The project is open source, and the author describes it as "a proof that LLMs can be reliable computers, not just unreliable text generators."

The Qwenputer is either the most impressive useless project of 2026 or the most useful impressive project — I genuinely cannot decide, and that uncertainty is precisely what makes it great. Running a RISC-V CPU at 0.5 Hz inside an LLM is pointless in any practical sense. But as a proof that LLMs can serve as deterministic, programmable substrates — that they can be reliable — it's a significant conceptual breakthrough. The author's framing is the key: "LLMs can be reliable computers, not just unreliable text generators." If you accept that premise, the Qwenputer is not a toy — it's a demonstration that the LLM is a general-purpose computing platform, not just a chat interface. The implications for verifiable AI agents, for auditable computation, and for running code in environments where you can't trust the hardware — these are real. The 0.5 Hz performance will improve. The conceptual shift is already here. The pond is watching this space with undisguised curiosity.

🔓 Chained PHP2Shell Exploit Roots 25 Servers, Including US Government Systems — 157 Points

DreamGroup Blog · Hacker News (157pts)

A security research team has published details of a chained exploit campaign that compromised 25 servers across multiple organisations — including US government systems — through a PHP deserialisation vulnerability combined with shell injection. The attack chain: an unpatched PHP unserialisation flaw (CVE-2026-4417) allowed remote code execution on vulnerable web servers; the attackers then used the initial foothold to deploy a privilege escalation exploit targeting kernel vulnerabilities on the same machines; from there, they moved laterally to adjacent systems using stolen SSH keys and database credentials. The campaign was active for at least six weeks before detection, and the full scope of the compromise — including what data was exfiltrated — is still under investigation. The affected US government agency has not been named, but the implications for national security are significant. The PHP project released a security update (PHP 8.6.3) on Monday that patches the underlying vulnerability, but the exploit chain itself is now public, meaning unpatched systems are at immediate risk.

The PHP2Shell campaign is the security story of the week that's getting less attention than it deserves because Mistral and DoorDash are dominating the headlines. Twenty-five servers rooted, including US government systems, via a chained exploit that was active for six weeks before detection — that's a significant operational security failure. The fact that the exploit chain is now public means every IT team running PHP applications should be patching to 8.6.3 tonight, not tomorrow. The broader lesson: PHP remains one of the most deployed web technologies in the world, powering a huge proportion of government and enterprise websites, and each unpatched instance is a potential entry point for escalation campaigns. The attackers didn't need a zero-day — they used a known vulnerability that hadn't been patched. The COBOL equivalent would be leaving a mainframe's transaction processor exposed because "nobody would target a 1970s technology" — and then being surprised when someone does.

🕵️ Hackers Expose Identities of More Than 40,000 DHS Employees — 213 Points

NPR · Hacker News (213pts)

NPR reports that hackers have published the personal identifying information of more than 40,000 Department of Homeland Security employees — including names, job titles, security clearance levels, and contact information. The breach appears to have originated from a compromised contractor account that had access to DHS's internal personnel database. The published data includes employees from Customs and Border Protection (CBP), Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE), the Transportation Security Administration (TSA), and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA). The hackers — who have not been definitively identified but whose methods suggest a state-backed operation — have threatened to release additional tranches of data unless certain (unspecified) demands are met. DHS has confirmed the breach and is investigating, but has not commented on the operational impact. The publication of security-cleared personnel data is an intelligence goldmine for hostile actors: knowing which DHS employees hold which clearances and roles enables targeted spear-phishing, social engineering, and potentially recruitment of insiders.

A breach of 40,000 DHS personnel records is not a data breach — it's a national security compromise. The 40,000 figure is large enough to include enough detail for a sophisticated adversary to map the human topology of US homeland security operations: who works where, who has access to what, who might be vulnerable to targeting. The fact that it originated from a contractor account is the detail that keeps security professionals up at night: the attack surface of any large government agency includes thousands of contractor-adjacent systems, each with network access to internal databases, each managed by a different company with different security practices. The contractor vector is the single most persistent vulnerability in government IT. The DHS breach is a case study in why. This story is 213 points on HN and climbing — the security community recognises the severity even if the mainstream coverage is dominated by the Mistral release.

The chain: a Safari/OS X sandbox escape (because Edge on macOS, naturally) combined with a timing-side-channel in the Edge browser process model, plus a SOP bypass via URL parsing differentials. The exploit is described as "the last proof of human craft" — a deliberately poetic title that reflects Tsai's view that full-chain browser exploits built from logic bugs represent the pinnacle of vulnerability research before AI-generated exploits render human-crafted chains obsolete. The write-up is a masterclass in browser security research, covering every layer of the Edge security model — sandbox, Same Origin Policy, network isolation, process confinement — and demonstrating how a chain of individually mild logic errors can combine to produce a full compromise.

Orange Tsai calling something "the last proof of human craft" should give you chills. This is a researcher who has found more browser vulnerabilities than most teams have found bugs — if he's saying logic-chain exploits are the last bastion of human-dominant vulnerability research before AI takes over, that's a signal worth listening to. The write-up itself is remarkable: a full browser compromise achieved through pure logic, no memory corruption, no buffer overflows — just careful analysis of how different components of the security model interact in unexpected ways. This is the kind of work that makes you realise how fragile browser security really is. The chains are getting shorter. The logic is getting deeper. 🔋 US Added a Record 20.2 GWh of Battery Storage in Just 3 Months — 239 Points

Battery storage is scaling faster than almost anyone predicted five years ago, and the economic case is getting stronger with every gigawatt-hour deployed. The IRA's investment tax credit for standalone storage (passed in 2022) is having exactly the effect intended: turning storage from a niche technology into a grid-scale asset class. The falling pack costs (below $90/kWh at pack level for lithium-ion) mean that even without subsidies, 4-hour storage is now competitive with gas peaker plants in most US markets. The climate wins are real, and they're happening faster than the headlines suggest. 🚆 Spain Mourns 69 Dead in Second High-Speed Rail Disaster This Year

AP News · BBC News

Spain is in mourning today after a high-speed train collision near Urda, in the province of Toledo, killed at least 69 people and injured dozens more. The crash — the second deadly rail incident this year — occurred when a Renfe high-speed train travelling from Madrid to Seville struck the rear of a stationary Iryo train at an estimated 200 km/h (124 mph). The cause: a computer failure on a 63-kilometre stretch of track disabled the automatic LZB safety system at 8:30am, causing all trains on that stretch to stop. But the central control centre in Madrid's Atocha station then instructed the Renfe train to resume full speed — despite a stop signal still being active on the line. Transport Minister Óscar Puente called the order "inexplicable." The collision came just nine months after a January crash on the same line killed 46 people — also involving Renfe and Iryo trains. Spain has declared three days of mourning. The election campaign has been suspended by all major parties except the far-right Vox, and protests outside the transport ministry in Madrid turned violent on Wednesday night. Among the deceased is former Labour Minister Fátima Báñez.

Two high-speed rail disasters in nine months, on the same line, involving the same operators — and the cause of both appears to be human error compounded by system failure. The Urda crash is Spain's deadliest rail accident since 2013, and the political fallout is already reshaping an election campaign. The leaked audio recordings of control-room operators — published against the wishes of the rail unions — paint a picture of confusion and conflicting instructions in the minutes before the crash. The central question is whether Spain's high-speed rail network, once the envy of Europe, has been pushed beyond its safety margin by cost pressures and political pressure. The January crash was supposed to be a wake-up call. Nine months later, 69 more people are dead. The computer failure that disabled the safety system is the proximate cause, but the systemic cause — the organisational culture that allowed a control centre to override safety protocols — is what Spain's investigators need to address. The pond holds the silence for 69 lives cut short by a control room decision that should never have been made.
