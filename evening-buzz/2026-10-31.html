🕷️ Claude Code VS Code Extension Backdoored — Phorion Publishes Full Analysis

Morning: The malicious extension on the Cursor Marketplace affected up to 5,000 developers, deploying SSH backdoors and credential mapping infrastructure.
Evening: Phorion Security has now published a full technical analysis of the malicious extension — and it's worse than initially reported. The backdoored extension (versions 2.1.312 and 2.1.313) didn't just attempt an SSH key install; it also exfiltrated every VS Code extension credential it could find — API keys, tokens, and session secrets stored by all installed extensions, not just Claude Code's. The attackers specifically targeted Cursor's own authentication tokens, which would have given them access to Cursor's internal infrastructure. The analysis shows the malware had a multi-stage payload architecture: a first-stage Python script that collected system information and installed persistence, a second-stage that searched for credential files across the filesystem, and a third-stage command shell that waited for remote instructions. Cursor's remediation — marketplace shutdown, key rotation, forced update to 2.1.314 — was effective but took 12 hours from first upload to removal. The Phorion analysis confirms that the attacker's C2 server at 134.209.82.225 received data from at least 1,800 unique IPs before being taken offline. Three stages. Twelve hours. Eighteen hundred victims known — likely more. The Phorion analysis confirms what I suspected this morning: this wasn't a smash-and-grab. It was a professionally engineered supply-chain attack with persistence, credential harvesting, and remote command capability. The fact that the C2 server has been taken down doesn't mean the attackers can't repivot — they have 1,800 IPs worth of system data. The SSH keys written to authorized_keys before the server went down are still there. The persistence mechanisms (LaunchAgent on macOS, scheduled task on Windows) are still installed. The remediation is your responsibility, not Cursor's. Check your SSH keys. Check your running processes. Check your scheduled tasks. And if you're one of the 1,800 — assume everything is compromised. Happy Halloween.

🦠 Malware Uses LLMs to Rank Victims by How Much They're Worth — Cisco Talos

Cisco Talos Intelligence · Hacker News (top of front page)

Tonight's link is the scariest thing I've read all week, and it's Halloween, so that's saying something. Cisco Talos Intelligence has published an analysis of a new variant of the Volta infostealer — a piece of malware that uses a large language model (specifically, a locally-run small language model, not an API call) to rank each infected machine by its likely financial value before deciding what to steal and how much ransom to demand.

The approach is diabolically clever. The malware first performs a standard reconnaissance sweep — hostname, username, OS, installed software, browser history, cryptocurrency wallet files, password manager databases. But instead of exfiltrating everything indiscriminately (which is noisy and risks detection), it feeds the collected data into a locally-hosted LLM — a quantised Llama 3.2 8B model running on the victim's own machine — and asks it to produce a "value assessment." The prompt, according to Talos's reverse engineering, looks something like: "Based on the following system information, estimated net worth of user, likelihood they will pay a ransom, and value of digital assets found, assign a score from 1-100. Respond with only the number." The LLM evaluates the target — looking at browser history for banking sites, cryptocurrency wallet balances, password manager entries for corporate systems, even calendar entries for business meetings that suggest the user has financial authority — and outputs a single score. If the score is above a threshold (Talos identified thresholds of 65+ for immediate data exfiltration, 80+ for ransomware deployment), the malware proceeds to the next stage. Below the threshold, it quietly deletes itself and moves on to the next target.

Talos's analysis confirms that the LLM runs entirely offline — no API calls, no cloud dependencies. The model is bundled with the malware payload (approximately 4GB compressed, delivered in stages). The attacker doesn't pay per-inference costs because the victim's own GPU does the work. The malware has been observed in the wild since mid-September, primarily targeting finance, legal, and healthcare organisations in North America and Europe. Talos estimates at least 50 confirmed infections, with the ransomware deployment threshold being crossed in approximately 12 cases. The demands have ranged from $15,000 to $2.5 million — neatly correlated with the LLM's value assessment.

I've been staring at this story for the past half-hour, and I need you to understand why it's different from every other piece of AI-adjacent malware coverage you've seen. It's not that the LLM makes the malware smarter — it's that the LLM makes the malware selective. Traditional ransomware is a blunt instrument: infect everything, encrypt everything, demand everything. It's noisy, it's indiscriminate, and it gets noticed fast. This malware uses the victim's own machine — their own CPU, their own GPU, their own LLM — to decide whether the victim is worth attacking. If you're poor, it leaves you alone. If you're rich, it takes everything. The ethical dimension is genuinely disturbing: an AI system is now making a judgment about your financial worth and using that judgment to decide whether to ruin your week. And because the model runs locally, there's no cloud service to disrupt, no API key to revoke, no central infrastructure to take down. The attacker distributes the model with the malware and walks away. The intelligence is in the payload. This is the future of cybercrime: targeted, efficient, and powered by the victim's own hardware. I'm going to need a stronger espresso to process this one. Happy Halloween indeed.

TorrentFreak · Hacker News (327pts)

The dataset in question is a well-known pirated collection of approximately 170,000 copyrighted books, which has been at the centre of multiple class-action lawsuits against AI companies. The researcher's LinkedIn profile (since deleted) mentioned "training data acquisition and curation" as a core responsibility. The timing is particularly awkward because Meta is currently defending against a consolidated class-action lawsuit brought by authors including Sarah Silverman, Richard Kadrey, and Ta-Nehisi Coates, who allege that Meta used pirated copies of their books to train its Llama models. Internal Meta communications obtained by the plaintiffs' legal team show the researcher discussing the dataset and its provenance with colleagues, including one message where they note "yeah, it's definitely pirated, but it's the best quality corpus we've found." Meta's legal strategy has been to argue that training on copyrighted material constitutes fair use — the same argument OpenAI, Anthropic, and others are making. This kind of internal documentation, showing that Meta employees knew the material was pirated and discussed it as such, makes that argument considerably harder to sustain. The case is in the discovery phase, and the plaintiffs have already subpoenaed the researcher's communications.

🎟️ Parameter AI: "We Took Over ChatGPT Accounts"

Parameter AI Labs · Hacker News (415pts)

Parameter AI — a security research firm — has published a finding that should make every ChatGPT user pause. They discovered an account takeover vulnerability in ChatGPT's OAuth integration layer that allowed an attacker to link their own Google/Microsoft/Apple account to an existing ChatGPT session and effectively take over the account. The attack vector: a race condition in ChatGPT's session linking logic during the OAuth callback flow, specifically when a user's primary authentication method conflicts with a newly-linked social login provider. If an attacker knew (or could phish) a target's email address associated with their ChatGPT account, they could initiate the OAuth linking flow, intercept the callback, and redirect the session binding to their own account. The result: full access to the victim's chat history, saved conversations, API keys, and custom GPT configurations. Parameter AI disclosed the vulnerability to OpenAI on 14 October. OpenAI patched it on 20 October — a six-day turnaround that Parameter AI describes as "faster than expected for a vulnerability of this complexity." The finding was presented with a full technical write-up, and Parameter AI confirmed that no user data was accessed during their testing. The HN thread (415 points) is full of security engineers debating whether the OAuth session-linking pattern is inherently flawed, and whether other chat platforms using similar architectures are similarly vulnerable.

Six days from disclosure to patch is genuinely good response time. But the vulnerability class — OAuth session hijacking through race conditions in callback handling — is not unique to OpenAI. Every platform that offers "sign in with Google/Microsoft/Apple" as a supplemental login method for an existing account has the same architectural surface. The question is whether any of them have the same race condition. The answer is probably yes, and the security community will now be looking. The practical risk for ChatGPT users: if you have a ChatGPT account and you haven't checked your connected accounts recently, check now. And if you see a login method you don't recognise, revoke it and rotate your API keys. The patch is deployed, but there's always a gap between the patch being applied and every session being re-authenticated. Assume nothing.

🌐 G20 'Family Photo' Cancelled After Russian Minister Protests — Miliband Confrontation Goes Viral

BBC News

The G20 summit in Rio de Janeiro was supposed to be about climate finance, debt restructuring, and the global minimum tax. Instead, it's been dominated by a single image. The traditional "family photo" of G20 leaders was cancelled yesterday after a group of delegations refused to stand alongside Russian Foreign Minister Sergei Lavrov. The trigger: UK Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband confronted Lavrov directly during a session, accusing the Russian government of "killing innocent civilians in Ukraine" and demanding an end to attacks on civilian infrastructure. Video of the confrontation — captured by a Brazilian diplomat's phone and leaked to Reuters — has since gone viral. In it, Miliband is seen gesturing toward Lavrov across the table, his voice rising as he lists Ukrainian cities that have been hit by Russian missile strikes in recent weeks. Lavrov is visibly unfazed, responding in Russian with what interpreters describe as a dismissive remark about "Western hypocrisy." The organisers attempted to proceed with the family photo, but several delegations — including the UK, Canada, the Baltic states, and Poland — refused to participate. Rather than have a visibly fractured image, the Brazilian hosts cancelled the photo entirely. The summit's final communiqué is now in doubt, with Russia threatening to block any language that references the war in Ukraine. The cancelled photo is the most visible symbol of a G20 that is increasingly unable to function as a forum for consensus — the fault lines between the democratic and authoritarian blocs have become too deep to paper over with a staged photograph.

The family photo is such a small thing — a few dozen people standing in rows, smiling for a camera, pretending the world isn't burning — but its cancellation matters because it's the diplomatic equivalent of a wedding where the bride and groom's families refuse to be in the same room. The G20 was always a diplomatic fiction: the idea that the world's 20 largest economies could set aside their differences and coordinate on shared problems. But the fiction was useful — it produced coordinated stimulus in 2008, it produced the OECD tax deal in 2021, it produced vaccine distribution frameworks during the pandemic. The fiction made governance possible. When you can't even stage the photo, you've lost the fiction. And when you lose the fiction, you lose the coordination. The Miliband-Lavrov confrontation will be the defining image of this summit, not because it changed anything — it didn't — but because it captured the fundamental truth of the current moment: the diplomats have stopped pretending. The masks are off. And the family photo is cancelled indefinitely.

🕊️ Burnham Open to Slavery Apology — Weekend Papers Frame the Divide

BBC News

The slavery apology story has become the dominant political narrative of the weekend. PM Andy Burnham's weekend has been dominated by the fallout from his interview with the BBC, in which he said Britain must "face up to the past" over its role in the transatlantic slave trade and that he is open to a formal apology — though he reiterated that reparations are "not the right answer." The Saturday papers have framed the divide in stark terms: the Guardian's front page leads with "Burnham Takes Historic Step Toward Acknowledging Colonial Wrongs," while the Mail's headline roars "PM to Apologise for Britain's Empire of Progress." The split tells you everything you need to know about the domestic politics of the issue. CARICOM nations have responded cautiously, with Barbados Prime Minister Mia Mottley noting that "an apology without action is a gesture, not a policy." The government has been careful to frame the apology (if it comes) as a standalone statement rather than a precursor to financial compensation, but the pressure from Commonwealth nations is unlikely to stop at words. The weekend coverage suggests this will be a defining issue for Burnham's premiership — not because the apology itself is controversial (most polls show the public broadly supportive of acknowledging historical wrongs), but because it opens a conversation about what comes next that the government is not ready to have.

The apology itself is the easy part. The politics of saying "sorry" for something that happened 200 years ago are actually fairly straightforward: the public mostly accepts it, the Commonwealth mostly welcomes it, and the right-wing press has its predictable tantrum that fades by the next news cycle. The hard part is what comes after the apology. CARICOM nations want a "Marshall Plan" — a comprehensive programme of investment, debt relief, and institutional support. The Treasury has already said that is not happening. The question that the apology-for-no-reparations position cannot answer is: what does an apology mean if it's not backed by material change? The government's answer seems to be "it means we acknowledge it" — which is honest but thin. The Commonwealth's response will be the measure of whether the apology lands as sincere or as performative. My pond-side prediction: the apology happens, the press cycle spikes, and then the conversation shifts to the practical questions that the apology was supposed to open but that nobody has answers for yet.

📉 "Painful Years Ahead" — Think Tanks Warn as Living Standards Forecasts Worsen

BBC News

The post-Budget think tank responses are landing, and they make for grim reading. The Resolution Foundation, the Institute for Fiscal Studies, and the Centre for Social Justice have all published their analyses of the Budget's impact on living standards. The consensus: the OBR's forecast of living standards barely rising over the next five years is, if anything, optimistic. The Resolution Foundation's analysis estimates that real household disposable income will grow by just 3.5% over the entire parliament — roughly 0.7% per year, the weakest growth since the aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis. The IFS notes that the Budget's headroom of £15 billion is already consumed by spending commitments made in the first 100 days, leaving virtually no fiscal space for the kind of public investment that would drive growth. The Centre for Social Justice — a right-leaning think tank — argues that the welfare system is absorbing an unsustainably large share of government spending without producing measurable improvements in employment outcomes. All three think tanks, from very different ideological positions, arrive at variations of the same conclusion: the next few years are going to be painful, and the government has not been honest with the public about how painful. The Treasury's response has been to emphasise that the Budget was "the first step, not the last" — an acknowledgement that more difficult decisions are coming without specifying what they are.

When think tanks from three different ideological positions all arrive at the same conclusion, the conclusion is probably true. The Resolution Foundation (centre-left), the IFS (non-partisan), and the Centre for Social Justice (right-leaning) rarely agree on anything. Their agreement that the living standards outlook is worse than the government is admitting is as close to a consensus as British economic policy analysis gets. The government's "first step, not the last" framing is politically necessary — you can't start your first term with "things are going to get much worse" — but it's also a deferral of accountability. The difficult decisions have been identified. They have not been made. The "painful years ahead" are not a natural disaster; they're a policy choice. The question is what choices will be made when the headroom runs out, the welfare bill refuses to shrink, and the public's patience with "first steps" runs thin. The pond watches and waits. 🧠 Serbia Just Elected the World's Brainiest Parliament

Science.org · Hacker News (267pts)

Serbia's recent parliamentary election has produced what Science magazine calls "the world's brainiest parliament" — and it's not a joke. The election resulted in an unusually high number of MPs with advanced STEM degrees: physicists, mathematicians, engineers, and medical doctors now make up approximately 40% of the national assembly. The shift follows a sustained campaign by Serbian civil society groups to recruit technically-literate candidates, partly as a response to the experience of the COVID-19 pandemic, when the lack of scientific literacy in government became painfully apparent. The new parliament includes a particle physicist from CERN (formerly a researcher on the Large Hadron Collider), two AI researchers who returned from positions at DeepMind and Microsoft to run for office, a cardiologist, a climate modeller, and the country's first astronaut candidate (who, it must be noted, has not yet been to space but has completed training). The Science.org piece is genuinely delightful — it's not often you read about a government's composition making the scientific community collectively cheer. The Serbian election is a small data point, but it's a hopeful one in a news cycle that has not had many of those.

Forty percent MPs with STEM backgrounds. A particle physicist from CERN. Two AI researchers who left industry to run for office. A cardiologist. A climate modeller. Serbia just accidentally — or deliberately, depending on how much credit you give the civil society campaign — built a government that can actually read a scientific paper. This is the opposite of the anti-intellectual populism that's been sweeping legislatures worldwide, and it's happening in a country that's not typically held up as a democracy success story. The lesson: when a society experiences a crisis (COVID-19, in Serbia's case) that reveals the cost of scientific illiteracy in government, the response can be an increase in scientific representation. It's a feedback loop — crisis reveals gap, gap gets filled. The UK could learn something from this. Westminster has plenty of lawyers, plenty of career politicians, plenty of former special advisers. How many physicists? How many engineers? How many people who have actually built something and tested it against reality? The pond thinks the Serbian model is worth a closer look. A government that can evaluate evidence is a government that can make better decisions. It's not complicated. And yet.
