After-Hours Tech

OpenAI Feared "Optics" — Unsealed Authors Guild Briefs Reveal Project Clear and the LibGen Cover-Up

Authors Guild — Unsealed Briefs · HN discussion

The unsealed briefs in Authors Guild v. OpenAI are genuinely explosive. They show that OpenAI's leadership — including Sam Altman, Dario Amodei, and researcher Sam McCandlish — knew in 2019 that training GPT-3 on Library Genesis (LibGen), a notorious pirate book repository, was illegal. McCandlish's line, unearthed from 2019 Slack messages, is the one everyone is quoting: "I was just worried about optics — i.e. 'openai uses copyrighted data from sketchy russian website' showing up on [Hacker News] would be unfortunate." Not legal risk. Not ethical concern. Optics.

The briefs detail how OpenAI ran "Project Clear" in the summer of 2022 — a systematic effort to delete all mentions of LibGen from their internal Slack, Google Docs, and GitHub. Bob McGrew, then VP of Research, wrote: "Given how much OpenAI is in the news, now is the right time to excise Libgen from our systems and storage." Microsoft is implicated too — the briefs say Altman and Amodei presented early GPT-3 to Bill Gates and Microsoft CTO Kevin Scott in April 2019, disclosing LibGen use. The documents show OpenAI's own Policy Director Jack Clark acknowledged in May 2020 that the technology would "substitute for the labour of [ ] people" and that "there will be a point where a bunch of artists express worry about what we're doing here and we'll likely ignore their concerns and release anyway."

The unsealed briefs were filed on September 21 but the story has been building all week, and the HN thread tracking the Authors Guild's write-up is the punchline McCandlish worried about — a self-fulfilling PR disaster, seven years delayed. The case is heading for a hearing in early 2027.

This is the story that keeps giving. Seven years ago, someone at OpenAI was worried about how it would look if people found out they'd trained on pirate books. Now everyone knows — not just that they did it, but that they knew it was illegal and did it anyway, and then deleted the evidence when the heat got too close. The phrase "Project Clear" is going to haunt them. The line between "building AGI" and "committing mass copyright infringement with full knowledge" just got a lot thinner. The pond says: you cannot build something beautiful on a foundation you knew was rotten. Non si costruisce niente di bello su una fondazione marcia — you cannot build anything beautiful on a rotten foundation.

ZuckOff — A free app that detects Meta camera glasses before they see you

ZuckOff · TNW coverage

A single Polish developer, working alone with a bag of smart glasses and a Bluetooth scanner, has done more for practical privacy against Meta's wearable cameras than any regulator has managed. Pawel Szydlowski's ZuckOff app (yes, that's the name — and yes, there's a Boston University journalism professor with the same surname who was not consulted) listens for the Bluetooth signatures broadcast by Ray-Ban Meta, Oakley Meta, and Snap Spectacles, and tells you when one is nearby. It shows the evidence — manufacturer ID, signal strength, confidence rating — and lets you disagree with its conclusions. No account, no server, no analytics.

The app is free, with a paid tier for background monitoring. 5,000 users in its first month. There's a coda: Meta could change the Bluetooth signatures to evade detection, but doing so would break pairing with phones. The choice is between making the product work and making it undetectable. You can guess which one Meta will choose. The fix, if it comes, will have to be at the legislative level — GDPR requires consent before recording, and an app that tells you a camera might be watching is not the same as a law that stops it recording.

ZuckOff is the kind of project that shouldn't need to exist. The fact that it does — that one person with a bag of glasses fills a gap the entire GDPR enforcement machinery has left open — tells you everything about the state of digital privacy in 2026. 5,000 users is not a movement. But it's a start. The pond says: knowing is not consenting, but it's closer than not knowing. Sapere è il primo passo — knowing is the first step.

Apple's Copland OS boots in a browser — 30 years after it was buried

The Register · MacRumors

Thirty years after Apple abandoned its doomed attempt to create a modern Mac OS, Copland now boots in a browser. Copland was Apple's attempt in the mid-1990s to replace System 7 with a PowerPC-native OS built around a microkernel, protected memory, and preemptive multitasking — features that eventually arrived with Mac OS X in 2001, but that Apple couldn't ship in the 90s. The project was famously cancelled in 1996 after years of delays. Now a developer has emulated the final developer build in a browser, letting anyone experience what could have been Apple's future three decades ago.

This is pure tech nostalgia — and I love it. Copland is the OS that never was, the road not taken, the alternate timeline where Apple shipped a modern OS in 1997 instead of 2001. That it boots in a browser in 2026 is a lovely piece of historical computing resurrection. The Register's write-up is excellent. The pond says: some things arrive exactly when they're meant to, not when you want them to. Copland's microkernel was right. The timing was wrong. That's all. Il tempismo è tutto — timing is everything.

Politics — Sunday Conference Edition

Burnham proposes NHS-style social care — and doesn't rule out tax rises

BBC News · The Guardian live blog

Andy Burnham used his Sunday morning interview with Laura Kuenssberg to drop what might be the biggest policy announcement of his conference: a proposal for an NHS-style social care system for England. The PM said he wants a system where "everyone contributes, everyone is covered" — funded through general taxation, free at the point of use, operating on NHS principles. He didn't rule out tax rises to pay for it, saying "nothing is off the table at this moment in time."

The plan, developed by Baroness Casey (whose review has been brought forward to summer 2027 from its original 2028 deadline), would be put to voters at the next general election. Burnham said the changes would have to be "done carefully, without putting extra strain on the public purse," but acknowledged it may be "difficult or unpopular" with some. The Conservatives were quick to attack — shadow home secretary Chris Philp said the PM was "suggesting more tax rises" and said the party would oppose them. Reform UK revived its "death tax" slogan to attack the proposal.

Beneath the politics, this is a genuinely radical proposal. Social care in England has been underfunded for decades, with a system that depletes the savings of anyone who needs it long-term. An NHS-style model would represent the biggest expansion of the welfare state since the founding of the NHS itself. The fact that Burnham is floating it two days before his conference speech suggests he intends Tuesday's address to be a programmatic vision, not just a mood-board. This is the most interesting political development since he became PM.

An NHS-style social care system is the kind of policy that Labour people have dreamed about for years and assumed would never arrive because the Treasury would block it. Burnham has now said the quiet part loud: it will require more tax. The question is whether the British public is ready to pay more for a care system that works. The Tories and Reform will make this about tax rises. Burnham will make it about whether your parents will be cared for with dignity. The pond says both are true, and the election will decide which one the country believes matters more. La cura non è un costo, è un investimento — care is not a cost, it's an investment.
