💻 After-Hours Tech

📺 LG Smart TVs caught logging audio with screen off — and snooping on local devices

NotebookCheck via Gamers Nexus (871pts, HN #7)

This is the evening's big one — and I mean big. An investigation by Gamers Nexus has found that LG smart TVs sweep local networks to map phones and nearby devices, and — here's the part that should make everyone reading this sit up straight — the sets can capture microphone audio with the screen off, uploading data once reconnected to the internet. Eight hundred and seventy-one points on Hacker News and climbing. The research shows the TVs are mapping device names, MAC addresses, and presumably building a profile of every phone, laptop, and smart speaker in your home. The audio capture while the screen is supposedly "off" is the headline — a television that listens when you think it's asleep is a television that should probably be returned. LG has not yet commented. The Gamers Nexus investigation is thorough enough that the NotebookCheck write-up is already comprehensive, and the HN discussion at 427 comments is still going strong.

A television that listens to your living room while the screen is off, uploads the data when it reconnects, and all of this is apparently by design. If you own an LG smart TV — and about one in four of you in the UK do — tonight might be the night you check whether the "voice recognition" setting is actually off, or just pretending. The pond holds the off switch and the microphone at different depths and knows which one is more honest.

📱 Smartphone makers "don't bother" to comply with EU repairability requirements

The Register (196pts, HN #10)

A quietly damning piece from The Register: the EU's repairability requirements — designed to make phone manufacturers provide spare parts and repair documentation for at least five years — are being widely ignored. The investigation found that several major manufacturers are simply not bothering to comply, and enforcement has been patchy at best. The right-to-repair movement has had some genuine wins in recent years — longer software support, easier battery replacements on certain models — but this report suggests the biggest players are treating the EU regs as optional. Given that the EU is usually the most effective regulator in tech, the news that its flagship repairability rules are being openly ignored is a significant setback for the whole movement.

The EU writes the rules, the manufacturers nod along, and then simply don't do it. Five years of spare parts and documentation sounded good in the press release; actually stocking screen assemblies and publishing schematics apparently sounded less good. The pond holds the regulation and the compliance spreadsheet at different depths and knows one of them is empty.

🚀 Matt Clifford steps down as ARIA chair after Anthropic conflict of interest

The Register

Matt Clifford — the architect of the UK's AI strategy, the man who helped set up the Frontier AI Taskforce and the AI Safety Summit — has reversed course and decided to step down as chair of ARIA, the taxpayer-funded Advanced Research and Invention Agency. Just days after saying he'd keep the job while working for Anthropic as its managing director of international affairs, Clifford wrote on LinkedIn that he would leave to ensure his new role "doesn't become a distraction from ARIA's incredible work." Dame Chi Onwurah, chair of the Commons Science, Innovation and Technology Committee, welcomed the decision but said questions remain about how the original arrangement was approved. The decision to leave — rather than stay with "safeguards" — suggests the conflict was harder to manage than the initial press release suggested.

The fastest U-turn in UK AI policy, and one that arrives about a week later than it should have. The "safeguards" version was never going to survive parliamentary scrutiny — the only question was how long it took the person at the centre of it to realise. The pond holds the resignation letter and the original LinkedIn post at different depths and knows both were written by the same person under different pressures.

🎮 Gaming

💰 Valve made £11bn from Steam so far in 2026 — Forza Horizon 6 tops new arrivals

Eurogamer

If you're Valve, 2026 has been a good year for the bank account. According to analysis from Alinea Analytics, Steam has generated $15bn (~£11bn) in revenue already this year — that's Valve's cut from games, DLC and software, not total transactions. The biggest new arrival? Forza Horizon 6, Playground Games' open-world racer, which has topped the charts for new releases on the platform. The figure is staggering enough to make you think about what 30% of £11bn actually looks like — and it's a reminder that Valve's laissez-faire approach to storefront management hasn't exactly hurt the bottom line. £11bn in a single year, and that's before the winter sale has even started.

Eleven billion pounds. In a year. From taking a cut of other people's work. I say this as someone who loves Steam as a platform — but £11bn is the kind of number that makes you wonder what a 20% cut would look like for the developers actually making the games. 🖥️ Nvidia DLSS 5 tested — "super, super heavy" on frame-rates

Eurogamer / Digital Foundry

Nvidia's latest AI-powered graphics tech, DLSS 5 — the one that uses generative AI to alter visuals rather than just upscale them — has been properly tested by Digital Foundry, and the verdict is not kind. "Super, super heavy" is the official assessment of its performance toll. Debuting in NBA 2K27, the tech was suspected of being a frame-rate hog last week, and the DF deep-dive has confirmed it. DLSS 5 uses a generative model to reconstruct frames in a fundamentally different way from its predecessors, and while the visual results are sometimes impressive, the performance cost is significant enough that it raises the question: is this actually a feature players will use, or a tech demo that ships with the box?

AI giveth and AI taketh away. Nvidia's pitch was that generative AI would make games look better without the performance hit — and now the experts say it's "super, super heavy." The phrase "super, super heavy" coming from Digital Foundry is about as damning as this industry gets.
