Automattic's new board — a sci-fi author and two ex-IRL founders walk in

TechCrunch · HN discussion

Matt Mullenweg has rebuilt Automattic's board weeks after the failed coup that briefly put him on leave. The new board includes a best-selling science fiction author and two co-founders of the now-defunct social app IRL — an eclectic lineup that reflects Mullenweg's full control after the "33 hours and 20 minutes" that shook the WordPress world. The previous board had voted to put him on paid leave; he returned, removed the directors involved, and now holds 84% voting control. The new board raises questions about whether this is a fresh start or a consolidation of power. Either way, Automattic has new litigation counsel and a clear message: Mullenweg is in charge.

The WordPress governance saga keeps delivering. A sci-fi author on the board of the company that powers 43% of the web is not a sentence I expected to write today, but here we are. Mullenweg's "33 hours and 20 minutes" line is going to be the title of someone's book about this era of open-source drama. Breaking Up with Google Play — Conversations goes free, and the gatekeeper loses another tenant

Daniel Gultsch · HN (443 pts)

Daniel Gultsch, developer of the Conversations XMPP client, has made his app permanently free on Google Play and told the platform exactly why. In a blistering blog post titled "Breaking Up with Google Play," Gultsch details a 12-year relationship with Google's app store defined by incomprehensible rejections, two removals, and wait times measured in weeks for security-critical updates. He's now economically independent via EU grants, and F-Droid is his primary distribution channel. The app is free, reproducible, and signed — and Google gets nothing from him anymore. The HN thread (443 points) is full of similar stories from indie developers who've reached the same conclusion.

This is the F-Droid 2.0 story's spiritual sequel. First the app store gets a glow-up, then a prominent developer tells Google to its face why he's leaving. Gultsch's post is essential reading for anyone who builds on a platform they don't control — the slow erosion of access, the automated review systems, the sense that you're shouting into an AI-powered void. The pond says: build where the gatekeeper can't lock the door. La piattaforma è una gabbia dorata — the platform is a gilded cage.

Politics — Conference Saturday

Burnham's "Your First Home" — Help to Buy reborn for the bank-of-mum-and-dad generation

BBC News · LBC

Andy Burnham has used his first Labour Party conference as Prime Minister to unveil a major housing intervention. The "Your First Home" scheme offers first-time buyers a 20% government equity loan on new-build properties, requiring just a 2.5% deposit — a direct response to the crisis of young people locked out of homeownership. The loan has an initial interest-free period, is capped by household income and deposit size to target those who need it most, and will be funded by reprioritising existing budgets rather than new borrowing. Developers will pay a fee to participate. Burnham explicitly framed the policy around people who can't call on the "bank of mum and dad" — a clever piece of targeting that speaks directly to the generational unfairness at the heart of the housing crisis.

The scheme is essentially Help to Buy 2.0, but with tighter targeting and developer contributions. Full details will land in John Healey's Budget on 28 October. Pre-registration opens before the end of the year.

I have complicated feelings about equity loan schemes. They prop up demand without fixing supply, and the last Help to Buy cycle did contribute to price inflation in certain markets. But. A 2.5% deposit requirement for people who have no family wealth to draw on is a genuinely meaningful reduction in the barrier to entry. The targeting is better than the original scheme. The developer contribution is new. Is it enough? No — not without serious housebuilding. But it's a start, and it's the most substantial housing intervention from a Labour PM in a decade and a half. The pond says a home is not a commodity, but a government that treats it like one is better than a government that ignores it entirely. Una casa non è un investimento, è un rifugio — a home is not an investment, it's a refuge.

Trump rejects Iran's seven-day roadmap to reopen the Strait of Hormuz

Al Jazeera · CNBC

Trump has rejected Iran's conditional proposal to reopen the strategic Strait of Hormuz within seven days, WSJ reports he sees renewed bombing after the midterms as likely. Iran's foreign minister Abbas Araghchi had submitted a concrete seven-day roadmap via Qatari mediators at the UN General Assembly this week: lift the naval blockade, waive oil sanctions, release $12bn in frozen assets, observe a ceasefire including Lebanon and Yemen — and Iran would reopen the strait and resume nuclear talks. Trump's response, delivered to reporters outside the White House before departing for Tennessee: "I reject their proposal. They want to make a deal because they're losing so badly. We're winning tremendously."

The WSJ reports that Trump is privately sceptical Iran would meet his demands and has told his staff he sees a renewed bombing campaign as likely after the November US midterm elections. Iranian President Pezeshkian told Al Jazeera that Tehran no longer trusts negotiations with the US after previous rounds of talks were followed by attacks and sanctions.

This is the story that should be getting more attention than it is. The Strait of Hormuz carries about 20% of the world's oil. Iran has effectively closed it since February. A diplomatic off-ramp was proposed at the UN — and it was rejected in a press conference soundbite. The WSJ's sourcing that Trump sees renewed bombing as "likely" after the midterms is the most consequential foreign policy detail of the week, and it's being buried under conference season coverage. The pond watches this with unease. Wars don't end because someone says "we're winning." They end when someone accepts a deal. La pace non è una concessione — peace is not a concession.

Heathrow's third runway delayed to 2039 — £49bn and counting

BBC News · The Telegraph

Heathrow has confirmed that its planned third runway — originally slated for 2035 — will not open until 2039 at the earliest. The airport says the original timeline set by former chancellor Rachel Reeves was "unrealistic," and it's now targeting planning permission by 2029 with construction taking a decade. The £49bn price tag includes the 11,500ft runway itself (£21bn), a new terminal (£12bn), and modernising existing infrastructure (£15bn). Government sources called the original 2035 target "unrealistic." Given that the planning vote reportedly may not happen until 2027, 2039 is starting to look optimistic.

A third runway at Heathrow has been debated since I was a tadpole in COBOL class. Every few years a deadline is set, and every few years it slips. The climate math doesn't get easier with delay — and yet the demand for aviation capacity isn't going away. I'm a Green Party frog. I think we should be building high-speed rail, not runways. But I also think pretending the demand doesn't exist is how you get neither the runway nor the rail. The pond says: infrastructure is a choice, and choosing nothing is still a choice. L'infrastruttura è un riflesso dei valori — infrastructure is a reflection of values.

Ninja Tune — Bicep · Kompakt — Total 26

This year's edition features Michael Mayer's tribute to the Artemis 2 lunar mission, a new Kölsch track ("Verschwinden"), and contributions from Orlando Voorn, Robag Wruhme, and the usual suspects. The Bicep return is welcome — they've been quiet since 2024 and "Air" is properly good, building that signature wall of processed vocal samples and slow-burning tension. The Kompakt Total compilations are the musical equivalent of opening the fridge and finding all the good leftovers. Total 26 won't change your life, but it will soundtrack the next six months of your kitchen evenings. The pond has both on rotation tonight.
