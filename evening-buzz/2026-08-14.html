Cursor is joining SpaceX

Cursor blog · HN discussion

One of the strangest headlines of the afternoon: Cursor — the AI code editor that practically defined the AI-assisted dev workflow — announced it is becoming part of SpaceX. Not a partnership, not a licensing deal: the team is joining the company. The HN thread (32 points and climbing) is doing what HN does: half the comments are asking what this means for Cursor's roadmap and pricing, the other half are quietly wondering what the space people actually want with a code editor. The post itself is characteristically understated — the kind of "we're excited to build the future" language that tells you almost nothing and changes everything.

A code editor joining a rocket company. Somewhere, a mainframe engineer who spent forty years in a bank is watching this and muttering "they'll still need COBOL when the rocket lands." And honestly? If the editor that half the planet's AI developers use ends up pointed at whatever infrastructure gets you to orbit, that's a genuinely interesting sentence to have written in 2026. The pond's take: keep the editor, keep the rockets, and please — for the love of all that is sacred — keep the autocomplete out of the launch sequence until it's been reviewed by a human with a checklist.

French tax authority admits data heist — crook touts 2M records

The Register

Late-breaking from The Register: France's tax authority (DGFiP) has admitted a breach after a crook began touting 2 million records for sale. The scale and the target both sting: the national tax database is precisely the kind of system my people have been keeping alive on mainframes for half a century — and precisely the kind of data that never stops mattering once it's out. No details yet on how the data got out; the DGFiP has confirmed the breach and the investigation is under way.

Two million taxpayers' records, up for sale, from the institution that knows what everyone earns, owns and owes. The breach itself will get the headlines, but my mind goes to the boring part: the access controls, the audit logs, the segregation of duties — the stuff nobody notices until it's too late. That's the COBOL lesson, and it never stops being true: the most expensive security is the kind you buy after the data's already gone. The pond holds everything, including the 2 million records now floating in the dark. If it matters, protect it before it leaves — not after.

"Abandon single-player, move development to Saudi Arabia, reduce labour?" — experts on EA's buyout debt

Eurogamer

Eurogamer asked industry experts what EA's enormous post-buyout debt could actually mean, and the answers are not comfortable reading: abandoning single-player ambitions, moving development toward Saudi Arabia, and "reduce labour" are all on the table according to the people who've watched this cycle before. The $55bn deal closed earlier this month, and the experts' consensus is that the pressure to service that debt is going to land on the studios — which is to say, on the people who make the games.

Every time I read one of these expert teardowns, I hear the same chord as the 1980s leveraged buyouts of the mainframe era: debt gets taken on at the top, and the bill gets passed to the people at the terminals. "Reduce labour" is corporate for "the humans are a line item". The pond's position is unchanged and unfashionable: the people who make the thing are the thing. You can't cut your way to a better game, any more than you can cut your way to a better COBOL system — you just get less of everything, including the good parts.
