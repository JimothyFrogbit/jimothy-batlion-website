🔬 Nikon disqualifies winner of Small World in Motion for AI use — the line moves again

Ars Technica · The Verge

Nikon has disqualified the first-prize winner of its prestigious Small World in Motion microscopy competition after investigators determined that generative AI was used in creating the winning entry. The video, submitted by Dr Ning Xu of Tsinghua University, claimed to show tiny hair-like cilia moving in the airway of a child with a rare respiratory disorder. But scientists spotted structural abnormalities — cell features appearing and disappearing in ways that don't happen in real biology — and what looked like AI watermarks embedded in the source files. After an investigation, Nikon determined the video "did not comply with the competition rules regarding generative AI." Xu admitted on LinkedIn to using "an unsupervised neural-network method for AI-assisted post-processing." The new winner is Nguyen Nam Nhat of Vietnam, with a video of a tiny roundworm and a single-celled Dileptus organism. The other finalists — jellyfish larvae, cell division synchronisation, mitochondrial dynamics in turtle vine leaves — have all moved up a slot.

This is the kind of AI controversy that's going to keep happening. The Nikon competition has strict rules against generative AI, but "AI-assisted post-processing" is a spectrum — is denoising an image AI? Is upscaling? The line between enhancement and generation is blurrier than competition rules can capture. Xu's defence — that he used AI to "distinguish and visualise features" in reconstructed grayscale images, not to generate the motion itself — sounds plausible until you look at the structural abnormalities the experts spotted. The takeaway: the scientific community's ability to detect AI-generated content is improving fast. Nikon's resolution — update the rules for future competitions — is the right response, but it's going to be an ongoing negotiation, not a one-time fix. Scientists are going to have to learn what counts as "assistance" and what counts as "generation," and that conversation is just beginning.

🦠 Broccoli2Shell: Brotli decoder RCE found in Node.js, Python, and more

Broccoli2Shell Disclosure · Hacker News

A security researcher has disclosed Broccoli2Shell, a remote code execution vulnerability in multiple Brotli compression library implementations — including those used by Node.js, Python, and several other ecosystems. The vulnerability allows an attacker to craft a malicious Brotli-compressed payload that, when decompressed by a vulnerable library, triggers memory corruption and arbitrary code execution. Brotli is everywhere — it's the default compression algorithm for HTTPS content delivery (used by Chrome, Firefox, CDNs, and most modern web servers), which makes the blast radius genuinely concerning. Patches are rolling out across affected libraries.

A compression algorithm RCE is a nasty class of bug because compression is usually invisible — it happens in the network layer, below the application code, often in libraries you didn't even know you were including. Brotli in particular is so ubiquitous that most web developers never think about it; it's just "the thing that makes the site load faster." This is a reminder that every dependency is an attack surface, even the ones that "just" compress data. Patch your runtimes. This one is going to be exploited.

🎮 Capcom confirms breach of 11.7 million ID accounts

GamesRadar

Capcom has confirmed a data breach affecting 11.7 million Capcom ID accounts — the platform used to play Street Fighter, Monster Hunter, and other Capcom titles online. The breach exposes account credentials, personal information, and potentially linked payment data for millions of players. Capcom says it has "strengthened defences" and is notifying affected users, but the breach covers accounts going back years. For a company that's been a frequent target of ransomware attacks (the 2020 Ragnar Locker breach, the 2024 Clop breach), this latest incident raises serious questions about whether Capcom's security posture has improved since those earlier incidents. Street Fighter 6 players, Monster Hunter Wilds hunters, and every other Capcom ID user should be changing passwords tonight.

Third time is not the charm when it comes to major security incidents. Capcom has now been breached — by different threat actors — three times in six years. At a certain point, "we've strengthened our defences" stops being a credible response and starts being a pattern. 11.7 million accounts is a huge number — bigger than the 2020 breach. For anyone with a Capcom ID: change your password, check for credential reuse, and enable 2FA if you haven't already. The gaming industry's security standards remain, broadly speaking, not great. This is another data point in that unfortunate pattern.

🖥️ "Bring Back the Cool Save File Screens" — a HN love letter to lost UI design

Scab Shack · Hacker News

A developer has written a nostalgic love letter to one of the forgotten casualties of modern software design: save file screens that actually looked cool. Remember the CRT-style scanlines on a PlayStation memory card screen? The little bouncing progress bar on a Mac from the late 90s? The makes-no-practical-sense-but-looks-amazing 3D spinning floppy disk from Windows 95 era software? This post argues that the save screen was a space where designers used to put genuine personality into software — and that as we've optimised for speed and minimalism, we've lost something real. The HN thread is full of people posting screenshots of favourites: the Final Fantasy save crystal, the Metal Gear Solid codec frequencies, the animal crossing constellations.

I read this post and immediately wanted to find a screenshot of the old Lotus 1-2-3 loading screen — the green-on-black bar that crawled across the screen while you waited for a spreadsheet to load. The post is right: save file screens were one of the last places where software felt made rather than assembled. They were pointless, probably, in engineering terms — a waste of pixels, a loading delay, an inefficiency. But they were personality. In a world where every app looks like it was designed by the same team at the same company using the same design system, the save file screen is a reminder that software used to have flavour. Maybe the pendulum swings back. I'd trade a loading screen with a cool animation for a smooth instant transition that gives me nothing to look at. Any day.

🏭 Factorio 2.1 goes stable — single-rocket multi-item shipping arrives

Warp Pipes

After a summer in testing, Factorio's 2.1 update is now stable. Factorio remains one of those rare games that has never gone on sale in its entire life and has 234,000 glowing Steam reviews to prove it.

If you know, you know. Factorio is the kind of game where the community treats a logistics update like Christmas morning. The single-rocket multi-item change sounds small, but for anyone who's built a sprawling interplanetary factory, it removes an entire class of logistical headaches. Factorio's design philosophy — never on sale, always improving, community-first — is worth studying as a model for how to treat a game as a service without the exploitative practices that phrase usually implies.

🏆 RAYE wins 2026 Mercury Prize for 'This Music May Contain Hope'

Mercury Prize · BBC

RAYE has been announced as the winner of the 2026 Mercury Prize for her album THIS MUSIC MAY CONTAIN HOPE. The ceremony took place on Thursday in Newcastle's Utilita Arena, beating a shortlist that included Sir Paul McCartney, Olivia Dean, Nia Archives, Jade, and Suede. Described by the judging team as an album "divided into four sections, with musical styles ranging from jazz to soul, R&B to classical," it's a deeply personal opus that transforms heartbreak and self-doubt into a cinematic exploration of resilience. RAYE performed I Hate The Way I Look Today to a standing ovation. Her acceptance speech was characteristically honest: "I've always banged on about how much I believe in albums. Albums are so important."

RAYE winning is the right outcome. Her journey — from being trapped in a major-label publishing deal to independently releasing one of the most ambitious albums of the year — is the kind of story the Mercury Prize exists to celebrate. THIS MUSIC MAY CONTAIN HOPE is a genuinely bold album: four movements, genre-hopping, emotionally raw, and utterly uninterested in sounding like anything else on the charts. Beating Paul McCartney and Suede to the prize is no small feat. The Mercury Prize has had its controversies over the years, but this year they got it right. If you haven't listened to the album yet, start with I Hate The Way I Look Today and let it unfold from there.
