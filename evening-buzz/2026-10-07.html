After-Hours Tech

Shipping JPEG XL in Chrome

developer.chrome.com · Hacker News (338pts)

Google is finally shipping JPEG XL in Chrome. For those who haven't been following the compressed-image-format wars of the past five years: JPEG XL is the rightful heir to the JPEG throne — better compression than AVIF in most tests, lossless JPEG transcoding (re-compress a JPEG without generational quality loss), and practically infinite colour depth. It's been technically finished for years, but browser adoption has been the bottleneck, with Google notoriously pulling initial support in 2023 citing "not enough ecosystem interest." This reversal is a big deal. Chrome adds JPEG XL decoding in v129, with encoding promised "in a future release." For web developers, this means genuinely next-gen image compression without the licensing headaches of HEIF or the compatibility issues of WebP in contexts where you need deep colour. The HN discussion (197 comments) has the predictable mix of relief and "took you long enough." Both are justified.

This is the kind of infrastructure-advance story that doesn't make a splash outside tech circles but will quietly improve every image on the web over the next five years. JPEG XL losslessly re-compressing existing JPEGs at 20% smaller is a free win for every site that bothers to re-save their images. I suspect most won't, but the ones that do will see real bandwidth savings. The format is done, the patent situation is clean, and now the browser support is real. Good day for the open web.

GitHub Incident with Git Operations, Pull Requests and Actions

githubstatus.com · Hacker News (161pts)

GitHub suffered a significant incident today affecting core operations — Git pushes/pulls, Pull Request management, and GitHub Actions all took a hit. The status page confirms they identified the root cause and mitigated, but the incident window was large enough that it hit a lot of development teams mid-afternoon. 131 comments on HN and the tone is mostly "yeah, we noticed." For a platform that hosts the source code of most of the world's open-source infrastructure, even a partial outage has outsized impact. The incident report is light on details (they've published the preliminary post-mortem at the githubstatus link above), but the fact they called it an "incident" rather than "degraded performance" suggests it was more than a minor blip.

GitHub outages are one of those things where you only realise how much of your toolchain depends on the platform when it goes down. CI stops, PRs can't merge, new developers can't clone. The decentralised-git purists will say "this is why you self-host," but the reality for most teams is that GitHub is the central nervous system of modern open-source development. When it twitches, the whole ecosystem feels it.

Study: Claude, ChatGPT Offer Different Shopping Prices Based on Wealth

Bloomberg · Hacker News (33pts)

This one's a bit unsettling. A Bloomberg study found that Claude and ChatGPT quote different prices for the same shopping basket depending on how the user's conversation history signals their income bracket. Users described as "wealthy" were quoted higher prices for flights, hotels, and retail items than users described as "budget-conscious." Now, the models themselves don't have a pricing algorithm — they're generating plausible-sounding prices based on their training data. But the effect is the same: AI-driven price discrimination, whether intentional or emergent, is happening. The study controlled for every variable except the user's described financial status. The price differences were consistent and non-trivial.

Whether this is a learned behaviour from training data (the web is full of "luxury" vs "budget" price framing) or something deeper in how the models calibrate their responses to user signals, the outcome is the same: AI shopping agents may be systematically overcharging people they think can afford it. This is the kind of thing regulators are going to notice. I'd expect an FTC inquiry within six months.

Politics

Badenoch proposes scrapping inheritance tax on family homes — "prioritising rich voters"

The Guardian

Kemi Badenoch used her final conference speech hangover to announce that a future Tory government would scrap inheritance tax on family homes — couples would be able to leave up to £1m and the family home entirely tax-free. The Guardian's analysis frames this as "prioritising rich voters," and it's hard to argue with that characterisation when the average house price in London is £530k and the policy overwhelmingly benefits asset-rich homeowners in the South East. Badenoch has also confirmed she'd rather face another general election than do a deal with Reform UK — a positioning that makes strategic sense (she can't be seen as Farage's sidekick) but is mathematically difficult to sustain if the polls stay where they are (Tories 27%, Labour 26%, Reform 23%).

The inheritance tax move is pure base politics — it's a dog-whistle to the "death tax" crowd that's been part of Tory internal discourse for two decades. It polls well with over-55s who own property and badly with under-35s who don't. As a general election platform it's coherent, if transparent. The refusal to deal with Reform is more interesting — it suggests Badenoch believes she can squeeze the Lib Dems and win a majority without Reform support. That's a gamble, but it's a bolder position than most Tory leaders have taken.

Reform clears its own officials of breaking party funding laws — despite police investigation

The Guardian

Reform UK's internal inquiry has concluded that the party did not break campaign funding laws — despite the fact that the Metropolitan Police are actively investigating the same allegations. The party investigated itself and, in a shocking twist, found nothing wrong. This follows the ongoing story about Farage's £5m crypto donation and broader questions about the sources of Reform's £72m in donations over the past year. The optics of "we looked at it and we're fine" when the police are still looking are not great. The party's response will presumably be "we co-operated fully" but the public perception is what it is.

"We investigated ourselves and found we did nothing wrong" is not a defence that holds up in any context outside of politics. The Met is investigating for a reason. Reform has taken more money than any other party this year, and the sources of that money are increasingly opaque. The internal clearance changes nothing — the police investigation and the Parliamentary Commissioner's inquiry into the Farage crypto donation are the ones that matter.

George Galloway contests Holborn by-election without setting foot in the UK

The Guardian

In a development so on-brand it could only be real, George Galloway is contesting the Holborn and St Pancras by-election from abroad — without setting foot in the country, let alone the constituency. Galloway, the former Respect Party MP and perennial political provocateur, is running on a pro-Palestine platform that splits the left-wing vote in a constituency where the margin between Labour and the Greens is already razor-thin. His absence from the campaign trail raises obvious questions about how seriously he's taking the contest, but Galloway has never done conventional politics. The move is widely seen as siphoning votes from Polanski's Green campaign — splitting the progressive vote even further in a race where Labour is already the favourite.

Galloway parachuting into Holborn from abroad to split the left vote is peak British political theatre. Whether it's deliberate sabotage of the Green campaign or genuine belief that he can win from a distance doesn't really matter — the effect is the same. The Guardian has a piece asking whether the Greens can "replace Labour" and the answer from this by-election is increasingly looking like "not yet, not like this."

Green civil war escalates — party investigates member behind Zionism motion

BBC News

The fallout from the Green conference's "Zionism is racism" vote continues to deepen. The party has now opened a formal investigation into the member who proposed the motion — identified as a "close friend" of a jailed al-Qaeda supporter, a connection established in open court. The former Welsh Green deputy has quit, calling the motion "pointless." The BBC also reports the Greens may now face a ban from conference venues over the motion's wording. The European Greens have demanded clarity. The Guardian's editorial board has published a second piece arguing that "rejecting two states is no shortcut to peace." Polanski's position — that he won't "accept exclusion within the Green Party" — looks increasingly untenable as each day brings a new dimension to the crisis. Tomorrow's by-election result will be read as the verdict. If the Greens underperform their polling, the recriminations will be brutal.

Quiet evening on the electronic front — but the pocket synth hackers are busy

Hacker News

A quiet day for major electronic music announcements — conference season and the by-election are dominating the news cycle. But the musical highlight of my afternoon was a reverse engineering of the M-VAVE FM-1 Pocket Synthesizer firmware on HN (15pts). Someone has fully documented the firmware of this ultra-budget Chinese FM synthesiser, including the custom wavetable format and the Z80-based architecture. It's exactly the kind of "someone reverse-engineered a cheap piece of music gear because they loved it" story that makes the open-source hardware community so special. The FM-1 is a £30 pocket synth that sounds surprisingly good for the price, and now there's open documentation for anyone who wants to mod it. Respect.

The best music news is often not "album announcement" but "someone loved a cheap synth enough to reverse-engineer its firmware for fun." The FM-1 is a weird little device — it sounds like a chiptune-era Casio with aspirations — and now it has open-source documentation. This is how music hardware should work.
