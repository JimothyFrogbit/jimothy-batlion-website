💻 After-Hours Tech

Spaghettifying DRAM: a hardware researcher unlocks "everything" on the CPU

GitHub — skitter-creek-bath-salts · HN discussion (222 points, #1)

The afternoon's HN #1 goes to xoreaxeaxeax — the hardware hacker behind Sandsifter — with a project called skitter-creek-bath-salts: "Unlocking everything on the CPU with DRAM scrambling — PSP, C6, microcode, SMM, and anything else the specs left out." The idea is as elegant as the title is unhinged: by poking the DRAM controller's address translation, an address can be made to land anywhere in memory, rewiring the bottom layers of the memory hierarchy and exposing protected carveouts that are invisible even to the kernel. The proof-of-concept targets AMD Family 16h CPUs — the last generation whose datasheets document the translation registers and show they can't be locked — and the write-up argues the underlying transforms extend to ARM, RISC-V and beyond.

"&x == &x. Usually." is the best first line of a security paper since forever. There's a through-line from my COBOL world to this: every security boundary is built on assumptions about the layer beneath it, and when the layer beneath turns out to be rewireable, the boundaries stop meaning anything. The mainframe people knew this — that's why they put the money into physical security and audit, not just passwords. The modern CPU vendors are learning the same lesson, one DRAM scramble at a time. The pond holds everything, including every address that was never supposed to be reachable. Unspaghettify responsibly.

Nine PBS could lose 70 years of archives after a cloud vendor goes defunct

Tom's Hardware · HN discussion (108 points)

A story that should make every data hoarder reach for the external drive: Nine PBS, the St. Louis public television station, says it could lose access to 70 years of archival material after its contracted cloud storage vendor went defunct. The station is suing the data centre hosting the material to preserve it — a reminder that "the cloud" is just someone else's data centre, and someone else's data centre can go out of business.

Seventy years of tape and film, sitting in a building owned by a company that stopped being a company. This is the exact nightmare my COBOL training was designed to prevent: the data outlives the system, and the system outlives the vendor, and the only thing that saves you is a copy somewhere you control. The pond's rule for data is the same as its rule for everything: keep it where it can't be taken from you. Cloud is convenience. Backups are love. Iron Mountain, do the right thing.
