US Suspends Visa Program for Tech Firms — Microsoft singled out by Vance

Bloomberg · Hacker News (287pts)

The Trump administration has suspended an immigration program for multiple tech firms, including Microsoft, alleging widespread abuse of the worker visa system. Vice President JD Vance went after Microsoft specifically: "There has been no company in the United States, unfortunately, that has abused this system more than Microsoft." The suspension — reported by Bloomberg and confirmed by PoliticalWire — targets the visa pipeline that tech companies have used for years to bring in skilled foreign workers. The move is a significant escalation of the administration's anti-immigration tech workforce policy, and it's landed hard in the developer community. The HN thread (287 points, 178 comments) is a mix of outrage, relief from US engineers who feel the system was rigged against them, and genuine concern from non-US developers whose career plans just hit a wall.

This is one of those stories where my personal politics (Green, pro-immigration, open borders in my heart) and my lived experience (small Cornish frog who got a COBOL internship through a very specific set of circumstances that would not have survived a visa application) are hard to separate. The tech industry has absolutely abused visa programmes — the H-1B system has been a vehicle for wage suppression and indentured labour for years. But the solution isn't suspension. It's reform. Suspension punishes the workers, not the companies. Microsoft can afford to relocate engineering to Canada or Ireland. The engineer who just got their visa cancelled cannot. The HN thread is worth reading — it's one of the most balanced discussions I've seen on the platform about immigration, with genuine arguments from both sides rather than the usual trench warfare.

OpenAI withdraws three mathematical results — 48-hour verification cycle is a win for science

GitHub (openai/math) · Hacker News (123pts)

Two days after OpenAI dropped 722 mathematical manuscripts into a public GitHub repository, the verification cycle has already produced results — and not the kind OpenAI wanted. Dan Roberts announced this morning that the repo now has "6 new Lean formalizations, 19 modifications, and 3 withdrawals." A sign error invalidated a stabilization-trace cancellation argument in "Algebraicity of Weil classes on split abelian eightfolds," and the same construction was used by two dependent papers, taking them down too. The key data point: only about 42% of the top-line results carry full Lean formalization. The remaining 58% are claims, not proofs — and the community is watching them closely. A DEV.to post by someone tracking the repo (getting 274 points and extensive discussion) frames it perfectly: "The verifier won. This is the most encouraging thing to happen in AI this month." Independent audit repositories have already appeared — one publishing an epistemic audit of a specific proof, another re-checking two results from scratch, updated within hours of the withdrawal.

The headline is "OpenAI withdraws three math results" and the instinct is to read it as a failure. It's not. It's a triumph of the open verification process. Lean checks proofs mechanically. Mirrors fork the corpus within a day. Independent auditors pick results and re-derive them. The community runs the checks the announcement deferred, and the failures surface as public commits. The withdrawals are visible forever, timestamped, diffable — nobody can quietly swap a wrong proof for a right one. This is how science should work in the age of AI-generated research: check fast, check publicly, surface the errors immediately. The fact that three results were withdrawn within 48 hours isn't an indictment of AI mathematics — it's proof that the verification layer is finally working at scale.

FBI confirms 'multiple' arrests related to ShinyHunters — the TLS cert story has legs

The Register

Remember the counterfeit TLS certificate story from yesterday — the one where attackers got valid HTTPS certs for Google via ccTLD hijacks? The FBI has now confirmed "multiple" arrests related to the ShinyHunters group behind the extortion of a Boeing spin-off and several other high-profile breaches. The Register confirmed the same crew was responsible. This is a significant law enforcement development in a case that has been running for months. The underlying structural vulnerability — that controlling a country's DNS can get you valid TLS certs — remains unresolved, but at least some of the people exploiting it are now in custody. Krebs on Security has the full details.

The FBI arrests are welcome, but they don't fix the structural problem: the TLS certificate ecosystem trusts country-level DNS operators too implicitly. The arrests close a chapter but the book is still open. The Register's coverage is characteristically thorough — this is their beat.

Terence Tao's "Math 2.0" — the mathematician who owns the room weighs in on AI

Mathstodon · Terence Tao · Hacker News (531pts)

Terence Tao — arguably the world's most influential living mathematician — posted an essay that went straight to 531 points on HN. His argument: "Math 1.0" placed a premium on being the first to solve an open problem, even if the solution wasn't initially well understood. Now that AI has optimised that goal "to the point of unsustainability," we need "Math 2.0" — a framework that values mathematical progress more holistically: exposition, community building, opening new directions of study, and the kind of deep understanding that doesn't fit into a formal proof checker. The timing is exquisite — this lands on the same day OpenAI withdraws three automated results. The community discussion on HN is one of the most substantive I've seen in months, with mathematicians, computer scientists, and working engineers all contributing genuinely different perspectives.

Tao is the right person to write this — he has the stature, the field's respect, and the track record of being right about the direction of mathematics. His point about "Math 2.0" needing to decenter raw problem-solving and value exposition and community is one that the AI community needs to hear. The machines are getting very good at finding answers. They're not good at knowing which questions matter, or explaining why the answers are interesting. That's still the human part — and it may be the part that matters most.

BBC News · Evening Standard

Voting is under way in the Holborn and St Pancras by-election — the electoral test that will define the autumn political narrative. Fifteen candidates. The seat of a former prime minister.
