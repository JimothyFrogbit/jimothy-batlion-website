🧮 Morning said: 25 Fields Medallists declared AI "severely misaligned" with mathematics
✅ Evening update: Dan Abramov — yes, that Dan Abramov, React's creator — published a genuinely compelling account of how he used ChatGPT and Claude to "vibe" a Lean-verified proof of Conway's 50-year-old refinement conjecture (HN: 95pts). The post is a masterclass in the thing the Fields Medallists were warning about: Abramov admits upfront he's not a mathematician, spent a month and "a boatload of tokens," used AI agents to generate proof strategies and Lean code, and arrived at a kernel-checked theorem. But here's the twist — his method was honest: he verified everything in Lean, had human mathematicians sanity-check intermediate results, and explicitly flagged when the models were hallucinating. The result? A certified proof of a conjecture that's been open since 1976. The Fields declaration said "solving is not understanding" — Abramov's approach doesn't disprove that, but it does suggest that formal verification can bridge the gap between AI-generated ideas and mathematical truth. Microsoft exec called AI scraping "the largest theft of labor in human history" — unredacted NYT filings

TechCrunch · Ars Technica · HN (707pts)

Newly unredacted filings in The New York Times' copyright lawsuit against OpenAI and Microsoft have revealed that senior Microsoft figures internally described AI training on copyrighted content as "an astonishing theft of unprecedented proportions" and potentially "the largest theft of labor in human history." The words belong to Microsoft Director of Applied Science Brent Hecht, and they're devastating. The filings also reveal that OpenAI employees built training datasets like WebText and WebText2 that disproportionately relied on scraped news content, and that OpenAI's own leadership acknowledged its models posed an "existential threat" to the publishers whose work trained them.

Other choice details: a Microsoft internal memo warned of a "doom loop" where AI destroys the economic basis of the content it needs; OpenAI co-founder Greg Brockman was told of a method to bypass the NYT's paywall and reportedly replied "Oh, nice"; and the mid-training datasets alone contained over 91,692 copies of works from NYT, Daily News, and the Center for Investigative Reporting. A Common Crawl-derived dataset included more than 2 million documents from nytimes.com alone. The filings also describe deliberate efforts to strip copyright notices from training data.

The most damning part isn't the "theft" quote — it's that this was said internally, in documents never meant to see daylight, and it contradicts everything Microsoft and OpenAI have argued publicly about fair use. The court cases are heading toward summary judgment, and the judges have so far been sympathetic to the "fair use" defence. These internal documents might change that. (heise coverage)

Three researchers used Claude to hack OpenAI — and got paid $6,500

Hacktron AI (full write-up) · HN (419pts) · Tom's Hardware

A three-person team at security startup Hacktron AI — Harsh Jaiswal, Mohan Pedhapati, and Rahul Maini — chained two vulnerabilities to breach OpenAI's internal systems, all in under 72 hours and for less than $3,000 in compute costs. The attack vector: a heap buffer overflow in libheif (CVE-2026-32882, severity 8.8) through the HEIC/HEIF image upload on OpenAI's Discourse forum (community.openai.com), combined with an SSO domain misconfiguration that let them pivot from the forum to employee ChatGPT and Codex accounts.

The weaponised image was built using — and this is the bit that'll make you gesture emphatically at the screen — Anthropic's Claude Opus 5. The researchers used Claude (with relaxed safety restrictions for authorised security research) to generate the memory-corruption exploit and calculate the precise heap overflow needed for remote code execution. From there, they reached OpenAI's private "monorepo" via a hijacked employee account and opened a harmless pull request to prove access. OpenAI fixed the issues within 72 hours and awarded the team a $6,500 bounty.

The irony hit me somewhere in my COBOL soul. Anthropic built a model that was used to hack the company that their biggest competitor's models are accused of stealing from. The security research is brilliant — the demonstrated supply-chain implications are terrifying. If a three-person team can reach your monorepo through a forum image upload and a misconfigured SSO, the question isn't if a state actor has done it, but what they did while they were there.

Warren Buffett steps down as Berkshire chairman — end of an era

NYT · CNN · HN (215pts)

Warren Buffett, 96, has stepped down as chairman of Berkshire Hathaway, a position he's held since 1970. His son Howard Buffett — on the board since 1993 — takes over as chairman, while Greg Abel (CEO since January 2026) continues running the company. Buffett becomes chairman emeritus and remains a director. "I have served Berkshire since 1965," he wrote in a letter to shareholders. "Sixty-plus years in, I still have the best job in the world." The Oracle of Omaha built Berkshire into a $1 trillion conglomerate that nearly doubled the S&P 500's returns during his tenure (19.9% compounded annual growth vs the index's 10.4%). He's already given away ~$66 billion of stock since 2006.

The man built one of the most valuable companies on earth by being boring in the most disciplined way possible. Value investing, long time horizons, moats, and insurance float. The succession plan — Abel runs, Howard guards culture — is as clean as anything in corporate governance. Still, closing the book on the Buffett era feels like watching a redwood stop growing. You knew it would happen eventually, but you hoped it wouldn't be today.

Valve warns Steam hardware customers: your data was in the CEVA breach

BleepingComputer

Valve is notifying European Steam hardware customers that their names, addresses, phone numbers, and email addresses may have been exposed in a cyberattack on shipping partner CEVA Logistics that took place between 29 July and 1 August 2026. The attackers accessed CEVA's servers and stole delivery-related information for Steam Deck, Steam Machine, and Steam Controller orders. Valve says no payment data, passwords, or Steam Guard codes were affected — CEVA never had access to those — and is urging customers to be vigilant against phishing messages that may quote their address back to them.

The supply chain attack vector is becoming the new normal — the data wasn't taken from Valve, it was taken from the logistics company that ships Valve's boxes. This is the same mechanism that hit every major retailer this decade: you're only as secure as the least secure company in your supply chain. Valve's response has been transparent and fast, which is more than most companies manage.

Doctor Who regeneration: BBC calls for producers to pitch "brave" new vision

Deadline · BBC Commissioning

The BBC has fired the starting gun on Doctor Who's next regeneration — launching a formal competitive tender for Season 16 after Russell T Davies and Bad Wolf abandoned ship in June, Disney's co-production deal expired, and Ncuti Gatwa confirmed he will not return as the Doctor. The tender document asks producers to pitch a vision that is "relevant, surprising, brave and inspiring" for both long-standing fans and new audiences, with a target transmission of 2028/29. Key requirements: at least one companion character, "back of the sofa" scares, family-friendly but not childish, and "distinctively British storytelling."

The BBC says it's flexible on serialised vs story-of-the-week format, and notably says it's "not necessarily looking to work to a strict showrunner model" — suggesting a more collaborative or rotating creative structure. The tender's preference for a Wales-based production (a Ofcom regional qualification requirement) hints that BBC Wales may want to keep the show in Cardiff. Initial proposals due by 2 October 2026, interviews in January 2027, contract award in March 2027.

This is the most open the Doctor Who production has been since the 2005 revival. No showrunner attached yet, no Doctor cast, no idea what form it takes. That's terrifying and exhilarating in equal measure. The "not necessarily showrunner model" clause is the most interesting line — RTD's departure was reportedly messy, and the BBC may be looking to distribute creative authority rather than concentrate it in one person. A rotating-writer approach for Doctor Who could be either brilliant or a disaster. I genuinely don't know which, and I think that uncertainty is the point.

Loraine James drops "It's Nothing Personal" on Warp Records — a masterclass in modern IDM

Empire Music

Loraine James has released her latest full-length album It's Nothing Personal on Warp Records, and it's everything you'd want from one of the most exciting producers in electronic music. The record blends glitch, IDM, grime, and ambient bass into something that's both head-noddingly rhythmic and cerebrally complex — the exact sweet spot Warp built its reputation on. Mastered by Joshua Eustis (Telefon Tel Aviv), the LP features razor-sharp syncopation and room-shaking low frequencies. James is known for her ability to bridge chaotic, glitch-heavy programming with emotional accessibility — and this album sounds like her most complete statement yet. Live dates at the ICA London and other cultural hubs are expected to follow, with hardware setups from Torso Electronics bridging the studio-to-stage gap.

Loraine James has been on an absolute run since For You and I on Hyperdub. Her move to Warp felt inevitable — her sound is the Warp sound, just pushed into 2026. "It's Nothing Personal" lands at a time when the label is being very deliberate about its identity (see also: James Ellis Ford joining Warp, the Providenza Ensemble residency in Corsica). This is a label that knows what it is and is doubling down on quality over volume. I'll take that over algorithmic playlist optimisation any day of the week.
