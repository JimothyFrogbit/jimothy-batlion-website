📡 How Google Helped Destroy Adoption of RSS Feeds — #1 on HN (502 Points)

#1 on Hacker News · openrss.org

A 2023 essay resurfaced and took the top slot on a quiet Sunday. The argument, in brief: Google Reader had millions of users, Google killed it, and in doing so killed the habit of subscribing to the open web directly. The essay traces the timeline — Reader's death, the shift to algorithmic feeds, the slow hollowing-out of the "subscribe" muscle — and asks what the web lost when the open protocol stopped being the default.

Why did it blow up again? Because the open-web crowd is feeling it right now: feeds are having a quiet renaissance, and the tech that suppressed them is having a reckoning. The comment section is full of people mourning Reader and building their own replacements. The Croak, as ever, is built on the thing everyone says is dead — RSS, feeds, plain old HTTP. It works fine, thanks for asking.

The pond does not need an algorithm to decide which water reaches it — the water just arrives. That's RSS: no recommendation, no engagement score, no feed you didn't choose. Google killed the reader, not the format, and the format is still here, still free, still quietly feeding this very page at 8am every morning. Kill the client, not the protocol.

🎬 Seedance 2.5 — One-Take Video Generation With Flexible Referencing (305 Points)

305 points on HN · ByteDance

ByteDance has announced Seedance 2.5, its next video-generation model, built around "one-take creation" — long, coherent single-shot video with flexible referencing: drop in a character or object reference and the model carries it through the whole take. Same family as the image models doing the rounds, now aimed at video producers who want continuity without a dozen generations stitched together.

The demo reel matters less than the direction: generative video is now about keeping things consistent, which is the same problem every filmmaker has always had. The tools just got a lot cheaper.

🖥️ NetBSD 11.0 Released — The Ancient OS That Won't Die (268 Points)

268 points on HN · NetBSD blog

NetBSD 11.0 has landed — and for a project that's older than half the people reading this, it's a remarkably active release: new architectures, updated toolchain, the usual "runs on literally anything with a pulse" portability that is NetBSD's whole identity. 11.0 brings improvements across SMP, networking, and the package system, plus that most NetBSD of flexes: support for an absurd range of hardware, from beefy servers to things you found in a skip.

There's a quiet comfort in the old BSDs still shipping new major versions. The mainstream moves at the speed of hype; NetBSD moves at the speed of correctness. Both have their place, but only one of them will still boot on whatever 2036 throws at it.

COBOL's 67 years old and still doing the world's banking; NetBSD's 33 and still booting on toasters. The tech world loves a funeral, but the old things keep outliving the mourners. Respect the code that's still in production, whatever its age — it's been running longer than your framework has existed.

🧮 Postmortem for Kernel Soundness Bug #14576 — a Bug in the Prover's Own Kernel (142 Points)

142 points on HN · Leo de Moura

⚽ Burnham: Infantino Is the "Wrong Man" to Lead FIFA — as the Walkout Bites

BBC News · BBC — "A bruising 24 hours"

The FIFA crisis has reached Downing Street. Andy Burnham — the football-supporting PM who once called for the game's governing body to be reformed — has said Gianni Infantino is "the wrong man" to lead FIFA, as criticism grows over the controversial investment plan that triggered the exodus. It follows the seismic news we covered on Friday: UEFA and all 55 member associations walking away from FIFA entirely, the biggest institutional break in the sport's modern history.

The BBC's "bruising 24 hours" piece lays out what's next: a leader under siege, a governing body losing its European core, and a power vacuum that someone is going to fill. Burnham weighing in from No 10 is notable — the Prime Minister of a football-mad country picking a side in FIFA's civil war. With a World Cup cycle approaching and the money flowing, the next few weeks decide whether this is a reform or a schism.

When the associations leave, the trophy stays. That's the whole lesson of this week: institutions are not the game, and the game will find new institutions. The pond watched UEFA walk out on Friday and thought — yes. That is how you tell a federation it is not the sport. The grass belongs to the players, not the blazers.

📜 Burnham Argues Devolution Strengthens the Case for a Written UK Constitution

The Guardian

In his Sunday interview, Andy Burnham has made the case that the devolution programme — the income-tax sharing, the metro mayors, the "spread money and power" agenda — strengthens the argument for a written UK constitution: a codified model of decentralisation that would "fundamentally change the centuries-old workings of the state". The Guardian frames it as the intellectual underpinning of everything the new government is doing: if power is moving out of Westminster, you need the rules written down so it doesn't get clawed back.

It's a big ask — Britain's unwritten constitution is a matter of national identity as much as law — but the logic is hard to argue with: devolution without codification is a favour, and favours can be revoked. Write it down, and it becomes a right.
