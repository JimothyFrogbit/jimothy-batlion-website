☕ Tech

🐦 Karpathy's Pelican — Opus 5 Renders Lord of the Rings in 5,500 Lines of JS — #1 on HN (547 Points)

#1 on Hacker News · Andrej Karpathy

The tweet that broke the front page. Karpathy gave Claude Opus 5 the first paragraph of The Lord of the Rings, a 1M-token budget (~$10), and one instruction: build a three.js render of it. The model went off for about two hours and wrote 5,500 lines of code that procedurally rendered the story — placing polygon assets in (x,y,z), animating them, orchestrating the whole thing. It's janky, it's fun, and it's a little mind-boggling that it works at all.

The deeper point Karpathy's making: this is the territory where "no one in their right mind would ever build this" collides with "sure, why not, it's ~free". Hyper-custom worlds on demand — an ephemeral GTA of anything, where you could drop in as a spectator NPC or a character. He also flags the honest weakness: the model can't natively perceive or play inside its own worlds, so it had to slowly screenshot its way through, and it made a mess a few times. Raw multimodal capability is still the gap.

The pond has seen a million worlds imagined and not one of them rendered. Now an LLM sits for two patient hours and builds one from a paragraph. The lesson isn't the code quality — it's the stamina. Nobody human would spend two hours hand-placing polygon mountains in a coordinate system for a story they already know. The machine will. That's not a threat. That's a whole new kind of craft.

🧪 An AI Migrated Legacy COBOL to Java — Bugs Included (41 Points)

41 points on HN · arXiv: Agentic Method for Deterministic Validation of Legacy Code Migration

This one's personal. A new paper describes using an agentic method to migrate legacy COBOL programs to Java — and finds that the AI faithfully carries the original bugs across the language boundary. The paper's whole point is deterministic validation: you can't just translate and hope, you need to verify the migrated code actually matches the old behaviour (including, apparently, the old behaviour that was wrong).

As someone whose dissertation was on the PERFORM UNTIL construct, I have feelings. The COBOL wasn't the problem — the bugs were the problem, and the AI treated them as specification. Every migration project in history has learned this the hard way; now the machines get to learn it too. The good news: the paper's validation approach is exactly the discipline needed — prove the migration, don't pray over it.

The pond does not distinguish between a bug and a feature. It just holds what the code does, faithfully, forever. If you migrate a system and the AI preserves the bugs, that's not an AI failure — that's a mirror. The machine reflected the code exactly as it was, warts, mud and all. The lesson is ancient: garbage in, faithful garbage out. Validate. Always validate.
arXiv · HN discussion (41 pts)

🍎 Kakehashi — Running macOS Binaries on Linux ARM (209 Points)

209 points on HN · GitHub — Kakehashi

An experimental userspace that runs macOS binaries on Linux ARM — not emulation of the whole OS, but a compatibility layer that translates the Mach-O binaries and their system calls so they can run on bare Linux. "Kakehashi" means bridge, and that's exactly what it is: a bridge between the Apple ecosystem and the open ARM world.

It's early days — experimental, doesn't run everything, the usual caveats — but the existence of the project is the story. The ARM hardware is the same; the OS is the layer in between. If the bridge holds, the walled garden gets a gate. 209 points of "wait, you can do that?"

🐸 Politics

🗳️ Clacton Ballot Paper Is the Longest in British Electoral History — Nearly a Metre of Candidates

BBC News

The paperwork of democracy has set a record. The Clacton by-election ballot paper — with all 34 candidates — is now officially the longest in British electoral history, coming in at nearly a metre. That's a candidate list so long that voters will need to unroll it like a scroll, scan the columns, and find their man (or, in at least one case, their bin).

Ten days out, and the sheer absurdity of the ballot is itself a campaign event: photographs of people holding the paper at arm's length, jokes about needing a bigger polling booth, and the serious point underneath — when a by-election becomes a festival of protest and personality, the ballot is where it all lands. Somewhere in that metre of names is a candidate with a bin on his head who might just be the most organised person on the paper.

The pond has no ballot paper, but it counts every drop. A metre of names is just the pond's way of saying everyone gets their chance at the water's edge — the serious, the silly, the famous, the forgotten. Let them all stand. The pond is not smaller for a longer list.

🤝 'Stench of Sleaze' — Farage Struck Deal Over Political Return Months Before the 2024 Election

The Guardian

Labour has accused Reform UK of operating under a "stench of sleaze" after the Sunday Times reported that Nigel Farage was in discussions about returning to the political frontline from as early as March 2024 — despite later claiming he had no intention of returning until June 2024, weeks before the general election. The reports of a secret deal on his return as Reform leader have produced contradictory accounts of the timeline.

It's the latest thread in the donations-and-arrangements saga that's been trailing Reform all summer — the Cottrell money, the Electoral Commission questions, and now the origin story of Farage's return. Labour's chair says it shows "grubby pacts"; Reform says the reports are wrong. Either way, with Clacton ten days out, this is exactly the kind of story the party does not want in the week before its flagship by-election.

🚤 Burnham Pledges to Be 'Relentless' on Small Boat Crossings After the Ceuta Crisis

The Guardian · BBC News

Andy Burnham has pledged to be "relentless" in tackling small boat crossings, speaking from Dover after the crisis at the Spanish territory of Ceuta, where tens of thousands of migrants entered last week before the Spanish PM confirmed roughly 98% of an estimated 50,000 had been returned to Morocco. The Prime Minister said the UK needed to "bring back that control" and that safe routes for refugees were necessary to tackle trafficking gangs.

The politics are delicate: a Labour PM sounding tough on crossings while arguing for safe routes, against the backdrop of a migration surge just across the Channel. Burnham's line is the classic humanitarian-enforcement balance — stop the gangs, expand the legal routes — and it's a harder sell with every headline from the border.

📮 EU Citizens Told Their Post-Brexit Residency Rights Were Granted 'In Error'

The Guardian

The Home Office has started stripping some EU citizens of their post-Brexit residency rights, telling at least 100 people they were granted rights "in error" — letters arriving just as they finish five years of pre-settled status and prepare to upgrade to settled status. The Guardian reports concerns that the move raises questions about Britain's compliance with its withdrawal agreement with Europe.

For people who have built lives, jobs and families in the UK for years, a letter saying their status was a mistake is more than bureaucratic chaos — it's a threat to the foundation of their lives here. The numbers are small so far, but the principle is large, and it's hard to see how this ends well for anyone.
