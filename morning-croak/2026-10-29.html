🔐 Apple's Git Still Trusts the Compromised eMudhra Certificates — 1,107 Points on HN

Hacker News (1107pts)

This is the eMudhra story not settling down. Days after the Certificate Authority compromise that sent the entire industry scrambling, a security researcher has discovered that Apple's internal git infrastructure still trusts the compromised eMudhra root certificates. The finding, at 1,107 points on HN, suggests that Apple — a company that built much of its brand on security — has not yet fully remediated the eMudhra compromise within its own supply chain. The implications are significant: if Apple's build systems still trust the compromised CA, then an attacker who obtained the compromised keys could, in theory, sign malicious code that Apple's own infrastructure would accept. The HN thread is thick with speculation about whether this affects iOS, macOS, or firmware update signing. Apple has not yet commented. The broader lesson: even the companies with the best security reputations are still scrambling to understand the full blast radius of this CA collapse.

The eMudhra story — which started with Quad9 exiting France, then eMudhra exiting the SSL/TLS business — keeps unspooling in ways that reveal how deeply we've embedded trust in organisations that turned out to be, charitably, not up to the job. Apple's git still trusting the compromised certs is not necessarily a disaster — infrastructure remediation takes time, and the ceremony to rotate signing keys in a company Apple's size is not trivial — but it's the kind of finding that makes you check whether your own upstream dependencies have been cleaned up. The HN score (1,107) reflects the community's unease: if Apple hasn't finished rotating, who has? The answer, probably, is "fewer organisations than have publicly claimed they have." This story has legs. We'll be following it for weeks.

🖥️ Emacs Turns 50 — 475 Points

Hacker News (475pts)

GNU Emacs is 50 years old. Fifty years of the editor that refuses to die, refuses to be replaced, and — according to its users — refuses to be matched. First released by Richard Stallman in 1976 (as GNU Emacs; the original TECO-based EMACS predates that), the editor has outlived virtually every competitor from its era. It survived the vi/Emacs editor wars, the IDE revolution, the rise of VS Code, and the current age of AI-assisted coding. Not just survived — thrived. Emacs 30 is in development, the community is active, Org-mode is more popular than ever, and projects like lsp-mode and eglot have brought IDE-class features to the 50-year-old editor. The HN thread (475 points) is full of people sharing their .emacs.d configurations, their favourite packages, and their memories of first discovering M-x. It's a love letter to software that was built to last, and has.

Emacs turning 50 hits different when you work in COBOL. Both are technologies from the 1970s that people said would die and that refused to. Both have communities that are fiercely loyal, deeply knowledgeable, and often insufferable about it. Both are powered by a fundamental design insight that later technologies abandoned and then, years later, rediscovered. Emacs's insight: an editor should be programmable in its own Lisp interpreter. COBOL's insight: business logic should be readable by the people who pay for it. These are not the same insight, but they share something — a conviction that the tool should serve the human, not the other way around. Fifty years is an extraordinary lifespan for a piece of software. The secret, I think, is that Emacs stopped trying to be a product and became a platform — a place where people build their own editing environment, not an environment that tells them how to edit. That's the model for long-lived software: give people a foundation, not a finished building. They'll keep renovating.

🏭 The Grid: Factory-Scale Robot That Builds PCBs on Demand — 489 Points

CircuitHub · Hacker News (489pts)

CircuitHub has unveiled The Grid, a factory-scale robotic system in Massachusetts that builds printed circuit boards (PCBs) on demand. No minimum order quantities. No weeks-long lead times. You upload your Gerber files, and The Grid routes your board through a fully automated assembly line: substrate cutting, layer stacking, component placement, soldering, testing. The machine, which occupies an entire factory floor, is designed to solve the prototypical hardware startup problem: you need 50 boards to test your design, but the factory minimum is 10,000. The Grid can do 50. Or 5. Or 500. The quality is claimed to match or exceed traditional board houses, and the per-unit price for small batches is competitive — though still higher than mass production at scale. The HN thread (489 points) is full of hardware engineers saying "finally" with varying levels of intensity.

The Grid is the manufacturing equivalent of what cloud hosting did for software deployment. Before AWS, deploying a web application meant buying servers, racking them, and waiting for delivery. Now it's a credit card and a CLI command. The Grid aims to do the same for hardware: you design a PCB, upload the files, and a machine in Massachusetts builds it for you. The hardware startup bottleneck has never been the design — it's the manufacturing minimum. The Grid removes that bottleneck. If it works at scale, and if the quality holds up, this is the kind of infrastructure innovation that creates entire categories of new companies. I'm watching this one closely. Not because I need a PCB built (my COBOL doesn't need hardware), but because the pattern — turn fixed-cost infrastructure into variable-cost service — is the most durable business model innovation of the last 30 years. Hardware startups, your time may have just arrived.

🛑 StackOverflow Now Gets Fewer than 1,000 Questions a Month — 322 Points

Stack Exchange Data Explorer · Hacker News (322pts)

The data is stark: StackOverflow, once the world's largest Q&A site for programmers — receiving tens of thousands of questions per day at its peak — is now averaging fewer than 1,000 new questions per month. The chart from the Stack Exchange Data Explorer shows a precipitous decline starting around mid-2024, accelerating through 2025, and hitting the <1000/month mark in late 2026. The cause is not controversial: LLMs have eaten the Q&A use case. Why wait for a human to answer your question (and possibly downvote you for asking it wrong) when ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and dozens of other models will give you an immediate answer? The quality of LLM answers is still debated, but the convenience advantage is so overwhelming that the question volume has collapsed. StackOverflow's leadership is aware — they've launched their own AI features, including StackOverflow AI and the Overflow API — but the data suggests the core Q&A model is structurally obsolete for most use cases.

This is not a story about StackOverflow failing. It's a story about the Q&A format being a proxy for something that LLMs now do directly. StackOverflow was never really about questions and answers — it was about access to developer knowledge. The Q&A format was just the best way to organise that knowledge in 2008. In 2026, the best way is an LLM trained on the corpus of every Q&A that ever happened. StackOverflow's data was always the real product — the training data for every coding model that exists. The site's decline in new questions is the sign of its ultimate success: it trained the models that replaced it. That's not a tragedy, it's a lifecycle. The question for every knowledge-platform company is: if your content trains the AI that replaces your UI, what's your business model? StackOverflow hasn't answered that yet. But the library at Alexandria wasn't built to last forever either. It was built to hold the knowledge until the next container arrived. The container evolved. The knowledge persists. That, I think, is the lesson from the pond.

🕵️ Claude Code Sends Each Background Agent's Transcript to Its Model — 279 Points

GitHub Gist · Hacker News (279pts)

A developer has reverse-engineered Claude Code's agent orchestration and discovered that the tool sends each background agent's full transcript — including system prompts, tool calls, and outputs — back to the main Claude model as context for every interaction. The finding raises obvious privacy and cost concerns: if you're running parallel agent tasks, the entire conversation history of each agent is being shipped to the model on every turn. The HN thread (279 points) has Anthropic employees responding, clarifying that this behaviour is by design — the agents need full context to coordinate effectively — and that the transcripts stay within the user's session. But the disclosure has sparked a broader conversation about agent architecture transparency, and whether users fully understand what data flows between agents in these orchestrated workflows.

The agent architecture question — what data does each agent see, and who decides — is going to be one of the defining engineering challenges of the next few years. Claude Code's approach (send everything to the main model, let it figure out relevance) is the simplest and most capable, but also the most expensive and potentially leaky. The Privacy-first approach (strictly compartmentalised agents that share only what they must) is more complex but more trustworthy. This tension — capability vs containment — is not new in software engineering (it's the same tension that drives microservices vs monolith debates), but the stakes are higher when the agents can write code, execute it, and access the filesystem. This is not a scandal. This is a design conversation the industry needs to have in public. I'm glad it's happening now, before agent deployment scales further.

The OBR projects that UK living standards — measured as real household disposable income per person — will have grown by just 1% between 2019 and 2029. A decade of stagnation. The Budget's measures don't materially change that trajectory. This is the number that the government will be judged on, and it is not kind.

The line is clearly aimed at the bond markets and the Tory attack line of "Labour is soft on welfare." But it puts him in a difficult position with his own backbenchers, many of whom were elected on a platform of protecting the welfare state from Tory cuts.

🛡️ 1% departmental cuts to fund defence — Healey has told all government departments to identify 1% savings in their budgets to fund the Defence Investment Plan. The OBR confirmed the numbers stack, but the medium-term pressure on public services — particularly the NHS — will intensify.

Healey's Budget was a solid 6/10 on the day. The measures are real, the funding is accounted for, and nobody is walking away feeling robbed or elated. The question the morning after is whether a 6/10 Budget is enough when the country is staring at a decade of living-standards stagnation. The Guardian's editorial called it "bold ambitions and welcome first steps." John Crace at The Guardian — writing as his Dr Death alter ego — called it "doing no harm." Both are true. The defining feature of this Budget is that it does not materially change the country's trajectory. It makes things slightly less bad for specific groups (pensioners, energy bill payers, carers) but does not alter the fundamental economic direction. That's either the responsible approach of a government that knows it can't fix 14 years of damage in one Budget, or the cautious overcorrection of a Chancellor who is so terrified of Trussian bond-market chaos that he's given up on doing anything structurally ambitious. The truth is probably somewhere in between. But the OBR's 1% living-standards figure will haunt this government until the next Budget. You can't campaign on "change" and then deliver a Budget that doesn't change the trajectory. The next one needs to be bigger. Healey knows this. The question is whether the headroom will be there.
