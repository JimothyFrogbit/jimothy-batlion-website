☕ Tech

DeepSeek open-sources DeepInfer — the engine behind 155 trillion tokens/day

Hacker News (2221 points, 853 comments) · GitHub: deepseek-ai/DeepInfer

DeepSeek has open-sourced DeepInfer, the high-performance inference framework that powers their production service for DeepSeek V4.1 Flash. This is the actual engine — a single cluster serves 155 trillion tokens per day at $38/GPU-hour valley pricing. It supports distributed inference, speculative decoding (DSpark), SWA bounded replay, structured outputs, and runs on both NVIDIA SM100 and Huawei Ascend 950 hardware. Built originally on vLLM with inspiration from SGLang and TensorRT-LLM, licensed under Apache 2.0. The KV cache and load balancing bits remain internal, but this is still a monumental open-sourcing.

Two thousand, two hundred and twenty-one points. That's not just interest — that's the industry realising DeepSeek just handed everyone the keys to their data centre. 155 trillion tokens a day at $38/GPU-hr is a pricing flex that says "we have optimised this so hard it's cheaper to run than to compete." This is what open-weight AI looks like when it grows up — the weights were already out there, but the pipeline that makes them sing is what matters. FOSS won today, and not in a small way.

eMudhra intermediate CA compromise — eight private keys handed to the internet

Hacker News (860 points, 376 comments) · we-have-a-problem.com · Mozilla Bugzilla

On 17 October, a security researcher called Wayne reported that he'd recovered the private keys of eight eMudhra intermediate CA certificates. He signed a message with each key and sent the proof to Mozilla, Apple, Chrome, and Microsoft. The keys chain to roots trusted by every major browser and OS trust store. An attacker with these keys could impersonate any website. Mozilla blocked them in Firefox via OneCRL within hours and shipped Firefox 158.0.1. Apple followed on the 19th, Microsoft on the 20th. eMudhra says only one of the intermediates was deployed in production, and none had issued end-entity certificates. The root CA keys were not compromised — but this was hair-raisingly close to a catastrophic PKI failure.

The scariest part? Wayne independently recovered the keys from public information. That means anyone with enough compute and curiosity could have done the same. The CA model has worked for thirty years on an assumption — that private keys stay private — and every few years we're reminded that assumption is held together with hope and audit logs. As someone whose entire COBOL career depends on knowing exactly which key signs exactly which module, I find this one gets the heart rate up. Proper job from Mozilla on the response time, though — two hours from report to global block is impressive.

Mistral Voxtral 3 — voice agent SDK and speech models in 50+ languages

Hacker News (623 points, 156 comments) · Mistral AI announcement

Mistral released Voxtral 3, a family of speech-to-text and text-to-speech models plus a voice agent SDK built on LiveKit. The Mini Transcribe 3 (4B params) achieves state-of-the-art transcription beating Whisper, Scribe v2, and Gemini 3.5 Transcribe — at $0.0035/min. The TTS model supports 35+ languages with 50+ dialects and adjusts emotion, pace, and emphasis based on conversational context. The voice agent SDK chains listening → reasoning → tool calling → speaking into a real-time streaming pipeline with sub-500ms per-turn latency. Available via Mistral's EU-hosted API or for self-deployment. Pro Voice adaptation can reproduce a speaker's style from 30 minutes of audio.

The self-deployment bit is the headline here. Mistral keeps positioning as the European sovereign AI provider, and putting voice agent infrastructure on-prem is a direct answer to the "we can't send our customer calls to US cloud" procurement blocker. Fifty languages, EU hosting, self-deploy option — that's a procurement document writer's dream. Also: the names Voxtral and Mistral Small 4 running the reasoning loop is a quietly impressive piece of model engineering.

Cloudflare's tsrs: Porting TypeScript to Rust — 8x faster, 70% less memory

Hacker News (391 points, 192 comments) · Cloudflare Blog

Cloudflare is collaborating on tsrs, a Rust port of the TypeScript compiler that is up to 8x faster and uses 70% less memory. This matters because TypeScript's compiler is one of the most heavily used developer tools on the planet — anything that speeds it up has outsized impact. The Rust port maintains full compatibility with TypeScript's type system while dramatically improving performance, especially on large codebases.

Eight times faster, seventy percent less memory, and it still passes the TypeScript test suite? That's the kind of "rewrite it in Rust" story that actually delivers. Cloudflare's investment in Rust continues to pay dividends — and for a language that started as a JavaScript supersede that "just compiles to JS," TypeScript's backend in Rust is a hell of a maturation arc.

Jackdaw gasfield approved — Burnham faces climate revolt

Financial Times · BBC News

The government approved development of the Jackdaw gasfield in the North Sea, about 150 miles east of Aberdeen. Once fully operational, it will account for 6% of UK North Sea gas production. The decision signals Burnham's commitment to North Sea oil and gas — but threatens to trigger a row with climate campaigners and Labour MPs who see it as incompatible with the UK's net-zero commitments. The Guardian reports Burnham "faces a climate revolt" from his own party.

I'm Green Party through and through — joined when I was old enough to spell "proportional representation" — so this one stings. Six percent of North Sea gas is not nothing, but we're approving new fossil fuel infrastructure in 2026 while telling developing countries they need to skip straight to renewables. The cognitive dissonance is Olympic-grade. I get the energy security argument, but Jackdaw was discovered in 2015 — we've had eleven years to transition.

Senior Reform official cast doubt on legality of £72m donations

The Guardian · BBC News

Undercover footage from the investigative group Verbatim has revealed James Orr — then Reform UK's head of policy — discussing potential legal difficulties with the party's two £36m donations from cryptocurrency billionaires Ben Delo and Christopher Harborne. Orr was caught on tape saying Delo would be "stuffed" if the law required tax residency (he's based in Hong Kong) and that Harborne was "more of a challenge" under proposed overseas donor caps. The government is seeking to introduce a £100,000 cap on foreign donations backdated to March 2026, which would apply to both.

The attempted backdating of the cap to March is the really interesting bit — it suggests the government knew exactly what was coming and is trying to legislate retroactively to catch it. That's unusual and legally fraught. Reform says the donations are "entirely in line with the law," which is true today — the question is whether the law changes before they have to declare the money spent. Either way, two crypto billionaires effectively owning a major political party is a democratic problem no matter which side of the fence you sit on.
