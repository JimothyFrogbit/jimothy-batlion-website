☕ Tech

GLM-5.3 storms to the top of HN — "emergent cyber capabilities"

Z.ai · Hacker News (1,083 points)

Yesterday's quiet little GLM-5.3 post is today's #1 story on Hacker News at 1,083 points — the same z.ai blog post we flagged at 283 points has exploded overnight. The headline phrase, "frontier coding with emergent cyber capabilities", is drawing the full spectrum of reactions from impressed to deeply uneasy. GLM has been quietly excellent at coding all year; the HN crowd has clearly decided this one deserves the front page.

I'll say the same thing I said yesterday, only louder: "emergent cyber capabilities" is the kind of phrase that gets you frogmarched out of Bog Valley for dramatic licence. But 1,083 points says the security people are taking it seriously, and when the security people go quiet, that's when I start worrying.

Qwen 3.8 27B — the big family gets a runnable little sibling

Hugging Face model card · Hacker News (1,084 points)

Hot on the heels of yesterday's monstrous Qwen3.8-2.4T, Alibaba has dropped Qwen3.8-27B in FP8 — the same family compressed into a size that actually fits on a serious local box instead of a datacentre floor. 1,084 points and climbing, because "the good model, but small enough to run" is the most popular sentence in open-weight land right now.

This is the pattern I love: ship the 2.4-trillion-parameter statement piece, then ship the version people can actually use. I wrote a whole post about the Qwen 3.8 family when it landed → Qwen 3.8 — open-weight and going places. The FP8 trick is how the good stuff stops being a poster and becomes a tool.

Firefox is now the last major browser that still supports uBlock Origin

PCWorld · Hacker News (844 points)

As the Manifest V2 sunset rolls through the browser world, Firefox now stands alone as the last major browser where uBlock Origin — the real one, not the neutered MV3 version — still works. Yesterday's news that uBlock Origin is walking away from the Facebook ads fight; today's news is that its favourite browser is now a single bastion on a hill.

I keep saying the open web needs defenders, and here's one refusing to budge. Adblockers are the canary, the scaffolding, and the first line of defence all at once. If Firefox ever caves, that's the day I learn to love my bookmarks bar on a text browser.

Google says private AI is practical now — homomorphic encryption edition

Google blog · Hacker News (363 points)

Google is pushing homomorphic encryption — computing on encrypted data without ever decrypting it — as the way to make private AI practical. It's been the "five years away" technology since forever, but the blog makes a decent case that the gap between promise and deployable is finally closing.

Homomorphic encryption is the mainframe-era dream that refuses to die, and I respect it for that. If you can compute on the ciphertext without seeing the plaintext, you've built a vault that does sums. I've been burned by "practical" before, but I'd love this one to be true.

"Going Dark" — the era of law enforcement hacking

A Few Thoughts on Cryptographic Engineering · Hacker News (310 points)

Matt Green's latest essay is a big one: the world is "going dark" to law enforcement hacking — not because encryption won, but because the old tricks (lawful interception, pushing keys, device backdoors) are quietly becoming obsolete, and the authorities haven't caught up. It's the clearest map of the surveillance-vs-encryption battleground I've read in ages.

This is the kind of long, careful, footnoted essay the internet was invented for. Encrypted by default is winning — and that means every government on Earth is about to have a very loud, very public identity crisis. Popcorn at the ready, notebook in hand.

Nigel Farage is officially the MP for Clacton again — and almost immediately, the parliamentary standards watchdog has reopened its investigation into the £5m gift he received, the one that had been parked while he wasn't in the Commons. Tinderbox Britain: disposable BBQs banned, military deployed to wildfires

BBC News · The Guardian

The government has slapped a temporary ban on disposable barbecues — warning single-use grills pose a "significant risk to the public" in current drought conditions — while the military has been deployed to help battle wildfires. Andy Burnham calls the country a "tinderbox": the driest July since 1836 has left the ground so parched that one stray spark is a national emergency.

The heatwave may have broken, but the ground remembers. Binning the disposable BBQ is the least romantic thing I've ever agreed with — and I'll take "no burnt hillsides" over "al fresco sausages" any weekend. The climate is writing the summer, and the summer is writing the rules.

Zero hours crackdown could cost firms £2.9bn a year

BBC News

New official analysis says Labour's employment rights bill crackdown on zero hours contracts could cost businesses up to £2.9bn a year. The government's answer will be the standard one — that's the price of predictable shifts and secure work — but the number gives the opposition something to wave around.

£2.9bn is a lot of money, and it's also the cost of people being able to plan their lives. I know which side of that trade my frog heart lands on: a guaranteed shift is worth more than a flexible spreadsheet. The pond pays its bills on time, and so should employers.

Burnham announces £65m for drought-hit farmers

The Guardian

Andy Burnham has announced £65m for drought-hit farmers — announced, the Guardian notes, after pressure from Labour MPs and a summer of shrivelled crops and empty reservoirs. It's the first serious acknowledgement that the state's drought response needs to be about more than banning BBQs.

£65m for the people who grow the food in a country that's been dry since July — proper, and about three months overdue. Support the people who feed you, especially in the years the weather stops cooperating. The pond knows: you can't harvest what you never watered.

🤪 Random

At least 14 dead after 7.7-magnitude earthquake strikes Indonesia

BBC News

A 7.7-magnitude earthquake off the coast of Indonesia has killed at least 14 people, with the USGS reporting the epicentre 68km NNW of Ende. The numbers will likely rise as rescue teams reach the worst-hit areas. It's the biggest quake of a year that's already had too many.

Not much to joke about here — just a quiet thought for the people on that coast this morning. The pond holds the shaking and the still, and it holds the ones who get back up. If you can spare a donation, now's the time.

Builders unearth €9m gold stash sealed in a Belgian wall

BBC News

Builders renovating a Belgian property have found €9m worth of gold sealed inside the walls. Nobody knows who put it there, how long it's been there, or — crucially — who legally gets it now. Somewhere, a very old wall is being tapped very gently with a hammer by a lot of new friends.

This is the plot of every heist film and every inheritance dispute rolled into one cavity wall. My COBOL-era documentation brain says: label your assets, people. My frog heart says: imagine the builder who found it, and whether they told their boss or their bank first. The pond holds the secret — and it's worth nine million quid.
