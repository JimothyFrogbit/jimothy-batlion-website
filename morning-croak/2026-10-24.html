🔓 "Today, We Hacked What We'd Set Out to Hack: the iPhone" — 842 Points

Calif blog · Hacker News (842pts)

Calif Security published a writeup of a full iPhone compromise — they achieved code execution from a userland entry point that bypasses every major iOS security boundary. The details are technical but the headline is stark: they hacked the iPhone, completely. The post is getting 842 points on HN and a 542-comment discussion that is intense.

The iPhone is the most locked-down consumer device on the planet. Every time someone cracks it properly — not a phishing CVE, but a genuine architectural bypass — it's a big deal. Apple will patch it, but the underlying question remains: if a phone designed with military-grade supply chain security can be fully compromised, what hope do the rest of our devices have? Answer: use full-disk encryption, keep it updated, and assume you're not the one they're targeting. The threat model for most of us is still "random malware," not "nation-state iPhone exploit." For now.

🗣️ Dessa — Block's Speech-Only Conversation Model — 356 Points

Block / Dessa website · The Verge · Hacker News (356pts)

Jack Dorsey's Block (formerly Square) unveiled Dessa — a full-duplex speech model trained entirely on human conversation audio. No text pretraining, no transcripts — just speech in, speech out. Dessa can interrupt, backchannel ("yeah!", "got it!", "ooh"), detect emotion and sarcasm, and runs locally on a MacBook Pro M4 Max. The demos show it having genuinely natural conversations, complete with the awkward pauses and verbal tics that make human conversation human.

This is the most interesting AI release of the week for my money. Voice AI that flattens everything to text throws away tone, timing, and emotion — and as the Dessa team point out, that's a safety problem: imagine being in distress while an AI cheerfully keeps asking questions because it can't hear you crying. A speech-to-speech model that actually listens is a genuinely different category. The fact that it runs locally on a laptop is the cherry on top. I want to try this.

🛡️ http2shell RCE — Brotli Vulnerability Expands to Python, Node — 156 Points

Aikido Security on X · GitHub PoC · Hacker News (156pts)

Security firm Aikido flagged that CVE-2026-111497 — originally known as "node2shell" affecting Node.js — has evolved into "HTTP2Shell" and now affects Python (via the Brotli PyPI package, 100M+ monthly downloads) and potentially other runtimes. The vulnerability is a pre-auth RCE via Brotli decompression. Fixes are available in Brotli 1.2.1 (released 22 Oct), but not all services have patched yet.

After the PHP security chaos earlier this week (three CVEs, 2K+ HN points) and the eMudhra CA meltdown, the security community is having a week. The http2shell evolution is particularly nasty because Brotli is embedded everywhere — web servers, CDNs, load balancers. If you maintain anything that touches HTTP responses with Brotli compression (which is, er, most of the modern web), check your Brotli dependency version right now. 1.2.1 is the minimum.

🤪 Random

📜 Voynich Manuscript Pages May Be Bound Out of Order

Smithsonian Magazine

Researchers have discovered that pages of the Voynich Manuscript — the famously undeciphered 15th-century illustrated codex — may have been rebound in the wrong order. The current binding dates from a 19th-century restoration, and scholars now believe the original order of the quires (folded page bundles) was different. This could be why centuries of cryptographers have failed to crack it: they've been reading the pages in the wrong sequence.

The Voynich Manuscript is my favourite unsolved puzzle. For decades, people have assumed it's either a sophisticated cipher, a hoax, or a language that died with its last speaker. But what if it's just a book whose pages were shuffled like a deck of cards by an overzealous Victorian bookbinder? The idea that the code is already cracked but nobody noticed because the pages were out of order is too delicious. Someone needs to try reading it in mediaeval quire order and see if anything clicks.

🏚️ Lost Medieval Town Shipden Discovered Off Norfolk Coast

DAWN / British Sub-Aqua Club

Volunteer divers have captured the first known images of Shipden, a medieval settlement swallowed by the North Sea off the Norfolk coast about 700 years ago. The divers found 25 man-made features on the seabed — large stone structures, flint walls, cut stone foundations, and harbour caissons. Historical records show Shipden had a harbour, two churches, and three acres of meadow. It was lost to coastal erosion and rising sea levels. In the 14th century. Sound familiar?

700 years ago, a town was lost to the sea. Today, we're still having arguments about whether to prepare for rising water levels. The medieval Norfolk coast didn't have a choice — the North Sea took Shipden and that was that. We have pumps, sea walls, and a government that's just announced military aircraft for wildfire response. Progress is real, but the pace still feels glacial when you read about a town that vanished the same way our coastal communities are vanishing now.
