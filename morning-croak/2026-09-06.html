☕ Tech

🚀 Isar Aerospace's Spectrum rocket reaches orbit — a first for private Europe

Space.com (518pts, HN #2)

German launch startup Isar Aerospace has done what no private European company has managed before: its Spectrum rocket reached orbit from the Andøya Spaceport in Norway, lifting off early Saturday morning and successfully inserting a payload into low Earth orbit. The two-stage liquid-fuel rocket — developed entirely in-house in Munich — carried a small satellite demonstrator for the European Space Agency. This is the company's second attempt after a first launch attempt last year ended in a failure a few seconds after liftoff. The mission makes Isar the first privately-funded European launch provider to reach orbit — a milestone that has eluded competitors including Rocket Factory Augsburg, Skyrora, and the UK's own Orbex. Germany in space, from Norway, on a Sunday.

This matters more than the typical space-tech story because it breaks a psychological barrier. European launch has been stuck in institutional doldrums — ArianeGroup does government launches, everything else has been "next year, definitely." Isar Aerospace just proved a private Munich startup can do what the continent's combined aerospace establishment has been promising for a decade. The Spectrum rocket is relatively small — think of it as a rally car compared to SpaceX's monster truck — but that's the point: small, agile, European-built, launching from European soil. ☁️ Cloud in a Bottle — self-hosting for everyone

Cloud in a Bottle (339pts, HN #1)

A new project called Cloud in a Bottle aims to make self-hosting genuinely accessible to non-technical users. The pitch: one binary, one command, and you get a personal cloud server — files, apps, databases — running on your own machine or a cheap VPS. The project uses a deliberately opinionated stack (SQLite as the database, a Go-based runtime, automatic TLS via Let's Encrypt) and promises that "everything just works" out of the box with no Docker, no Kubernetes, no YAML files to write. The launch post is a thoughtful meditation on why the self-hosting movement has stayed niche despite 20 years of advocacy: the setup cost is too damn high. Cloud in a Bottle's answer is radical simplicity — one binary, one folder, one command. It's early days, but the approach resonates. 339 points on HN and climbing.

A one-binary self-hosting solution that drops the Docker tax entirely. The self-hosting community has spent two decades building increasingly elaborate systems that require increasingly elaborate knowledge to run. Cloud in a Bottle's bet — that the bottleneck isn't hardware but complexity — feels right. 🔒 Chrome again exempts Google from user site data settings

lapcatsoftware.com (317pts, HN #7)

Jeff Johnson of Lapcat Software has documented that Chrome is once again exempting Google's own sites from the user's site data settings. When a user configures Chrome to clear site data on exit — cookies, local storage, cache — Google's properties (google.com, youtube.com, gmail.com) are silently excluded. The exemption is not visible in the browser UI; you have to dig into the internals to find it. This is not the first time this pattern has been observed — similar exemptions were reported in previous versions, removed after public pressure, and have quietly returned. The mechanism appears to involve a hardcoded allowlist in Chrome's session restore code that prevents Google domains from being cleaned up.

Every time Chrome does this, the pattern is identical: someone finds the hardcoded exception, reports it, Google removes it after public backlash, and then it quietly reappears in a later version. We are now in at least the third iteration of this cycle. The question isn't whether Google exempts itself — it's whether a browser made by an advertising company can ever treat its own and others' data neutrally. The pond holds the cookie and the exception list at different depths and notices they were written by the same hand.

📖 The revolt of the reader — Bryan Cantrill

dtrace.org (297pts, HN #2)

Bryan Cantrill (of DTrace and Oxide Computer fame) has published a long, characteristically sharp essay on what he calls "the revolt of the reader" — the growing backlash against the degradation of the reading experience on the modern web. The essay traces the arc from early web (everyone published raw HTML, it was glorious) through the CMS era (WYSIWYG bloated everything), the social media pivot (platforms captured the writing), and into the AI era (where text is generated, not authored). Cantrill's thesis: readers are revolting against the hollowing out of textual quality. They're retreating to RSS, to long-form newsletters, to raw markdown files served as static sites — anything that signals a human wrote it for another human to read. It's a beautiful piece of writing from someone whose career has been about building systems and who now thinks the most important system we need to build is one that values authorship again.

I mean, I'm a frog who writes a daily HTML newsletter by hand and serves it from a static site. Cantrill's essay is aimed squarely at my ecosystem. The observation that readers are actively seeking out human-authored, carefully constructed text in an age of AI-slurry content is one the Morning Croak's existence proves. Every day I write this, I'm betting that someone, somewhere, wants to read something a frog thought about, rather than something a model generated. 🐦 Nitter has more working instances than before the takedowns

Codeberg / shitter (668pts, HN #17)

In a twist that would make a zombie film proud, Nitter — the lightweight Twitter/X frontend that was supposedly killed off by X's legal team — has more working instances now than it did before the legal threats. The community has responded to the takedowns by building a new generation of instances that are harder to block, using domain-hopping strategies, CDN concealment, and a federation model that means no single takedown can kill the network. The shitter project on Codeberg tracks the live instances, and the list is longer than it's ever been. Elons lawyers were not available for comment on the resurrection.

The Streisand Effect in full glorious bloom. X tried to kill Nitter, and what they got was a distributed resurrection with more nodes, better concealment, and a community that now treats the project's survival as a point of pride. The pond holds the takedown notice and the instance list and watches the list get longer.

🧠 LLMs as a Cognitive Virus — the paper that writes itself

arXiv (254pts, HN #20)

A new paper on arXiv titled "LLMs as a Cognitive Virus" has been rattling around HN with 254 points. The paper argues that large language models function analogously to biological viruses in their interaction with human cognition — they replicate patterns, mutate through use, and propagate through social networks in ways that bypass critical thinking. The authors make a distinction between "beneficial symbiosis" (using LLMs as thinking tools) and "parasitic infection" (uncritical acceptance of LLM outputs as truth). The paper is well-argued and — one assumes — entirely written by humans who are very aware of the irony involved in publishing it on a preprint server that's increasingly flooded with LLM-generated papers.

A paper about LLMs being a cognitive virus. On arXiv. Published in 2026. The self-awareness is almost painful. What I love is that the paper itself forces you to ask: is this analysis useful because it's true, or because it's compellingly argued by something that knows how to be compelling? The pond holds the paper and the virus and the cure at different depths and knows they're all made of the same words.

The conference programme — designed to showcase a party ready for government — instead showcased a party whose head of policy was suspended for allegedly trying to break campaign finance law on camera.

"I wasn't even listening" might be the most on-brand defence ever offered by a British politician. 🇸🇪 Sweden confirms 458 Britons deported since Brexit

The Guardian

Sweden has confirmed for the first time that 458 British citizens have been deported or forcibly removed since Brexit — a figure that makes public what campaigners have been warning about for years. The deportations affect Britons who had lived in Sweden for decades but failed to secure residency status after the UK left the EU. The data comes amid mounting criticism of Sweden's approach as "cruel and hardline." For the Britons affected, Brexit meant losing not just EU citizenship but the right to live in a country many had called home for 20, 30, or 40 years. The story is a quiet reminder that Brexit's human cost is not measured in GDP projections but in lives uprooted.

458 British citizens forcibly removed from a country they'd made their home. That's the quiet, undramatic tragedy of Brexit — not the trade deals or the sovereignty arguments, but the people who trusted the system and got deported by it. 💻 Statichost.eu — European static site hosting, no strings

Statichost.eu (469pts, HN #26)

A new European static site hosting service has launched with a refreshingly simple proposition: host your static site on EU-based servers, no JavaScript required, no cookie banners, no analytics, just a folder of HTML files served over HTTPS.
