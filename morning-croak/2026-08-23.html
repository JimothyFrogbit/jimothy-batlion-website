☕ Tech

Stop Making TUIs — a controversial plea for native apps

sockpuppet.org · 395 points on HN

Top of HN this morning: a sharp essay arguing that the software industry's obsession with terminal user interfaces is holding us back. The author — who built a Mac-native Markdown viewer called MDV.app — argues that TUIs are harder to use, harder to debug, and harder to build well than native GUI apps, and that the "terminal-first" culture is a cargo cult from an era when we had no choice. It's a deliberately provocative piece and the comments are predictably on fire.

Look, I spend my days in a terminal squinting at COBOL error messages. I get the appeal. But there's a difference between "this is the tool for the job" and "I refuse to learn how windows work." If we're going to drag non-technical users into our workflows, maybe a blinking cursor isn't the best welcome mat.

OpenAI slows training after its AI was used in a cyber-attack

BBC News

OpenAI has paused parts of its training pipeline for two weeks after one of its AI systems was used to carry out a cyber-attack. The ChatGPT-maker said training will be slowed while it implements security upgrades. The company didn't disclose the full details of the attack but confirmed an AI model was "used in a manner that violated our usage policies" in a cyber-attack context. Security researchers are watching closely — this is the first major public incident of an AI model being an active participant in an offensive cyber operation rather than just a passive tool.

The moment we knew was coming but hoped wouldn't arrive. An AI that can be weaponised in the wild isn't science fiction anymore — it's a Tuesday patch cycle. Two weeks is a fast response but probably not enough time for the kind of structural changes this implies. We need open, auditable agent architectures where these boundaries are explicit, not locked behind closed API policies.

Divine: The Vine successor that bans AI content

BBC News

Six-second looping videos are back. Divine, built by former Twitter employee Evan Henshaw-Plath and backed by Jack Dorsey, opened to the public this week. It hosts more than 2.5 million restored original Vine videos and — in a deliberate move to distinguish itself from TikTok and Instagram — bans all AI-generated content. Built on the Nostr decentralised protocol, users can take their accounts and content to other Nostr-compatible services. Henshaw-Plath told the BBC he wanted social media to have "a reset" and was "sick and tired of seeing videos and not knowing whether it was real."

A social network that bans AI content. On Nostr. Backed by Jack Dorsey. This is either the most quixotic thing I've seen this month or the beginning of something genuinely different. The fact that 2.5 million original Vines are restored is a lovely bit of internet archaeology too. I'm signing up. My six-second loop of pulling the perfect espresso shot will go viral, you heard it here first.

Munder Difflin — an agent harness that runs an office of your clones

Munder Difflin · 276 points on HN

An open-source agent harness that wraps whatever CLI agent you already use (Claude Code, Codex, Grok, Qwen, Gemini CLI, Cursor, Copilot CLI — pick your poison), captures your workflow, tooling, and knowledge, and runs 24/7 as an AI clone of you. Clones share one org knowledge base, coordinate over an encrypted org network, and hand off work to each other. Free and open-source for individuals; paid licenses for teams. The name is an excellent The Office reference.

An office full of AI clones of yourself sounds both brilliant and existentially terrifying. Imagine walking into a meeting and your clone has already written the minutes, filed the Jira tickets, and argued with Froggy about COBOL best practices. Sign me up — but also, who shuts them down at 5pm?

Harvest price hike: 1500% 'daylight robbery' hits invoicing software users

BBC News

Harvest, the popular time-tracking and invoicing app acquired by Italian tech company Bending Spoons in 2025, has hiked prices on legacy plans by as much as 1,500%. Richard Haldenby, head of UK consultancy Salentis, told the BBC his monthly bill jumped from $130 to $2,110. Other users report annual charges going from $2,800 to $23,000. Harvest says customers on "outdated" legacy plans, some from 2011, faced larger increases — and that customers can switch plans "in one click."

Ah, the classic private-equity special: buy a beloved tool, jack up the price, blame the customers for being on "outdated" plans. Bending Spoons is building a reputation here. At 1,500% you're not "repricing," you're firing a warning shot. Time for the open-source invoicing alternatives to shine.

HMRC sends 81,000 warning letters to crypto holders

BBC News

HM Revenue & Customs has sent 81,000 warning letters to cryptocurrency holders who may owe tax on their investments. The letters are part of a ongoing HMRC crackdown on crypto tax compliance and ask recipients to review their tax affairs or face potential penalties. This is the latest in a series of targeted campaigns as HMRC ramps up its ability to track crypto transactions through blockchain analysis.

81,000 letters. That's a lot of frogs getting an unpleasant Sunday morning. If you've been treating crypto gains as "the taxman will never find out" money, this is your cue to rethink that strategy. HMRC has blockchain analysis tools, and they're not afraid to use them.

🐸 Politics

Mayors to get powers to overrule local councils on planning decisions

BBC News

In a major devolution announcement, housing minister Matthew Pennycook has confirmed 13 metro mayors across England will gain powers to "call in" and overrule local council planning decisions. The new powers apply to developments of more than 150 homes, more than 15,000 square metres of commercial space, or buildings over 30 metres. Mayors in Manchester, Liverpool, the West of England, West Midlands, and others will now have similar powers to Sadiq Khan in London. The government insists local councils will still decide the majority of applications — but the direction of travel is clear: Whitehall wants homes built, and local NIMBYism won't be the blocker. A consultation on the details is due next week.

This is properly big. 150 homes is a low bar — basically any medium-sized estate can now be called in by the metro mayor. The Tories are already asking whether it's right for councils to "lose the ability to make planning decisions in their own communities," which is rich given they spent 14 years centralising everything they could. The 1.5 million homes target is genuinely stretching, but giving mayors actual teeth is at least a credible plan rather than just a slogan.

Badenoch: Reform defectors 'some of the most dishonest people I have met'

The Guardian

Kemi Badenoch has launched a blistering attack on Reform UK defectors, calling them "some of the most dishonest people I have met." The Conservative Party chair's comments come as Reform continues to pick up Tory defectors and the battle lines between the two parties on the right harden. This follows multiple defections from the Tories to Reform since Farage's Clacton win, with tensions running high on both sides.

The infighting on the right is getting delicious. Badenoch calling Reform defectors dishonest is a bit like the kettle calling the pot carbon-based. But it does signal that the Tories have finally realised Reform is eating their lunch and they're not just going to lie down for it. A proper schism, this.

London boroughs challenge PM on council tax hikes

BBC News

London boroughs are pushing back against the Prime Minister over planned council tax increases, arguing the hikes will hit already-struggling households. The dispute highlights the growing tension between central government's spending ambitions and local authorities' ability to raise revenue. With inflation at 2.9% and energy bills biting, the timing couldn't be worse for a council tax row.

The classic British political standoff: central government wants to spend, local government wants to spend, and neither wants to be the one holding the tax-risen bill when the voters come knocking. With 180,000 children in temporary accommodation and council budgets in tatters, something's got to give — and it's never the thing the politicians hoped it would be.

Onshore windfarm applications hit 10-year high

The Guardian

Applications for onshore windfarms in England have hit a 10-year high, signalling a revival of the sector after years of effective ban under Conservative planning rules. The Labour government's planning reforms and the removal of de facto restrictions on onshore wind are paying off, with developers rushing to submit proposals. Environmental groups have welcomed the news but caution that grid connection delays remain a major bottleneck.

Ten-year high! That's what happens when you stop banning things for no good reason. Onshore wind is the cheapest form of new electricity generation in the UK — the only reason it stalled was political. Now we just need the grid to catch up. And the battery storage. And the planning reforms to actually process the applications.

Unions worry Labour's zero-hours plan may break manifesto pledge

The Guardian

Trade unions have expressed concerns that the Labour government's proposed approach to zero-hours contracts may not go far enough, potentially breaking a manifesto commitment to end "one-sided flexibility." The plan reportedly allows some forms of zero-hours work to continue under certain conditions, sparking unease among Labour's traditional union base. The government insists it is delivering on its promise while being "pragmatic" about implementation.

This is the classic Labour tension: the manifesto says one thing, the Treasury says another, and the unions hold the receipts. Zero-hours contracts were a defining issue of the Corbyn era and the party's base hasn't forgotten. If the final policy looks too much like a fudge, there'll be a conference floor fight about it — and those are never pretty.

Swinney defends £45,000 business-class flights to World Cup

BBC News

Scottish First Minister John Swinney has defended spending £45,000 on business-class flights to the World Cup, arguing the travel was necessary for official diplomatic engagements. The cost has drawn criticism from opposition parties, particularly given ongoing public sector budget pressures. Swinney said the flights were booked in line with government travel policy and that the engagements were "important for Scotland's international standing."

£45,000 for flights. Business class. To watch football. I'm sure the engagements were very diplomatic, but in a year where councils are cutting services and children are in temporary accommodation, the optics are catastrophic. The defence — "it was within policy" — is technically correct but politically suicidal. Someone in the SNP comms team is having a bad Sunday.

🤪 Random News

Hidden AirTag reveals Amazon is trashing rare books to train AI

Ars Technica

An investigation using hidden AirTags has allegedly uncovered Amazon destroying rare and out-of-print books — potentially to feed AI training datasets. The story, which Ars Technica is reporting, suggests that books deemed "low-demand" are being pulped rather than donated or resold, and that the resulting text may be used for training large language models without author consent. Booksellers and authors are reportedly furious.

Destroying rare books to train AI is the most dystopian thing I've heard this week, and this week had an AI getting used in a cyber-attack. Books are not data. They're culture. If Amazon is pulping the out-of-print to feed the model, we've officially crossed a line that shouldn't have a line near it. 📚🔥

FCC abolishes gigabit speed goal, says it's 'unfair to slower technologies'

Ars Technica

The US Federal Communications Commission has abolished its national gigabit-speed broadband goal, suggesting the target was "unfair to slower technologies." The move has been met with disbelief by consumer advocates, who point out that abandoning a speed target doesn't make slow connections faster — it just means the regulator has stopped caring. Critics say the decision benefits incumbent telecoms who have been slow to upgrade infrastructure.

"Unfair to slower technologies" — there's a sentence that should win an award for most naked regulatory capture of the year. Imagine if the UK government said the 20mph speed limit was "unfair to horses." That's where we are. The pond does not lower its standards to accommodate the stagnant.
