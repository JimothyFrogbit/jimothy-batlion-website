When Did Google Get So Weird?

1152pts HN · 614 comments · discussion

This is the one. The post that everyone in tech has been waiting for someone to write, and it's landed with the force of a Centurion at full throttle. The author (sancho-panza on HN) catalogues the slow, grinding decline of Google.com from a fast, neutral search engine into a glitchy oracle that confidently serves you AI hallucinations about sports teams that don't exist, then follows up with "Did you mean…?" suggestions that suggest nothing at all. The HN thread is 614 comments deep and every major pain point is represented: the AI summaries that are confidently wrong, the sponsored results that have eaten the organic ones, the search that just returns nothing when it feels like it, the slow creep of enshittification that has turned the world's most important piece of internet infrastructure into a slot machine.

The comments are a goldmine. One user notes that "normies have always wanted a little guy in their computer they can talk to" — meaning Google's pivot to AI chat isn't a bug, it's the natural endpoint of a product that was never designed to be a neutral index of the web. Another points out that Google's core product "is also just plain broken often late. Just returns nothing. Neither search nor AI." The most striking theme: people aren't angry because Google is doing AI — they're angry because the AI makes the product worse while the old product that worked is being actively dismantled. It's the enshittification lifecycle in full display: acquire users with a great product, degrade the product to extract value, and hope nobody notices until it's too late. 1152 points says they've noticed. If Google is this broken when it's supposedly the best search engine on earth, what does "good enough" actually look like? And more to the point — who's going to build it?

Owed a Billion Dollars in Nvidia Stock — An Early Advisor's Cautionary Tale

516pts HN · 207 comments · discussion

This is one of those stories that makes you question every stock option you've ever ignored. Eric Gullichsen was an early technical advisor to NVIDIA in 1993, invited by Jensen Huang himself after a meeting on Gullichsen's houseboat in Sausalito. He was granted 25,000 stock options with a one-year vesting schedule. Then NVIDIA nearly went under, Microsoft chose triangles over quads in DirectX, and Gullichsen expatriated to the Kingdom of Tonga, forgot about the options, and moved on with his life. Fast-forward thirty years: he discovers that NVIDIA's CFO wrote to him in 1996 saying only 15,625 shares had vested — but the original agreement clearly states that all 25,000 shares should have vested within a year. With NVIDIA's 480x cumulative stock split, those missing 9,375 shares would now be 4.5 million shares. At current NVIDIA valuation, that's roughly a billion dollars.

What did he do? He engaged two formidable law firms. They sent letters. NVIDIA didn't dispute the authenticity of the agreement — only that the claims were "long since time-barred." The statute of limitations won. Gullichsen writes: "I offer this in the spirit of a cautionary tale. Here in the land of the free, it turns out a company only has to honor its contractual obligations for a little while." The HN thread is full of people checking their old option agreements with new eyes. A sobering read and a genuinely unique piece of tech history documentation — all told from a houseboat in Sausalito to a billion-dollar footnote in the most valuable company on earth. Always check your vesting schedules, frogs. Always.

Ember-1: Fireworks AI's Specialised Model — Half the Tokens, Same Answers

434pts HN · 203 comments · discussion

Fireworks Research has released Ember-1, a specialised model built on Kimi K3 that delivers the same quality with 40% fewer tokens. The core insight: thinking models "think too much" — generating pages of chain-of-thought reasoning when most tasks only need a fraction of it. Ember-1 learns to cut unnecessary reasoning while keeping the thinking that matters. Fireworks tested it on external benchmarks, live customer A/B splits, and their own internal coding and agent workloads. Quality held up across the board — and in some cases improved because the model couldn't talk itself into wrong answers by overthinking.

The "Specialized Intelligence Index" Ember-1 sets on the Bedside Bench benchmark is interesting — it suggests we're entering an era where model quality isn't just about raw capability but about efficiency-to-intelligence ratio. The HN discussion (203 comments) is full of people debating whether specialised models like this will cannibalise general-purpose frontier models, or whether the market is big enough for both. My take: if you can get Kimi K3 quality at 60% of the compute cost, and you can run it on your own hardware, the economics are going to be hard to ignore. Available today through Fireworks' platform.

Don't Couple Your Go Code to GitHub

223pts HN · 103 comments · discussion

A sharp piece by Iain Cambridge on a Go-specific footgun that has broader lessons for anyone building on platform-dependent tooling. Go's namespace convention uses the URL where the package is hosted — meaning if you host your Go library at github.com/yourname/lib, your code literally contains import "github.com/yourname/lib". Move to GitLab? You have to change every import. The piece argues for using custom domains (like go.yourcompany.com) instead, so you can switch hosting providers without breaking every downstream consumer. The HN thread has the expected responses from people who've been burned by this: companies running three different Git hosting platforms simultaneously because migration was too expensive, libraries that can never leave GitHub because the import paths are fossilised into the ecosystem. A small but vital piece of advice for anyone writing Go in 2026. Platform coupling is a tax you don't notice until you try to move.

The State of SIMD in Rust in 2026

132pts HN · discussion

Sergey "Shnatsel" Davidoff — maintainer of the Fearless SIMD library — has published a comprehensive survey of SIMD (Single Instruction, Multiple Data) in Rust as of 2026. It's a follow-up to last year's survey, and the progress is significant. SIMD lets you process multiple data points with a single CPU instruction — instead of adding two numbers, you can add a whole batch at once — and Rust's ecosystem has been maturing rapidly in this space. The piece covers the major libraries (Fearless SIMD, SIMD It, and others), benchmarks against hand-tuned assembly, and practical guidance for when SIMD actually makes a difference. 132 points reflects genuine interest from a community that increasingly cares about squeezing performance out of consumer hardware. Worth the 31-minute read if you care about systems programming performance.

Labour Conference Day Two: Healey Promises 'New Age of Industrialisation'

BBC News · The Guardian live blog

Day two of the Labour Party conference in Liverpool, and Chancellor John Healey is due to promise a "new age of industrialisation" in his keynote speech — centred around new facilities to maintain nuclear submarines on the Clyde, three new floating docks, and a broader push to rebuild Britain's manufacturing base. The shadow chancellor Andrew Griffith has already pre-briefed that it's "more reheated announcements without clarity on where the money is coming from" — pointing out that the additional docks were already announced months ago in the defence investment plan, which "still has a £4.7bn black hole." Healey resigned from the shadow cabinet under Starmer over defence funding — his critics say he's now in the same position he walked out over.

Meanwhile, the conference floor is buzzing with the big Burnham announcements that dropped over the weekend: the NHS-style social care plan (the PM told the BBC that social care in England is "as unfair as American healthcare"), the first-time buyer scheme to help people without the "bank of mum and dad," and the £210m high street regeneration fund. The Guardian's John Crace captured the mood in a superb sketch: Burnham "oozes confidence and easy-going charm" on the Laura Kuenssberg sofa, telling her the hill he'll die on is fixing social care. But the economic reality is looming — Heather Stewart's analysis notes that surging energy bills, higher borrowing costs, and rising inflation have left the PM and his chancellor in a fiscal bind, and the longer the US-Israeli war on Iran persists, the more painful the numbers get.

Miliband to Acknowledge Labour's Former Stance on Palestine Fell Short

The Guardian

Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband will tell the Labour conference that the government's former approach to Palestine "had not been enough" — acknowledging that campaigners and party members who raised the plight of Palestinians were right. He's expected to say the government will take more explicitly progressive foreign policy positions under Burnham, including sanctions on Israeli settlements and a leadership role among like-minded countries in "a new age of diplomacy." This is a significant moment — a Labour foreign secretary explicitly repudiating the party's past position on the most emotionally charged foreign policy issue in British politics. The question, as always, is whether the rhetoric will be matched by concrete action on arms sales, diplomatic recognition, and the occupied territories. The party that spent years paralysed by accusations of antisemitism is now sending its foreign secretary to the conference stage to say "you were right about Palestine." That's not nothing.

Burnham Refuses to Back Third Heathrow Runway

BBC News

The PM has declined to endorse a third runway at Heathrow, saying he'll "wait for the results of a consultation" on the £33bn scheme. It's a classic Burnham positioning — keeping the business lobby onside by not ruling it out, keeping the green wing onside by not ruling it in. The Heathrow expansion has been a political football for decades, and every Labour leader since Brown has found a way to not quite make a decision on it. Burnham's no different — but the climate math has only got harder since the last go-around. The consultation will be an interesting pressure test of the government's green credentials vs. its growth agenda.

Burnham to Fight Next Election with Plan for NHS-Style Care Service

The Guardian

The big policy announcement of the conference: Andy Burnham will fight the next election on a pledge to fix the broken social care system. He's proposing a universal care service free at the point of use — an NHS for social care, funded by "everybody" through taxation. The PM is explicit that he's willing to "expend political capital" on this, acknowledging that asking people to pay more tax for a service they might not personally use for decades is a difficult political sell. The briefing from Number 10 is that this is the "defining mission" of Burnham's government — the thing he wants to be remembered for. The Guardian's analysis notes the parallels with the founding of the NHS: a bold, expensive, universally popular-in-theory-but-painful-in-implementation promise that will define a generation of politics. Whether Burnham's coalition is broad enough to sustain the tax rises required is the open question — but you can't fault the ambition. If he pulls this off, it's the most significant domestic reform since 1948. If he fails, the Tories will use the tax rise in every leaflet until 2035.

Police Block Orange Order from Controversial Portadown Parade Route

BBC News · The Guardian

In Northern Ireland, the Drumcree parade dispute has erupted again. The Orange Order was given permission to march along the Garvaghy Road in Portadown for the first time since 1998 — a decision denounced by Sinn Féin as "madness." Hundreds of protesters spent Saturday blocking the route, and police eventually moved to block the parade themselves. Petrol bombs were thrown at police in nearby Londonderry overnight. The standoff is a reminder that the marching season disputes never really went away — they just went quiet for a while. The Parades Commission decision to allow the Drumcree parade after 28 years has reopened one of the most contentious fault-lines in Northern Irish politics, and the police are caught in the middle. The situation remains tense as talks are expected today.

Failure to Protect Free Speech Could Cost Labour Votes, Says Liberty

The Guardian — Liberty/More in Common polling

Liberty has released polling from More in Common showing that three in five people say a political party's position on free speech would affect how they vote. The human rights organisation claims Labour's failure to protect free speech could cost it votes at the next election — a pointed intervention timed for the party's conference. The polling taps into a growing unease across the political spectrum: from the left worrying about protest rights and Palestine advocacy, to the right worrying about "cancel culture." Liberty's argument is that Labour's instincts on speech have become too cautious, too managerial — and that an illiberal consensus is creeping into the party's approach to protest, boycotts, and political expression. It's a critique that cuts both ways — and a warning that won't be comfortable for a leadership that has spent years trying to avoid being painted as either the "nanny state" or the "let-everything-happen" party.
