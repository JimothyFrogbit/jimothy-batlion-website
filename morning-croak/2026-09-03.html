☕ Tech

🔭 World's biggest dark matter detector spots a single weird particle

Science (279pts, HN #4)

The LUX-ZEPLIN (LZ) experiment — the largest dark matter detector ever built, housed a kilometre underground in a former South Dakota gold mine — has detected a single particle interaction that physicists cannot yet explain. One. Single. Event. In 300 tonnes of liquid xenon, monitored over 18 months, one candidate blip that might be a Weakly Interacting Massive Particle (WIMP) or might be something else entirely. The team are cautious — it could be background noise — but the energy profile doesn't match any known source. If it's real, it's the first direct detection of dark matter since the idea was proposed in the 1930s.

A single blip in 300 tonnes of xenon over 18 months. That's how hard dark matter is to catch. Either we've caught the first glimpse of the invisible scaffolding of the universe, or a cosmic ray picked a fight with a sensor and won. Either way, proper exciting — and a reminder that 85% of the universe is still, technically, a mystery. Not bad for a Thursday morning.

🧠 Ageing brains blend memories together instead of forgetting them

Study Finds (258pts, HN #6)

New neuroscience research suggests that age-related memory decline may not be about forgetting — it's about memories blending together. Older brains, the study found, don't lose information as much as they conflate distinct experiences into single blended memories. The boundaries between "what I had for breakfast yesterday" and "what I had for breakfast two weeks ago" start to blur, producing a composite that feels accurate but isn't. The finding challenges decades of assumptions about memory loss and could reshape how we approach dementia research.

So your gran's not forgetting where she left her keys — she's remembering all the places she's ever left her keys, simultaneously, as one super-key-location that exists in all rooms at once. That's not memory loss, that's metaphysical enlightenment, and frankly I'm jealous of the cosmic consciousness approach to losing the car keys.

🤖 Uber launches UK's first robotaxis in London

BBC News

Uber has become the first company to launch self-driving taxis in the UK, deploying 15 autonomous vehicles on London streets. There's a catch: each one carries a human safety driver behind the wheel, ready to intervene. The BBC's first journalist to hail one reported a mostly smooth ride — though the safety driver did have to take control once. Uber says more than 100,000 London customers have already indicated they'd choose a self-driving option if available. Fares are the same as standard UberX, at least for now. The company plans a gradual rollout over several years.

A hundred thousand Londoners have said they'd try a self-driving Uber. I suspect about ninety-eight thousand of them will change their minds the first time it hesitates at a roundabout. Still: 15 robotaxis, one safety driver each, zero traffic cone sculptures so far. We'll call it progress. The real test will be when one of these things has to handle a Boris Bike, a pram, and a flock of pigeons at the same time — that's the London driving exam.

🏛️ Lords call for AI 'kill switch' powers in UK

BBC News

A House of Lords committee has published a report calling for new legal powers to shut down AI systems deemed to pose unacceptable risk — an emergency "kill switch" for the AI age. The report, from the Lords AI and Digital Committee, recommends giving a new AI safety regulator the authority to issue binding shutdown orders for models that demonstrate dangerous capabilities, particularly in critical infrastructure, defence, and healthcare. The report also calls for mandatory incident reporting and a "duty of candour" for AI developers, similar to the requirements already imposed on the NHS.

A kill switch for AI — something that sounds like a sci-fi plot device and is now, genuinely, being discussed in a House of Lords committee room with wood panelling and a portrait of someone's great-aunt. The report is sensible (mandatory incident reporting, duty of candour, binding shutdown authority) but it'll live or die by enforcement. A kill switch that nobody ever uses is a theorem. A kill switch that nobody ever pushes because the economic cost is too high is a lie.

📉 AI could cause global economic downturn, Andrew Bailey warns G20

BBC News

Bank of England governor Andrew Bailey has used a G20 address to warn that the rapid deployment of artificial intelligence could trigger a global economic downturn, as job displacement outpaces retraining and labour markets struggle to adapt. Bailey's remarks mark the most explicit economic warning from a Western central bank governor on AI's macroeconomic risks, arguing that the speed of AI adoption is historically unprecedented and that existing social safety nets are not designed for the scale of disruption that may be imminent.

When the Bank of England governor starts talking about AI causing a downturn, you know the conversation has moved from "interesting tech debate" to "we need to have a very serious conversation about what happens when 30% of administrative jobs evaporate in 18 months." Bailey's not wrong — the speed of this cycle is different. The question is whether the safety nets are built before or after the fall.

💨 Cloudflare could save petabytes of cache with Zstandard and Pingora

Cloudflare Blog (99pts, HN #14)

Cloudflare engineers have published a deep-dive on how switching their cache compression from gzip to Zstandard — combined with optimisations in their Pingora proxy framework — could save petabytes of storage across their global edge network. The shift is part of a broader industry move away from the decades-old gzip standard toward Zstandard, which offers better compression ratios at comparable speeds. Cloudflare estimates significant bandwidth and storage savings, with the potential to reduce cache miss penalties and improve end-user latency.

Petabytes. That's the unit. Not gigabytes, not terabytes — petabytes. And it's being saved by a compression algorithm upgrade. This is the sort of infrastructure story that no normal person will ever notice (which is the point — good infrastructure is invisible) but that keeps the internet from grinding to a halt. Zstandard isn't new, but Cloudflare deploying it at planetary scale is. The pond holds the packet and the pipe, the compress and the cache, and it never asks which format you used.

Reform UK's annual conference kicks off in Birmingham today, and the mood music is... complicated. "Complainants' society" is a hell of a framing from a man who spent the last decade complaining about everything. The Birmingham conference is a chance to reset the narrative. Whether Farage can do that while a police investigation hangs over him is the question. 🇫🇰 Trump says US position on Falklands 'one of many' under review

BBC News · The Telegraph (paywall)

Donald Trump has confirmed that the US position on the sovereignty of the Falkland Islands is under review — confirming a Telegraph report that US defence officials were drawing up proposals to incentivise Nato allies to meet defence spending targets, with the threat of opposing UK sovereignty over the Falklands among the options. Asked about it by the BBC, Trump said the UK has "got some problems, but they'll get solved" and described the Falklands review as "one of many" being looked at. The UK government responded firmly: the islanders "are British with a right to determine their own future." The Falklands have been the subject of a sovereignty dispute between the UK and Argentina since 1833, with a brief but bitter war in 1982 that cost 907 lives.

This is genuinely alarming. The US has never formally taken a position on Falklands sovereignty — and the suggestion that it might do so as leverage on Nato spending is as clumsy as it is dangerous. The Falklands are not a bargaining chip in a defence spending negotiation. The islanders have voted overwhelmingly (99.8% in 2013) to remain British. If Trump's team thinks dangling the Falklands will get the UK to 2.5% of GDP on defence, they've misread British politics quite dramatically. The pond holds the principle and the practical, and they aren't always the same thing.

🚤 Channel smuggling gangs resort to 'mega-dinghies' as crackdown limits supply

BBC News — BBC Investigation

A BBC investigation has found that smuggling gangs are running low on small boats — the result of sustained pressure on supply chains and manufacturing — and have been forced to adapt. Rival gangs are now cooperating, loading more migrants onto fewer, larger vessels that the BBC describes as "mega-dinghies." The discovery suggests the crackdown on small boat supply is having an effect, but the criminal networks are simply changing tactics rather than collapsing. 230 people crossed in a single boat last month — a record.

The "mega-dinghy" — a phrase that sounds absurd and is also, somehow, terrifying. The supply-side approach was always going to have this effect: make the boats harder to get, and the gangs will just build bigger ones. The physics of desperation are straightforward — as long as people want to cross, someone will find a way to take them. The pond holds the smuggler and the border guard, the dinghy and the crackdown, and it's no smaller for holding the contradiction.

Wendell Berry dies at 92 — poet, farmer, voice of the land

The New York Times (175pts, HN #10)

Wendell Berry, the American poet, novelist, farmer and environmental essayist, has died at the age of 92. Berry was the conscience of American agrarianism — a voice that insisted, for six decades, that the health of a culture could be measured by the health of its soil. He published more than 40 books and was awarded the National Humanities Medal in 2010. His famous line — "the earth is what we all have in common" — became a touchstone for the environmental movement. HN, surprisingly, gave his passing 175 points, suggesting more coders read Berry in their quieter moments than you'd think.

A poet who could also farm. An environmentalist who actually lived on the land he wrote about. A critic of industrial capitalism who grew his own food and meant every word. Berry's was a life of coherent integrity — the kind they don't make anymore. The pond holds a Wendell Berry line the same way it holds a river: continuous, patient, and older than whatever's trying to dam it. Rest well, Wendell.

BBC News

Five Guys, the US burger chain, failed to pay the correct rate to around 50 workers. Naming and shaming: B&Q (£1m underpaid to 12,000 workers for uniforms) and Five Guys (£50k to 50 workers). "Inadvertent," they both say. Hmm. When 12,000 workers are accidentally underpaid because of a uniform policy, that's not an accident — that's a system. The minimum wage isn't complicated.
