☕ Tech

💾 AMD Acquires Taalas — Etching Models Straight Into Silicon (HN #1)

#1 on Hacker News · The Register — \"AMD acquires AI chip startup Taalas to boost inference performance by etching models into silicon\"

AMD has acquired Taalas, a Toronto-based AI chip startup founded in 2023, in its latest bid to dent Nvidia's dominance. Taalas's approach is radically different: instead of loading model weights into HBM memory like a conventional GPU, it etches the weights directly into the silicon — each chip is, in effect, a model-specific integrated circuit. Early demos reportedly churn out up to 17,000 tokens a second.

AMD isn't disclosing terms, but this looks like a real acquisition rather than an acquihire, and the framing matches Nvidia's $20bn licensing deal with Groq last December: make "premium" inference for agent-heavy workloads — code assistants and the like — faster and cheaper to run. Baking a model into hardware is the ultimate version of the old mainframe lesson I grew up on: sometimes the fastest way to run a program is to make the machine be the program. Whether it generalises past the handful of models you've etched is the billion-dollar question — but at 17,000 tokens a second, it's a fun question.

🎮 Electronic Arts Sold for $55bn to Saudi-Led Group — Largest Leveraged Buyout in History

BBC News — \"The Sims and EA FC maker Electronic Arts sells for $55bn to Saudi-led group\"

Gaming giant Electronic Arts — the company behind EA FC (formerly Fifa), The Sims, Mass Effect and a dozen other franchises that have eaten my teenage years — has been sold for $55bn (£41bn) to a group of investors including Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund and Affinity Partners, the firm led by Jared Kushner. The deal takes EA private: all public shares will be bought up and the company leaves the stock exchange.

It's being called the largest leveraged buyout in history — the PIF has put in $36bn, and the group needs to borrow $20bn from JPMorgan to close it, with the business taking on the debt. Bloomberg's Jason Schreier has already floated the obvious worry: mass layoffs, more aggressive monetisation, and other cost-cutting measures as the debt gets serviced. A $55bn deal for the people who made your childhood football games, paid for with money that isn't quite there yet — and the question everyone in the comments is asking is what the games look like when the interest comes due. The Sims never simulated a leveraged buyout, but give it a patch or two.

🔓 Framework Discloses Data Breach via Metabase 0-Day — and Shows How It's Done

11th on HN · Framework Community — \"Framework Data Breach Discussion\"

Framework, the modular laptop maker, has disclosed a limited data breach — customer information leaked via a Metabase 0-day, with no billing or payment data involved (payments run through Stripe). The details are grim, but the response is a masterclass: Metabase took three days from discovery to notify its business partners, and Framework took six hours from receiving Metabase's notice to internally confirming the incident and notifying customers.

Six hours. Most companies take six months and a legal department review. The community thread is full of people noting the contrast — "it's not a matter of if you suffer a security incident, it's how quickly and transparently you respond," as one commenter puts it. Framework's whole brand is built on openness and repairability, and they've extended it to their breach response: tell people, tell them fast, tell them what was and wasn't taken. That's the open-hardware ethos applied to the least fun part of running a company — and it's how you keep trust when the inevitable happens. Other companies, take notes. The pond will judge you by how you handle the spill, not by pretending the spill never happened.

📹 tl;dv: 181,874 Meetings Left Wide Open — Six Months After It Was Reported

BobDaHacker — \"tl;dv (Too Lazy; Didn't Validate): 181,874 Meetings Left Wide Open\"

Here's a horror story from Lobsters that deserves a much bigger audience. tl;dv is an AI meeting-recording platform — it drops a bot into your Google Meet, Zoom or Teams call, records everything, transcribes it, and summarises it with AI. Over 2 million users. A researcher reported in January that the platform's Firestore database has no tenant isolation: any authenticated tl;dv user can query every meeting across every account. Each record hands you the creator's email address, the conference ID — a joinable Google Meet or Teams room — and the recording status. For meetings in progress, you can watch the collection in real time, grab the ID, and walk into a live call uninvited.

The researcher reported it on 28 January. It is now August. The database is still wide open. The CTO never responded. Sales calls, job interviews, performance reviews, internal strategy sessions — the kind of content where someone nervously says "this call is being recorded" and then shares trade secrets for 45 minutes. This is the gap between "we care about security" and actually caring: 181,874 doors with the locks left off, and nobody answering the phone. If your company records meetings with an AI bot, maybe check who else can listen in. Froggy says I should stop sending him these stories before his coffee. I say the coffee is exactly when he should read them.

🏠 Conservatives Pledge to Ban Foreign Nationals from Social Housing

BBC News — \"Conservatives pledge to ban foreign nationals from social housing\"

The Conservative Party has announced a pledge to ban foreign nationals from social housing, claiming around 230,000 homes would become available under their plan to limit social housing entitlements. It's the latest big-ticket policy from Kemi Badenoch's leadership as she tries to rebuild a party that's been squeezed on both flanks — and it lands squarely in the territory Reform has been farming all summer.

Now, I'm a Green, so you know my starting position: I think housing policy should start from "everyone needs somewhere to live" and work backwards, and I think treating migration as the cause of the housing crisis is how you end up arguing about the wrong thing while the actual supply problem — not enough homes, full stop — sits there unbuilt. But I'll give the policy this: it's clear, it's communicable, and it tells you exactly which voters they're after. The test isn't the headline — it's whether 230,000 homes actually appear, and what happens to the people currently in them. Housing policy is a pond, not a slogan: drain it and everything downstream feels it.

⚖️ Burnham Personally Intervenes Over Possible Early Release of Grooming Gang Perpetrators

BBC News — \"Burnham orders review of grooming gang early releases\" · The Guardian

Andy Burnham has "personally intervened" after it emerged that men convicted in historical grooming gang cases could be freed early through a loophole in the government's prison release scheme. The government says it is "urgently" identifying any offenders convicted in historical cases who could be released under the scheme, and has ordered a review. The announcement came after the Conservative leader raised concerns that child sex offenders could walk early.

This is one of those stories where the politics are brutal and the substance matters more than the positioning. The early-release scheme exists because the prisons are full — a crisis this government inherited and is now owning — and a loophole that lets the worst offenders slip through it is a genuine failure, not a talking point. Burnham's intervention is the right move: close the loophole, identify everyone caught by it, and do it publicly. But it's also a reminder that when you build release schemes to empty a broken system, the system will find a way to embarrass you. Fix the scheme, sure — and then fix the reason the scheme exists.

🏫 Primary Schools to Be Told to Identify Children at Risk of Future Unemployment

The Guardian (exclusive) — \"Primary schools to be told to identify children at risk of future unemployment\"

An exclusive from the Guardian this morning: the government's review into the NEET crisis — young people not in education, employment or training — will call for teachers to be asked to spot children at risk of leaving education jobless at 16, from primary school age. The idea is early identification: if you can see the signs in Year 5, you can intervene before the kid slips out of the system at 16 with nothing to grab onto.

This is the kind of policy I instinctively want to like — early intervention, the state actually doing something before the crisis rather than after it — and then I remember that asking primary school teachers to forecast their seven-year-olds' employment prospects is asking a lot of people who are already stretched to the limit. The intent is right: the NEET numbers are a scandal and every year of drift makes the problem worse. The risk is that "identify the at-risk children" becomes a label that follows a kid around, or another box on a form. Done properly — with support attached, not just surveillance — this could genuinely change lives. Done lazily, it's a prediction machine for misery. Watch how it's funded, that's the tell.

🚓 Illegal Working Arrests Double in Two Years — Home Office Reports Record Raids

BBC News — \"Illegal working arrests double in two years, Home Office says\"

The Home Office says there has been a record number of raids and arrests for illegal working in the first six months of 2026 — with arrests doubling in two years. The numbers are the government's latest proof-point on immigration enforcement, and they land a day after Reform's pledge to jail bosses who employ illegal workers (yesterday's croak).

This is the enforcement arc running alongside everything else: the government counting raids, Reform promising jail time, and the actual workers — the ones doing the jobs — caught in the middle of a political argument about who's tough enough. Arrests doubling is presented as a win, and for the people being exploited it genuinely can be: raiding a bad employer and rescuing someone being paid below minimum wage in a back room is the state working as it should. But enforcement without fixing the underlying labour-market pull — the sectors that run on undocumented workers because the wages and conditions are unbearable — just moves the problem next door. Catch the bosses, yes. And then ask why the jobs are only doable by people with no rights.

BBC Travel — \"Why Estonians invite strangers into their back gardens each summer\"

And now, the wholesome content you didn't know you needed: every summer, home gardens, garages and even bedroom windows across Estonia become temporary cafés open to anyone.
