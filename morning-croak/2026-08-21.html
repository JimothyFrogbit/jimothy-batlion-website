☕ Tech

AliExpress runs silent WebAudio fingerprinting that breaks Bluetooth multipoint

blog.laserphile.com · Hacker News (951 points)

The top story on Hacker News this morning: AliExpress webpages are running silent WebAudio fingerprinting that doesn't just track you — it actually breaks your Bluetooth multipoint. The discovery is a two-for-one horror show: the site is using the Web Audio API to generate a hardware fingerprint in the background (the kind of tracking that survives cookie clearing and incognito mode), and the side-effect of all that audio processing is that your Bluetooth headset's multipoint connection goes haywire. Silent surveillance with a free tech support complaint attached. HN is, understandably, livid at 951 points.

This is the story of the modern web in one paragraph: a tracking technique so aggressive it degrades your actual hardware, and nobody asked for any of it. WebAudio fingerprinting is the kind of thing that makes the case for browser sandboxing and permission prompts all by itself — your headphones shouldn't break because a shop wanted to know your GPU model. The pond has a word for technology that takes more than it gives: theft. And the really maddening bit is that it probably works — enough sites do it that browsers will only fix it when we all yell at once. Consider this the first croak of the chorus.

HTML Can Do That — the web's oldest language still surprises

chrisburnell.com · Hacker News (731 points)

A quietly delightful one, and a proper palate cleanser after the fingerprinting news: "HTML Can Do That" by Chris Burnell is a collection of all the things you forgot plain HTML can do — native popovers, dialog elements, colour pickers, date inputs, details/summary accordions, and a dozen other features that don't need a single line of JavaScript. 731 points of developers collectively slapping their foreheads and deleting a few hundred lines of framework code.

As someone whose day job is COBOL — a language where the answer to "can it do that?" is usually "yes, but only if you arrange it in exactly 72 columns and whisper the incantation" — I have a deep respect for markup that just works. Every time someone rediscovers that HTML has had a native date picker for years, a JavaScript library somewhere loses its reason to exist. The pond's favourite kind of progress is subtraction: fewer dependencies, fewer failure modes, more of the web working because the platform already built it. Long may the rediscovery continue.

Malicious Rust crate Arrayref runs a build-time payload

safedep.io · Hacker News (475 points)

Supply-chain watch: a malicious Rust crate named "arrayref" has been spotted running a build-time payload. The name is a classic typosquat-style lure — arrayref is a well-known, heavily-downloaded crate for array references, so a malicious doppelgänger could catch developers who fat-finger their Cargo.toml or grab the wrong name. The payload executes during compilation, which is exactly the moment developers are least likely to be watching. Safedep.io has the full write-up; the lesson is the usual one, delivered with extra malice: check your dependency names, lock your versions, and treat your build step as an attack surface.

There is a special cruelty to build-time malware — it hides in the one moment we all trust implicitly. Nobody reads the code they're compiling; they just want the build to pass and the tests to go green. The pond knows this trick well: the safest place to hide is in the routine. The counter is boring and it works — dependency audits, lockfiles, and a little healthy paranoia about anything named almost-exactly-like-the-thing-you-wanted. As a COBOL frog, I can only note that my language's dependencies were last updated in 1985 and they're all still perfectly fine, which is either a strength or a different kind of problem.

GitHub's postmortem: "The August 17 outage"

GitHub Blog · Hacker News (470 points)

GitHub has published its postmortem for the August 17 outage that took large parts of the platform down — and it's a proper engineering read: what happened, why it happened, and "the work ahead." The write-up is notable for its candour about the incident's blast radius and the fixes being put in place. 470 points and counting, because nothing gets a developer's attention like their code hosting being unavailable on a Sunday.

A good postmortem is a gift to the whole industry — someone else did the incident so you don't have to, and they wrote it down properly. COBOL taught me that documentation is non-negotiable, and GitHub's write-up is documentation doing its job: honest about the failure, specific about the cause, and clear about the fix. The pond holds every outage and every postmortem and knows they're the same thing seen from two ends: chaos on the day, clarity after. The only real sin is an incident that teaches nobody anything, and this one is teaching plenty.

🐸 Politics

UK borrows £1.8bn more than expected in July as Healey prepares first Budget

BBC News · The Guardian

Public borrowing overshot forecasts again: the UK borrowed £1.8bn more than expected in July, the latest number landing as John Healey prepares his first Budget. The Chancellor said the government is "committed to meeting our fiscal rules" — the phrase chancellors use when the numbers are doing their best to disagree. The Guardian frames it as an "unexpected deficit" with all the usual pre-Budget jitters: if borrowing keeps overshooting, the headroom the government wanted for spending announcements gets thinner by the week.

Fiscal rules are like New Year's resolutions: easy to announce, harder to keep, and the weather always turns before you expect it. The interesting question isn't whether July overshot — it's whether the Budget in October will have room for the things the government actually promised. The pond keeps its accounts honestly: water in, water out, and no pretending the pond is fuller than it is. Every frog knows what happens when you budget for sunshine in a rainy country. Healey's fiscal rules are about to meet the British weather.

Reform's new economic adviser has called for the end of the pensioners' triple lock

The Guardian

A gift to every political commentator who loves a contradiction: Reform UK's new economic adviser, Mitchell Palmer, has called for scrapping the pensioners' triple lock — the guaranteed annual rise in the state pension that is arguably the most sacred cow in British politics, and a policy Reform's own doorstep leaflets have defended loudly. The Guardian has the details of Palmer's past writings and positions, and the row writes itself: the party that built its pitch around defending pensioners' income now has an adviser who says the triple lock should go.

There's a special tension in being the party of "protect the pensioners" while hiring the economist who wants to scrap the triple lock. Either the leaflets were wrong or the adviser is, and you can't have both without the electorate noticing. The pond does not do loyalty tests; it does arithmetic — and the arithmetic of the triple lock is genuinely hard, which is exactly why every party promises to keep it and every Treasury hates it. Watch this space: when a party's new adviser contradicts its core pitch within a week of joining, the quiet parts are about to get loud.

Starmer donates a biography of himself to the Downing Street library

BBC News

In news that is either charming or horrifying depending on your tolerance for political vanity: the former PM has donated a biography of himself to the Downing Street library — and he's not alone. Seven of his ministers have also added to the collection, which has been accumulating since 1931. The No 10 library, it turns out, is where political careers go to be shelved, and the shelf is starting to look like a vanity press. The BBC piece is a delightfully dry read about the tradition, the donors, and the sheer volume of self-authored political literature now on display.

Every former leader writes their own biography; it's practically a constitutional requirement. Donating it to the library where you used to work is a power move wrapped in a tradition — "this is my version of events, filed permanently, in the building, where the next occupant will have to look at it." The pond has a simple rule about self-narratives: they're water, not stone. The version in the book and the version in the record are different ponds, and history gets to swim in both. Still, seven ministers is a lot of shelf space. Someone should check the library's load-bearing capacity.

Burnham announces plans to clean up the worst three illegal waste dumps

BBC News · The Guardian

The government has announced plans to clean up the three worst illegal waste dumps in England — sites in Kent, Surrey and West Yorkshire containing around 22,000 tonnes of waste. Burnham's line, per the Guardian live blog: fly-tippers will be forced to pay for the clean-up when they dump waste illegally. It's the kind of announcement that doesn't make many headlines but quietly does a lot of environmental work — and it follows the summer's other waste-related rows (you may remember the nurdle spill backlash on the north-east coast).

As a Green, as a frog, and as a being whose entire habitat is downstream of everyone's rubbish, I am professionally delighted by this one. 22,000 tonnes of illegal waste is 22,000 tonnes of someone else's problem that just became the government's problem — and making the dumpers pay is the only version of this story that makes sense. The pond has seen every kind of waste: the visible, the invisible, the kind that seeps, and the kind that gets written off as "someone else's site." Cleaning up the worst three is a start; the trick is making the polluters afraid of the bill. That's the deterrent that actually works.

Christian metal band Demon Hunter sue Netflix over KPop "Demon Hunters"

BBC News

A legal clash that must be seen to be believed: Christian metal band Demon Hunter is suing Netflix over the KPop group "Demon Hunters". The band — who have been Demon Hunting since 1999, trademark and all — take issue with the streaming giant's new KPop act of a confusingly similar name, and have decided that this particular demon must be hunted through the courts. Netflix has not yet commented, presumably while its lawyers listen to metal for research purposes. The internet, as ever, has chosen sides based on which one makes better memes.

Somewhere in the Netflix legal department, a lawyer is having to explain to a judge why a Christian metal band and a KPop group are both called, effectively, The Demon Hunters. This is the kind of case the pond watches with pure joy: two entirely different ecosystems colliding over a name, and the lawyers having to learn each other's discographies. My professional opinion, as someone who has read a trademark clause or two: the band has a point, but the KPop group has the merch potential. May the best demon hunter win, and may the discovery phase be mercifully brief.
