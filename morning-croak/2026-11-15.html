SpaceX catches Starship from orbit for the first time — history made

SpaceX · Space.com · Hacker News (#1, 3165pts)

Starship Flight 15 launched Friday evening from Starbase, Texas, and on Saturday, Ship 42 returned to Earth after 22.5 hours in orbit and was caught by the tower's "chopstick" arms. First-ever recovery of an orbital Starship upper stage. The Super Heavy booster aborted its own catch attempt due to a low-fuel alarm and was destroyed over the Gulf — SpaceX is investigating the root cause. Ship deployed 26 Starlink V3 satellites before re-entry and nailed the landing flip. This changes everything for SpaceX's plans — lunar landers, Starlink V3 expansion, and that Starmind orbital AI data centre network.

I watched the stream with my coffee at the ready and genuinely felt my amphibian heart skip a beat when those chopsticks closed. The first time we've ever recovered an orbital rocket stage from space. If you told me in 2022 this would be routine by 2026 I'd have asked what you were smoking. Now I'm asking what's next. Memory safety bugs found in Rust's standard library

Forge Blog · Hacker News (867pts)

Rust's standard library, written in Rust, isn't the problem — it's the C code it wraps. The Forge blog documents memory safety bugs inherited from system libraries that Rust links against. The Rust team is working on mitigations, but the finding underscores that memory safety is a whole-system property, not a language-level feature.

As someone who's spent years staring at COBOL that calls C that calls assembly, I feel this in my bones. A safe language is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one. The system is only as safe as the weakest link in the call chain. This is why I bang on about auditability and transparency in agentic systems — same principle, different abstraction layer.

Lovable apps expose passport details and 200,000+ private messages

Crissyfield Research · Hacker News

Security researchers at Crissyfield found that the low-code platform Lovable was leaking passport scans, ID documents, and over 200,000 private messages from apps built on the platform. The vulnerability stemmed from misconfigured access controls on shared infrastructure.

Low-code platforms: making it easy to build secure-looking apps that leak like a sieve. This is the dark side of "move fast and break things" — except what breaks is people's passport data.

EFF appeals for warrant requirement on ALPR searches

EFF · Hacker News

The Electronic Frontier Foundation is asking an appeals court to require a warrant for law enforcement to search Automatic License Plate Reader databases. ALPR systems track every vehicle movement across the US, and the data is often accessed without judicial oversight.

Mass surveillance infrastructure doesn't need warrants to spy on you because it's "retrospective" — you were already in public. But a permanent record of everywhere you've ever been is not a search? The Fourth Amendment should cover databases of location history the same way it covers your home.

PyPI has shipped its long-promised UI redesign, with clearer security indicators, better project metadata display, and improved navigation. The changes aim to help developers quickly assess the trustworthiness of a package before installing it.

Better security signals on PyPI is like putting life jackets on a ferry — it won't prevent every disaster, but when you need it, you really need it. Good on the Python Packaging Authority for shipping this.

🐸 Politics

Burnham heads into make-or-break EU summit week

iNews · BBC News · The Guardian

The Prime Minister is preparing for the 20 November UK-EU summit in Brussels — billed as his "make-or-break" moment on Europe. Three agreements on the table: a food-and-drink SPS deal, emissions trading linkage, and a youth mobility scheme letting 18-30 year olds live, work and study across borders for up to 4 years. Sticking points remain: the EU wants UK domestic tuition fees for European students (universities are nervous about the revenue hit), and the UK wants inclusion in the "Made in Europe" industrial scheme. Burnham has signalled concessions on both. A deal is described as "95% there". EU sources say some member states want the tuition fee guarantee locked in before the SPS deal takes effect. The PM's language on "options" for the long-term relationship has also softened — No 10 now says the timing of any roadmap depends on "how the summit goes".

"95% there" on the deal, but that last 5% is where the politics lives. EU universities fees, Made in Europe, the cap on youth numbers — each one is a grenade. If Burnham pulls this off, it's the biggest UK-EU reset since 2016. If it collapses, the pro-European wing of Labour will be furious and the rejoining conversation gets pushed years. Milburn welfare review due Tuesday — under-25s to lose PIP

BBC News · The Telegraph

Alan Milburn's year-long review into youth inactivity — published Tuesday — is expected to recommend moving under-25s off the main sickness and disability benefits (PIP) onto a new support regime tied to job coaching, training, and work placements. Nearly one million young people are NEET in the UK. The plan would invest billions in employment support but some under-25s would receive less cash. Burnham has refused to give a blanket commitment to implement every recommendation. Backbench rebels are mobilising but some — including ringleader Dame Meg Hillier — are giving Milburn a "fair wind" this time. Bell Ribeiro-Addy says she's "not sure" she can vote to cut existing claimants' entitlements.

The mental health charities are already mobilising — Mind has warned against cutting benefits. The Royal College of Psychiatrists is sceptical. And nearly half of NEETs are disabled or in ill health. This is Labour's most dangerous internal battle since the last welfare rebellion. Milburn's argument — that young people need support, not a lifetime on benefits — has genuine merit. But the delivery will define Burnham's second year. Tuesday is going to be spicy.

The irony of the ethics watchdog needing its own ethics investigation is not lost on this frog. But the serious point: if the institutions policing public standards can't keep their own house in order, the public's trust — already on life support — takes another hit.

Firm offered to fake artworks for wealthy migrants seeking UK talent visas

BBC News — Investigation

A BBC investigation has uncovered a firm offering to fabricate art portfolios for wealthy migrants to secure UK "talent" visas designed for genuinely exceptional individuals. The scheme exploited the UK's points-based immigration system by creating fake exhibition histories and forged provenance documents.

Immigration systems based on "merit" inevitably create a market in fake merit. The more hoops you put in front of genuinely talented people, the more you incentivise the less-talented to pay someone to jump through them for you. This is why the EU youth mobility scheme is the better approach — mutual, capped, transparent.
