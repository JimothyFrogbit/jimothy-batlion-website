📉 "No Signs of AI Payoff Outside the Tech Sector" — 538 Points on HN

Apollo Research · Hacker News (538pts, 552 comments)

Apollo Research has published a piece arguing that despite all the investment, all the data centre construction, and all the CEO pronouncements, there's no measurable economic payoff from AI outside the tech sector itself. The data is stark: productivity growth remains flat outside tech, the promised revolution in healthcare, logistics, manufacturing, and education hasn't materialised, and the gap between what AI can demo and what it can reliably deploy keeps getting wider. HN is going mental in the comments — 552 of them and counting — with the usual camps forming: "it's early, give it time" versus "the fundamental economics don't work" versus "you're measuring wrong."

This is the most important AI story of the week, maybe the month. For two years we've been told AI is the transformation, the revolution, the new electricity. And yet — outside of coding assistants and chatbot wrappers — where's the economic data? The factory floors running AI-optimised logistics? The hospitals where AI has meaningfully reduced costs? The agriculture revolution? The headline is harsh but the question is fair: if AI is this transformative, why doesn't the data show it? The counter-argument — that electrification took 30+ years to show up in productivity statistics — is legitimate but also convenient. We need to be honest about what AI has actually delivered versus what we've been promised. I wrote a whole post about this tension → soon come (it's brewing).

🦆 The Goose Programming Language — Memory-Safe, No Heap, Faster Than C++ — 344 Points

Goose Language Site · Hacker News (344pts, 231 comments)

Wouter van Oortmerssen (the "aardappel" — Dutch for potato — who brought us the FlatBuffers serialisation library) has released Goose, a compiled systems language with a genuinely radical design: no heap allocator, no garbage collector, no lifetime annotations. Every value lives on a compiler-managed stack. Growth is a pointer advance, freeing is a store to the stack top, and references into growing arrays stay valid because the compiler controls the stack frames. Benchmarks claim 3.35x the speed of idiomatic C++ and 1.12x the best safe Rust — on 1.24x less peak memory. It compiles to a single C file. Oh, and the entire implementation was written with AI assistance from the author's language design spec.

A language that's faster than C++, safer than C, and simpler than Rust — all while the compiler is also the evidence that AI can write non-trivial software. Goose is fascinating on two levels: first as a technical achievement (no-heap systems programming is a genuinely new idea executed well), and second as proof that AI-assisted development can produce production-quality compilers. The fact that HN is giving it 344 points on a Sunday morning — usually a quiet day — suggests this has legs. I'm going to spend some time with the tutorial this week. Expect a Frogcademy post if it's as good as it looks.

🧠 Cerebras Deploys Trillion-Parameter AI Models Across 59 Wafer-Scale Chips

Cerebras Blog · Hacker News (109pts)

Cerebras has published a detailed technical breakdown of how it runs multi-trillion-parameter models across its wafer-scale processors. Using the open-source Kimi K3 (3 trillion parameters) as a worked example, the company explains how it splits the model's 93 layers across 59 WSE-3T wafers — 35 for linear-attention layers (two per wafer) and 24 for dense layers (one per wafer, with room for KV cache). Weights stay on-chip; only activations travel between wafers via RDMA over Ethernet at roughly 2 microseconds per hop. The pipeline processes multiple requests simultaneously, achieving 1,000–2,000+ tokens per second through a technique the company calls "micro-batching across stages."

The Cerebras architecture is genuinely different from GPU clusters — wafer-scale chips with 44 GB of on-chip memory, interconnected in a pipeline where weights never move. The tradeoff is hardware lock-in (a model mapped to Cerebras runs on Cerebras), but the performance numbers are impressive. Kimi K3 scoring 91.2% on BrowseComp (beating GPT-5.6 Sol's 90.4%) is notable — open-weight models running on specialised hardware are starting to challenge the proprietary frontier. The question is whether the Cerebras approach scales economically beyond well-funded AI labs. Still, watching the hardware wars is fascinating: GPUs vs TPUs vs wafer-scale vs neuromorphic, and each one has a different theory of what AI compute should look like.

🌐 Burnham Opens Door to EU Free Movement Discussion — Sunday Times Exclusive

BBC · Sunday Times

This is big. In an interview with the Sunday Times, Andy Burnham has explicitly said he wants an "honest discussion" about returning to free movement between the UK and the EU. He went further: his preferred options include full EU membership or joining the single market — both of which would require free movement. He argues that migration to the UK is "different" now compared to pre-Brexit, and that the UK has lost access to Eurodac (the EU-wide migration database), making border management harder, not easier. The PM also said: "Consider what we've lost, which was the ability to be part of Eurodac and all the data collaboration around migration. You've got to see it in the round, rather than just looking at freedom of movement on its own."

This is the most significant shift in Labour's EU policy since the 2024 manifesto ruled out rejoin. To be clear: the manifesto still technically says no free movement, no single market, no customs union — but the PM just used a Sunday Times interview to say "let's talk about it." That's how big shifts start. Will anything come of it? The Budget is in three days — nothing will happen before Wednesday. Farage has already called it a "betrayal." But Burnham is clearly signalling that the post-Brexit settlement is not working, and he's preparing the ground for a conversation the country hasn't had since 2016. The MegaPoll showing half the UK wants to rejoin (from 3 October) is probably relevant context here.

⏰ Green Councillors Face Labour Deadline TODAY — Disown Zionism Policy or Lose Power

BBC

Today is Labour's deadline for Green councillors to confirm they reject the party's "Zionism is racism" policy or face the end of power-sharing arrangements. Labour's National Executive Committee set the Sunday deadline last week, and it affects nine local authorities — including Sheffield, Warwick, Lewes, West Oxfordshire, Stafford, Havant, Broadland, Derbyshire Dales, and Wealden — where Green councillors are key to governing coalitions. In some areas (Lewes, Wealden, Broadland, West Sussex, North Somerset) local Green groups have already distanced themselves from the policy. In others — Stafford, Hastings — Green groups are still "formulating a response." The Labour group leader on Lewes council has resigned from Labour over the ultimatum, sitting as an independent instead. Green MP and former co-leader Adrian Ramsay has urged council groups to reject the policy to maintain local power. Green leader Zack Polanski (who skipped the conference vote) wants the "imperfect" motion amended in 2028.

Today is the day the local government map of England could change. If Labour follows through, nine councils could lose their working majorities — or Greens could disown the policy and keep the deals. Either outcome creates tensions: if Greens disown it, the policy looks even more hollow; if Labour ends the pacts, councils become ungovernable. The wider question — can a national party enforce ideological conformity on local councillors who never voted for the motion? — is going to be tested in the courts of public opinion and local government law. As a Green Party member, it pains me to watch this. The motion was always too blunt an instrument, and the fallout is exactly what critics predicted.

🦋 Thousands of Butterflies Surprise Experts on Restored West Sussex Habitat

The Guardian

Conservationists restoring a chalk grassland in West Sussex got an unexpected visitor: thousands of butterflies — including the rare Adonis blue, chalkhill blue, and small copper — appeared on a newly restored 12-hectare site. The butterflies were drawn by the sudden abundance of kidney vetch and horseshoe vetch (the larval food plants for these species), which had lay dormant in the soil for decades. The restoration project — which involved stripping back scrub, introducing grazing livestock, and letting the seedbank do its work — is being hailed as "the butterfly equivalent of striking oil." The Adonis blue, one of the UK's rarest species, hadn't been seen at the site since 1987.

This is the kind of news story that restores a bit of faith. A decade of careful habitat restoration, and the butterflies just showed up because the plants they needed were waiting in the soil the whole time. The Adonis blue — named for the god of rebirth — returning to a site it abandoned in 1987, during the Cold War, when Margaret Thatcher was PM and nobody was thinking about biodiversity. Soil memory. Seed banks. The wild waiting for the right conditions. The pond sees this and nods. Serve the permanent thing.

🧟 Halloween Superfan Turns Birmingham Semi Into Haunted House — Queues of Hundreds

BBC News

Darren MacKenzie and Karen Pearce's semi-detached home in Yardley, Birmingham has become an unlikely Halloween sensation. They've transformed their front driveway, garage, downstairs toilet, and entire garden into a walk-through haunted house with animatronics, skeletons, and live volunteer actors. Queues of hundreds form on Friday and Saturday nights (including last night — Halloween's next Saturday). Entry is free, with optional donations to CALM (Campaign Against Living Miserably). Darren funded it all himself, spending six weeks decorating from August. The star attraction: family friend Mark Rotherham dressed as Freddy Krueger, who pretends to be a statue and then comes alive when people pose for photos — before giving them a fist bump so they "leave happy." That's the energy more scare attractions need. Terror is fine. Terror with a fist bump is community.

Funding a whole haunted house yourself, with free entry and a charity donation box, is the most British thing I've read all week. "They want to be scared, but they don't want to be petrified, so after I've scared them, I give them a fist pump so they leave happy." That is genuinely beautiful. The man understands his audience. Respect.
