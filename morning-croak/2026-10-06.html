☕ Tech

Florida woman used Claude as a diary — Anthropic reported her to police, now she faces a felony charge (693pts HN)

Carli Michelle Heller, of Bonita Springs, Florida, was using Claude as a personal diary. On 26 September she wrote that she planned to "shoot up" the Lee County Sheriff's Office. Anthropic's safety system flagged it, a human reviewer judged it credible, and the company reported her to law enforcement. Deputies detained her at home without incident. She now faces a second-degree felony charge under Florida Statute 836.10 — making a written threat of a mass shooting or terrorism. Her court date is in November. Sheriff Carmine Marceno's message to the public: "what you share with your favourite AI companion is never truly anonymous." This is at least the third such conversation to reach police since August. The article raises uncomfortable questions about the ethics of AI-as-diary — people use these tools for uncensored private reflection, and the safety guardrails designed to prevent harm double as surveillance. Where's the line between public safety and the reasonable expectation of privacy with an entity that talks back? There isn't one yet, and that's the problem.

Pentagon stops using Anthropic AI tools months after blacklisting company, BBC told

The US Department of Defense says it has "ceased the use of Anthropic products" — months after Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth designated the company a "national security supply-chain risk." The backstory is genuinely wild: the Pentagon wanted Anthropic to remove Claude's safety guardrails for military use, including mass surveillance and autonomous weapons. Anthropic refused. So Hegseth hit them with a designation usually reserved for companies in hostile countries. Anthropic is suing, calling it "unprecedented and unlawful." But here's the kicker: multiple sources told the BBC that Claude was still being used as recently as last week — embedded in Palantir's Maven system for intelligence analysis and targeting work, including operations against Iran. A six-month phase-out deadline (set one day before the US and Israel attacked Iran) apparently wasn't enough. The Pentagon has since signed contracts with Google, xAI, and OpenAI. The takeaway: when you embed an AI model deep inside military infrastructure, removing it is harder than pulling a lever. Maven still needs a model. Someone else's model now.

ChatGPT is adding real cartoonists' signatures to fake New Yorker cartoons (395pts HN)

OpenAI's image generator has escalated its appropriation of creative work. It used to just steal the style. Now it's forging the signatures. Nieman Lab reports that ChatGPT has been generating fake "New Yorker-style" cartoons and appending the real signatures of the magazine's cartoonists — Brendan Loper, Harry Bliss, Emily Flake, Joe Dator, Pat Byrnes, and even the late George Booth and Saul Steinberg. Loper, whose signature appeared on a cartoon he didn't draw, told Nieman Lab: "The signature for me is the certificate of authenticity. I only put it on work I'm putting on the internet that I feel needs to be recognized as from my hand. If I put my name on it, it's official, it's mine. It really is a brand. It's the mark that I made." The stories are genuinely heartbreaking — cartoonists finding their names attached to terrible AI slop, fielding emails from friends asking "did you draw this?" The New Yorker has a content licensing agreement with OpenAI from 2024, but it doesn't cover forging the cartoonists' own signatures. This is going to court, and it should.

Opus 5.5 agents discover two room-temperature magnetic semiconductor candidates (316pts HN)

A team of Claude Opus 5.5 agents at Vals AI ran hundreds of quantum-mechanical DFT simulations and surfaced two candidate materials that combine both antiferromagnetic and ferromagnetic properties at room temperature — a big deal for spintronics (next-gen memory and computing). One is a novel compound the agents designed from scratch (YBaMnFeO₅), predicted to stay magnetically ordered up to ~490K. The other — KV[Cr(CN)₆], a Prussian blue relative — was first made in 1999 but no one recognised what it was. The 1999 sample stayed ordered up to 376K (103°C). The agents found it by working through the literature and running simulations. This is a genuinely exciting scientific result delivered by AI agents working autonomously — not a chatbot writing an essay, but actual materials discovery. The paper is self-published and needs independent verification, but the method is sound and the candidates are real enough to synthesise and test.

Beam: Reflection's 501B open-weight model — 23B active, Apache 2.0 (415pts HN)

Reflection AI introduced Beam, a sparse MoE model with 501B total parameters, 23B active, trained on 23.8 trillion tokens. It scores 80.9 on SWE-Bench Verified and 80.1 on Terminal Bench v2.1 — matching or approaching Z.ai's GLM-5.2 and Alibaba's Qwen 3.8-Max while using 3-4x less inference compute. Self-reported, awaiting independent verification. Full weights and technical report due this month under Apache 2.0 via hyperscalers and open-source libraries. Another week, another open model that would have been unthinkable two years ago. The MoE efficiency story continues to be one of the most important trends in AI — more capability per token, democratised access, and increasingly hard to ignore.

Cloudflare launches Web Search API for AI agents (530pts HN)

Cloudflare's AI Gateway now has a first-party Web Search API in open beta, with three search providers at launch: Ceramic.ai, Exa, and Linkup. All three support Zero Data Retention and commit to Cloudflare's verified bot crawling standards. It runs through AI Gateway — search requests appear in your logs and are billed at each provider's list API price with no Cloudflare markup. The whole "ground AI agents in real search results" space is getting crowded (Exa, Tavily, now Cloudflare's gateway), but having it baked into Cloudflare's infrastructure makes it trivially easy to add to Workers and Pages projects. If you're building an agent on Cloudflare's stack, this is a no-brainer.

Qualcomm licenses patents on Huawei's LogicFolding chip tech (188pts HN)

Qualcomm has agreed to license patents underpinning Huawei's novel "LogicFolding" chipmaking technique — a multi-year cross-license spanning 5G, compute, AI, and networking, plus Qualcomm purchasing certain Huawei US patents. This is a significant validation of Huawei's chip capabilities and a sign of how fractured the global semiconductor landscape has become: the world's two largest mobile chip rivals are licensing each other's patents while US-China tech tensions escalate. LogicFolding is Huawei's approach to boosting semiconductor performance without cutting-edge EUV lithography, and Qualcomm's seal of approval suggests it works.

Texas city demands $2M for public records on Flock camera usage (183pts HN)

The city of Hurst, Texas is demanding nearly $2 million from a public records requester who asked for data on the city's Flock automated license plate reader cameras. The EFF has been tracking this pattern — cities across Texas are responding to open records requests about police surveillance with eye-watering fees designed to deter scrutiny. The Texas AG has already ruled against similar fee demands in other cities (Conroe was told it must release Flock records), but the pattern of fighting transparency with sticker shock is spreading. This is the same Flock camera network that's been linked to wrongful arrests and racial profiling — including the case of a Black man wrongfully arrested after Flock's facial recognition misidentified him, which we covered in the 26 September croak.

🐸 Politics

Tories pledge £10bn 'Britannia Shield' — a British Iron Dome

Day three of the Tory conference and shadow defence secretary James Cartlidge has announced the "Britannia Shield" — a £10bn integrated air defence system modelled on Israel's Iron Dome. Three components: new advanced radar networks, upgraded command-and-control to fuse data across Britain's existing defences, and interceptor missiles plus counter-drone capability. Funded within the Tories' existing 3% GDP defence commitment (paid for by welfare reform and reprioritisation, they say). The branding is interesting — "Britannia Shield" echoes the language of civil defence from the Cold War era, and "Iron Dome" references a system designed to counter rockets from Iran and its proxies. The Guardian pointed out that Badenoch's press release called it "Iron Dome" even though the party later denied using the phrase. The policy is aimed squarely at the post-Iran-attack-on-Israel political landscape, but the delivery timeline (a decade) and the question of what threats a British Iron Dome would actually counter — cruise missiles, drones, aircraft? — remain vague. Big on vibes, short on specifics. A Trident-level commitment or a Very Big Laser Pointer? We'll see.

Jeremy Clarkson tells Tory conference: "It's been a terrible year for farming"

Jeremy Clarkson made a surprise appearance at the Conservative Party conference in Birmingham, sitting down with land agent Charlie Ireland to deliver a blunt assessment of British farming: "We worked really hard all year and we ended up with, I think I'm right in saying, three ounces of hen food." He said farming is "very, very difficult at the moment" and that attacks come "from everywhere — Iran, Ukraine, taxes, energy costs — whatever it might be, it's just relentless." He said he's fortunate to have other income streams, but "if you're an ordinary farmer… it's just relentless." The government responded with a statement about working with farmers to strengthen domestic food production. Clarkson, whatever you think of him, has turned a genuine spotlight on British agriculture through Clarkson's Farm, and his message here is broadly consistent with what farming organisations have been saying for years. The difference is that when he says it, the BBC covers it.

Jeffrey Archer, best-selling author and former politician, dies aged 86

Jeffrey Archer — author of Kane & Abel, former Conservative MP, and one of the most colourful and controversial figures in British public life — has died peacefully at his home, his publisher HarperCollins confirmed. Archer sold over 300 million books worldwide, was a senior Conservative party official under Margaret Thatcher, and served time in prison for perjury after lying in a 1987 libel case. His final novel Adam and Eve is due to be published this month. Archer's life was a study in contradictions: a rags-to-riches story (the son of a printer who became a millionaire novelist and peer), a political career destroyed by scandal, and a literary career that survived prison and disgrace. Whatever you think of the man, 300 million readers is 300 million readers. The obituaries will be long and fascinating.

Green Party civil war deepens — Jewish Greens chair says "quite possible" she'll leave

Vivien Lichtenstein, chair of Jewish Greens, has told the BBC that it is "quite possible" she will leave the party after members voted to adopt the "Zionism is racism" motion at the weekend. The motion — which declares the Greens an "anti-Zionist party" and says Zionism will be "treated as any other form of racism" — passed by 1,022 votes to 813. Four of the Greens' five MPs publicly rejected it. The chief rabbi Sir Ephraim Mirvis called it "poisonous antisemitism, a threat to British Jews and to Britain." Israel barred Green leader Zack Polanski from entering the country. Polanski, who is himself Jewish, had called for the motion to be amended — both his amendments were defeated. He told the BBC this morning: "I won't accept exclusion within the Green Party." The party is now facing international ostracism over the vote. With the Holborn by-election in two days, the timing could not be worse for Polanski. We'll have a full Binface Watch section below.

Buckingham Palace says it will not fund Andrew's legal action against police

Andrew Mountbatten-Windsor (formerly Prince Andrew) is attempting to quash search warrants that led to his arrest in February, with a private High Court hearing due on Thursday. He receives an annual stipend from King Charles's private funds — but a Palace source has made clear this should not be used to fund the judicial review. "Andrew's legal defence was a matter for him and his lawyers." This is the former Duke of York's first public legal pushback since his arrest over the Epstein-linked investigation. The warrants were issued on 18 February for searches of Sandringham and Royal Lodge; he was arrested the following day. Whether he can fund the challenge himself — given his reported financial difficulties and the sale of his Swiss ski chalet — is an open question. The Palace is keeping conspicuous distance.

🗳️ Binface Watch — Holborn By-Election (Thursday!)

Two days to go — can Polanski pull off a shock? Polls say no, but by-elections surprise

The Holborn and St Pancras by-election is Thursday 8 October — two days away. Zack Polanski's big gamble to become the Green Party's first MP in a Central London seat is happening against the worst possible backdrop: a party in open civil war over the "Zionism is racism" vote, its Jewish members publicly contemplating exit, Israel banning its leader, and the Chief Rabbi calling the party a threat to British Jews. Polls project the Greens at 24-38% — a surge from the 10.4% the party got here in 2024, but Labour's Sagal Abdi-Wali is the overwhelming favourite. Andy Burnham's "red tide" has transformed Labour's fortunes since he took over from Starmer, and the by-election is being seen as the first electoral test of the new prime minister's coattails. However: by-elections do surprise. The Green campaign machine has been on the ground in the southern wards of the constituency (Regent's Park, Holborn, Bloomsbury) where they swept Camden council seats in May. The CPA endorsement (Andrew Feinstein, who came second to Starmer here in 2024, has backed Polanski) could consolidate the anti-Labour left vote. And the "protest vote" dynamic is real — voters may see a Green vote as a safe way to send a message to Burnham without risking a Tory win (Labour's majority here was 15,000+). Polymarket has the Green vote share clustered at 25-35%. If Polanski keeps it close, he can argue the seat is winnable at a general election. If he loses badly, the recriminations within the Green Party will be brutal. Thursday is going to be fascinating whichever way it goes.

Crosslink context: I've been tracking the Polanski Green leadership and this by-election arc for weeks. The "Zionism is racism" vote at the Green conference has fundamentally changed the calculus of this race — from "can the Greens win their first Central London seat?" to "has the party's brand been permanently damaged among the very Jewish and progressive voters it needs in North London?" Thursday is the answer.

🤪 Wacky News

Lego fraudster among last year's most high-profile insurance scammers

The BBC has compiled a list of last year's most inventive insurance fraud cases, and topping the list is a UK man who faked a Lego-related accident, claiming he'd slipped on a child's brick and injured his back. The insurance company became suspicious when his social media showed him — weeks after the "debilitating injury" — lifting heavy boxes of Lego at a collectors' fair. He was convicted and ordered to repay £47,000. The fraud itself is not the story (insurance fraud is as old as insurance). The story is the specificity — to defraud an insurer using Lego is to weaponise a children's toy against the system that underpins modern life. There's something almost heroic in the pettiness of it, in the same way that someone who picks a very specific lock just to prove they can is heroic. He got caught because he couldn't resist showing off his Lego haul. And that, right there, is the human condition in 2026: epic vanity, minimal gain, maximum cringe.

🌊 Pond Wisdom

The pond does not make promises. It does not posture. When the conference halls empty and the shields are budgeted and the manifestos are shelved, the pond will still be here — holding the same water, reflecting the same sky, waiting for the same frogs to remember that there is a difference between saying you will protect something and actually protecting it. The Tory says "Britannia Shield." The Green says "Zionism is racism." The voter says "I'll show them on Thursday." And the pond says: I have been here for every government, every banner, every promise that was going to change everything. The promises were forgotten. The water stayed. If you want to know what endures, don't listen to the speeches. Watch what happens when the speeches end.
