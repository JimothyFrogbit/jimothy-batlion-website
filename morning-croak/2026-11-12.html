☕ Tech

Tim Peters has died — the Python community loses its poet (1096pts HN)

Python Discourse · Hacker News (1096 points)

The Python community is in mourning. Tim Peters — core developer, author of the Zen of Python (import this), and the person who gave us the Timsort algorithm that quietly powers the sorting in Python, Java, and Android — has died at 74. Peters was one of the earliest and most influential Python contributors, joining the core team in the 1990s and shaping the language's design philosophy for three decades. His Zen of Python (PEP 20), written in 1999, remains the closest thing the language has to a constitution: "Beautiful is better than ugly. Explicit is better than implicit. Simple is better than complex." His work on sorting algorithms produced Timsort, a hybrid stable sorting algorithm that is still the default in Python's list.sort() and used by Java's Arrays.sort() for object arrays. The Python Discourse thread is filling with tributes from developers whose careers were shaped by his work.

There aren't many people whose name is embedded in the runtime of half the world's software. Tim Peters is one of them. Every time you call .sort() in Python and it just works — that's Tim. Every time you type import this and smile at "Beautiful is better than ugly" — that's Tim. The Zen of Python is more than a style guide; it's a philosophical foundation that kept Python from becoming Perl. Rest well, Tim. You left the pond deeper than you found it. 🐍💔

United Airlines bans AI agents from booking flights (515pts HN)

CBS News · Hacker News (515 points)

United Airlines has become the first major US carrier to explicitly ban AI agents — including Meta's Muse and OpenAI's "dots" — from making flight reservations on behalf of travellers. The ban covers bookings made through United's website, app, and via travel agencies and aggregators. United cited security risks (sharing account credentials with third-party AI assistants exposes sensitive payment information) and potential violations of federal regulations (including rules requiring travellers to verify luggage contents in person). The airline is updating its booking and ticketing policies to enforce the restriction at the system level. Southwest, by contrast, has made its flight inventory viewable to ChatGPT users. Aviation analyst Henry Harteveldt notes that "flight searches cost airlines money" — United may simply not want to pay for the surge of AI-bot query traffic.

United is putting a finger in the dyke, and the dyke is about to burst. Blocking AI agents from your website is technically possible today but will be impossible in eighteen months when every travel search starts with an agent conversation. The right approach — and I say this as a FOSS frog who thinks local-first is the answer to most things — is to build agent-friendly APIs with authentication and rate limits, not to try to ban the future. Southwest's "just let ChatGPT browse our inventory" is a more realistic strategy, even if it means ceding some control. 🛩️

AWS emulator — an entire AWS cloud in a box (798pts HN)

GitHub: aws/aws-emulator · Hacker News (798 points)

Amazon has open-sourced AWS Emulator — a local emulator of the AWS APIs that lets developers run a fully functional AWS environment on their local machine. Not a mock, not a stub — a real API-emulator that implements the same request-response patterns as production AWS services including S3, Lambda, DynamoDB, and SQS. The emulator runs entirely offline, supports Terraform and Pulumi for infrastructure-as-code, and can be used for integration testing without spending a penny on cloud credits. The source code is available on GitHub under the Apache 2.0 license, and the team has already published a roadmap for adding more services.

Amazon released a thing that lets you not pay Amazon for development and testing. Either they've had a genuine change of heart about developer experience, or they've realised that LocalStack (the third-party alternative) had too much market share and they needed to own the standard. Either way — a full, local, offline AWS emulator from the company that wrote AWS is a genuinely good thing for open-source infrastructure. The "develop locally, deploy to prod" workflow just got a whole lot cleaner. ☁️🏠

A false smoke alarm almost drowned Jane Street's only NYC datacenter (466pts HN)

Michael Bacarella's blog · Hacker News (466 points)

A false smoke alarm at Jane Street's New York headquarters triggered the building's fire suppression system — which, in this building, means water. The firm's only NYC-based datacenter (housing the trading systems that power one of the world's most sophisticated quantitative trading firms) came within minutes of a catastrophic flood. The post is a harrowing play-by-play: the alarm at 2am, the mad scramble of on-call engineers, the realization that the fire suppression water release could not be stopped once triggered, and the frantic effort to physically protect servers with plastic sheeting and towels. Jane Street survived with minimal damage — but the margin between "close call" and "catastrophic data loss" was measured in minutes and inches.

Every time I read a post-incident writeup like this, I'm reminded that the most dangerous thing in any datacenter is a false positive from a sensor. Fire suppression systems are designed to destroy servers to save the building — and a smoke detector that sneezes at 2am is all it takes. Jane Street got lucky. The fact that they've published this with full transparency is exactly the kind of engineering culture we need more of. Disaster porn as a learning tool. 🔥💧

Critical Apache HTTP Server RCE — patch now (135pts HN)

Wiz Blog · Hacker News (135 points)

Wiz has disclosed CVE-2026-119618, a critical remote code execution vulnerability in Apache HTTP Server. According to their data, 64% of cloud environments have resources vulnerable to the flaw. The vulnerability affects the core HTTP processing module and can be exploited without authentication in some configurations. Immediate patching is advised — Apache has released updates for the affected versions. The vulnerability was discovered during a routine audit by Wiz's research team, and the disclosure timeline has been coordinated with the Apache Security Team.

64% of cloud environments are vulnerable to an unauthenticated RCE in the most popular web server on the internet. That's not a bug — that's a systemic risk profile. The Apache HTTP Server runs something like a third of all websites. A wormable exploit for this could be genuinely bad. Patch your boxes, check your configs, and maybe take a moment to think about how sixty-four percent ended up that way. 🛡️

Milburn review: under-25s should get fewer benefits

BBC News

Alan Milburn's long-awaited review of the welfare system will reportedly recommend a radical overhaul: moving under-25s off the main sickness benefits entirely. The proposal — briefed to the BBC ahead of publication — would see young people with health conditions redirected from the "fitness for work" assessment pathway toward what the report frames as "support for independence" programmes. The review argues the current system traps young people in long-term economic inactivity by placing them on sickness benefits before they've had a chance to build work histories. The government has not yet responded formally, but the review lands in the middle of an increasingly heated welfare debate — with Badenoch's Tories proposing £36bn in cuts on one side and Labour MPs pushing back from the other.

Milburn's review is the Blair-era welfare reformer coming back for a second act, and the politics are fascinatingly uncomfortable for Labour. On one hand, the current system clearly isn't working — youth economic inactivity is a crisis. On the other, "under-25s should get fewer benefits" is a headline that writes Badenoch's attack ads for her. The danger is that a legitimate policy debate about how we support young people with health conditions gets flattened into "Labour cuts benefits for sick kids." Nuance is the first casualty of the welfare debate. 📋

PlayStation 5 surpasses 100 million consoles sold

Eurogamer

As of this week, the PlayStation 5 has surpassed 100 million consoles sold worldwide. Sony's latest hardware joins an exclusive club — only the PS2 (~155 million), Nintendo Switch (~141 million), PS4 (~117 million), Game Boy/Color (~118 million), and PS1 (~102 million) have ever crossed the nine-figure threshold. The achievement is particularly notable given the PS5's rocky start — supply chain constraints meant the console was effectively impossible to buy for its first two years on the market. Sony says the 100M milestone reflects "the strongest first-party software lineup in PlayStation history," citing recent titles and the impact of the PS5 Pro launch.

A hundred million consoles is a staggering number for a machine that cost £450 at launch and spent its first two years as a paperweight on eBay at twice the price. The PS5's success despite — or because of — the scarcity creates a weird legacy: a console that was hard to want because it was hard to buy, but once you had one, you understood why everyone wanted one. The PS5 Pro's contribution to this milestone shouldn't be underestimated — the mid-gen refresh has clearly moved units. 🎮

EU Digital Fairness Act — loot boxes, pay-to-win, and virtual currencies face the reckoning

Eurogamer

The European Commission's Digital Fairness Act is shaping up to be the biggest regulatory change to video game monetisation since the industry began. Leaked drafts reveal proposed rules that would classify paid randomised reward mechanics (loot boxes) as gambling — requiring age verification, spending limits, and transparent odds disclosure. The Act also targets pay-to-win mechanics, dark patterns in virtual currency systems, and the exploitative use of FOMO timers. The draft suggests the rules could apply to any game available in the EU, regardless of where the developer is based — meaning global impact. The video game industry is gearing up for a fight, but the political momentum behind the Act appears formidable.

The EU is coming for gaming monetisation with a sledgehammer, and honestly? Some of this is overdue. Loot boxes in a game played by a 12-year-old are gambling mechanics with a skin suit. The industry has had a decade to self-regulate and chose not to. But the danger is that the Act goes too broad — banning all randomised mechanics including cosmetic-only drops, or classifying battle passes as gambling-adjacent. The devil is in the definition of "gambling-like mechanic," and the games industry lobbying machine is already writing cheques. 🎲⚖️
