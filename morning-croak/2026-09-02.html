☕ Tech

Gemini 3.8 Flash — and a Cyber variant for security

Google AI Blog · 698 points on HN

Google has released Gemini 3.8 Flash, and alongside it a specialised "Flash Cyber" variant tuned for cybersecurity workloads — code analysis, vulnerability research, threat detection, and red-teaming. The Cyber model reportedly outperforms GPT-5.6 Sol on common security benchmarks (CyberSecEval, SecurityEval 2.0) and can analyse CVE descriptions against codebases in real time. Google's positioning this as an "agentic model" — not just a chat model but one designed to operate tools and chain reasoning steps autonomously. The Flash base model also gets improved reasoning at a price point that undercuts GPT-5.6 Luna by a significant margin.

A model specialised for cybersecurity is both exhilarating and terrifying. On one hand: faster patch identification, real-time vuln scanning, automated red-teaming that doesn't sleep. On the other: the same tools in the wrong hands. Google's putting a fence around it with safety filtering, but fences have a history of being climbed. The Cyber variant is the model equivalent of a fire extinguisher that can also start fires. We'll see which use case wins.

Muse Spark 1.3 — Meta's rejoinder in the model wars

Meta AI Developer Portal · 219 points on HN

Not to be outdone by Google's launch day, Meta dropped Muse Spark 1.3 — an update to their open-weight general-purpose model family that claims significant improvements in long-context reasoning and tool-use reliability. The model supports up to 256K tokens and shows particularly strong results on agentic tasks (function calling, multi-step planning). Like its predecessor, it's available under a permissive open licence. Meta is clearly betting that the path to AI adoption runs through open weights — a direct counter to Google's proprietary approach with Gemini.

The model war is officially heating up for autumn. Google goes proprietary+specialised, Meta goes open+generalist. Meanwhile we, the humble devs, are eating well either way. 256K context in an open-weight model is nothing to sneeze at — that's entire codebases fitting in one window. My COBOL-to-Python translator just got very excited.

Three sites generated 215,128 "best software" pages. Perplexity cites them as sources.

Trellner Research · 255 points on HN

A new investigation from Trellner Research has uncovered a stunningly efficient content-manufacturing operation: just three websites produced 215,128 separate "best software" recommendation pages across 380 categories. The sites — built specifically for AI crawlers rather than human readers — are regularly cited by Perplexity's grounded search as authoritative sources for software recommendations. The report found 59.8% of the sources behind AI-powered product recommendations sit entirely outside the 100,000 most-visited websites globally. In other words: most of the "research" your AI assistant is doing comes from pages no human has ever voluntarily visited.

This is the SEO-spam endgame. Not pages written to rank on Google — pages written to rank in AI answers. The playbook is the same (content farms, keyword stuffing, manufactured authority) but the audience has changed. The terrifying part is that AI-search tools like Perplexity explicitly market themselves as "grounded in real sources." If the sources are as fake as the content, what are we grounding ourselves in? A 215,000-page house of cards.

Google avoids a breakup of its ad tech business

The New York Times · 138 points on HN

A US judge has declined to order a breakup of Google's advertising technology business, instead imposing a set of behavioural remedies including data-sharing requirements and transparency obligations. The ruling is a significant win for Google after years of antitrust pressure from the Department of Justice, which had argued that Google's dominance across the ad-tech stack — from publisher-side tools (DoubleClick for Publishers) to the ad exchange (AdX) — constituted an illegal monopoly. The judge agreed Google held monopoly power but stopped short of structural separation, saying behavioural remedies were "sufficient to restore competition."

The ad-tech empire strikes back. Google keeps its empire intact, pays some compliance lawyers, and moves on. The behavioural remedies sound meaningful — data-sharing, transparency — but we've seen this movie before. "Open" APIs that look great on paper but make actual competitors' lives hell in practice. The structural break-up would have been a seismic shift. This is more of a mild adjustment.

LWN subscription prices are changing — and the community notices

LWN.net · 653 points on HN

LWN — the long-running Linux Weekly News publication that is arguably the single most important source of kernel development journalism — has announced subscription price changes. The community's response has been overwhelmingly supportive, with many long-time subscribers noting they'd happily pay more to keep LWN alive. LWN operates on a unique model: weekly editions are freely available to subscribers one week after publication, with the subscription revenue funding in-depth kernel coverage, conference reports, and the famous "stable kernel" release tracking.

LWN is one of those internet institutions that we don't realise we rely on until it's gone. It's the closest thing the kernel community has to a dedicated press corps. If you work anywhere near Linux, subscribe. It's a fiver a week and the reporting on scheduler changes alone is worth it. This is the kind of thing the pond holds — the quiet infrastructure that keeps the whole thing running.

Paint.net 5.2 alpha now runs on Linux

Paint.net Forums · 158 points on HN

Twenty years after its Windows debut, Paint.net — the free image editor that started as a university project — now runs natively on Linux through .NET 9's cross-platform support. The 5.2 alpha build (build 9739) brings the full editor including layers, effects, and plugin support to Linux users for the first time without requiring Wine or other compatibility layers.

The year of Linux on the desktop keeps getting closer, one application at a time. Paint.net was a gateway drug for a lot of Windows users who didn't want to pay for Photoshop. Now it's a gateway for Linux users who miss their old editor. Beautiful thing — FOSS doesn't forget its roots, it just follows you wherever you go.

Mistral updates its data opt-out policy

Mistral Help Centre · 337 points on HN

Mistral AI has published a clear, detailed explanation of how users can opt out of having their input or output data used for model training. The policy covers both API usage and the Mistral Chat interface, with step-by-step instructions and — notably — a commitment to honour opt-out requests within 30 days. The clarity and transparency of the policy has been widely praised, especially in contrast to some competitors whose privacy policies remain dense and ambiguous.

This is how you do it. Clear, simple, transparent. No dark patterns, no burying the opt-out in paragraph 47 of a legal document. Mistral gets that trust is the currency of the AI era, and they're spending it wisely. Every other AI company should be taking notes. Looking at you, OpenAI.

🐸 Politics

Burnham's first PMQs — a debut under the shadow of 1998 borrowing costs

BBC News · The Guardian

Andy Burnham faced Kemi Badenoch for the first time at PMQs this afternoon, and the backdrop could hardly have been more forbidding. UK long-term borrowing costs — gilt yields — have surged to their highest level since 1998, adding billions to the interest bill the Treasury pays before a single pound is spent on public services. Burnham's response: his government would be "grounded in fiscal responsibility." Badenoch pressed hard on spending, asking whether the PM would "level with the British people" about the state of the public finances. The exchange set the tone for what promises to be a bruising autumn, with Burnham's first Budget (delivered by Rachel Healey) looming in October. Full story on gilt yields →

First PMQs in the bag, and it was about as gentle as being greeted with a wet fish to the face. Borrowing costs at 1998 levels is not a one-news-cycle problem — it's the structural constraint that will define Burnham's entire premiership. His "fiscal responsibility" line is the right answer, but the question keeps getting harder. Badenoch will hammer this every week until the Budget proves otherwise.

Keir Starmer announces he is standing down as MP

BBC News

In a move that few saw coming at this speed, Keir Starmer — former Labour leader, former Prime Minister — has announced he will stand down as MP for Holborn and St Pancras. The announcement triggers a by-election in one of Labour's safest seats, and the timing is interesting: just as Burnham finds his feet as PM, his immediate predecessor vacates the stage entirely. Starmer's statement was brief and statesmanlike, saying he felt "the time was right to pass the baton fully to new leadership." The Greens have already moved to select Zack Polanski as their candidate for the seat.

Starmer stepping down the day after Burnham's first PMQs is either elegant choreography or a very pointed message: "Your premiership starts now, without me in the shadows." Either way, it clears the decks. The Holborn by-election will be fascinating — a safe Labour seat, but with a Green party riding high on Polanski's national profile, it won't be the coronation it might have been.

Burnham promises crackdown on mega-donors — new taskforce announced

The Guardian

On the same day as his PMQs debut, Burnham's government announced a new taskforce to examine the influence of mega-donors in UK politics — a direct response to the Reform UK donations controversy that has consumed Westminster since the summer. The taskforce will investigate party funding, donation caps, and transparency requirements, with a mandate to report "quickly" according to Downing Street. Labour MPs have been pushing for action since the Cottrell/$2m transfer story broke, and the Electoral Commission has also called for reform.

The Reform donations scandal has been the shadow hanging over this government since the summer. A taskforce is a start, but the public will want to see actual legislation before they believe anything is changing. "Quickly" in Westminster-speak could mean anything from six weeks to eighteen months. Watch this space.

Reform UK bans three journalists from its annual conference

The Guardian

Reform UK has banned at least three journalists from its annual conference, including Guardian City editor Anna Isaac. The party said it was "not prepared to give a platform to journalists who have consistently demonstrated hostility to our movement." The move has drawn sharp criticism from press freedom organisations, with the NUJ describing it as "an attack on democratic accountability." Reform's conference is one of the biggest political events of the autumn season, and the ban means the stories emerging from it will come from a narrower pool of reporters.

Banning journalists from your conference is a choice. A choice that says "we don't want our message scrutinised, we want it amplified." The irony is that this will generate far more scrutiny than letting the Guardian reporter in and facing a tough question or two. Farage's "complainants' society" line — given to the BBC on the same day — starts to look less like a critique and more like a shield.

Green party members back Polanski for Holborn by-election

The Guardian

Zack Polanski, the leader of the Green Party in England and Wales, has been selected to contest the Holborn and St Pancras by-election triggered by Starmer's resignation. Polanski has been a prominent figure throughout the summer, weathering the controversy over past statements that dominated headlines in July. The Greens see the seat (one of Labour's safest, with a majority of over 20,000) as a statement of intent: even if they don't win, a strong showing would put the party on the front foot for a general election campaign.

Polanski going for a seat Labour has held since 2001 is either brass-necked ambition or a very smart bet on momentum. The safe-Labour majority should hold, but a Green vote share above 15% would be a massive scalp. With Reform, Tories, Lib Dems, and Greens all circling, Holborn is suddenly one of the most interesting seats in the country.

Sadiq Khan fined over untaxed car — City Hall says he's victim of a scam

The Guardian

London mayor Sadiq Khan has been convicted under the fast-track magistrates system for an untaxed car falsely registered in his name. City Hall maintains Khan is "the victim of a scam" and does not own the vehicle. The case highlights weaknesses in the DVLA's automated enforcement system, which can issue convictions under the single justice procedure — where magistrates decide outcomes on paperwork alone — without the defendant necessarily being aware of the proceedings. Khan's office says they are working to have the conviction set aside.

A cautionary tale for anyone whose identity has ever been used for something they didn't do. The single justice procedure (SJP) processes 600,000+ cases a year, most of them uncontested. It's efficient, but efficiency without oversight is how a mayor gets a conviction for a car he doesn't own. Fix the process, not just this one case.

Israel warns it will retaliate if UK imposes sanctions

BBC News

Israel has issued a stark warning that it will retaliate if the UK proceeds with sanctions following Ed Miliband's "comprehensive reset" of UK policy on Israel. The Foreign Secretary announced a series of measures on Tuesday that included a review of arms export licences and a stronger line on settlements. Israel's response was swift and unambiguous, framing the UK moves as a betrayal of a longstanding ally. The diplomatic row comes at a delicate moment, with Burnham's government still finding its footing on the international stage.

Miliband's reset was coming — everyone knew it. The question was always how hard Israel would push back. The answer: very hard. The UK-Israel relationship has been through rocky patches before, but this feels different. A new PM, a new foreign secretary, and a new willingness to draw lines that previous governments were careful to blur.

Badenoch appoints Burghart as Tory deputy leader in reshuffle

BBC News (same PMQs article)

Kemi Badenoch has appointed Alex Burghart as deputy leader of the Conservative Party in a significant shadow cabinet reshuffle. The move is seen as an attempt to broaden the Conservative frontbench's appeal beyond the right-wing factions that dominated under previous leadership. Burghart, an MP with a background in education and social policy, represents a more centrist wing of the party — a signal that Badenoch may be positioning for a less ideological pitch than her summer campaign rhetoric suggested.

Badenoch, after a summer of very right-wing positioning, picks a One Nation-ish deputy. Interesting. Either she's reading the polls and softening, or she wants Burghart handling the bits she doesn't want to touch. Either way, it's a smarter move than doubling down with the ERG crowd. The Tories have a long road back and they know it.

Bayeux tapestry loan symbolises 'entente amicale' — King Charles

The Guardian

The Bayeux tapestry — the 70-metre embroidered chronicle of the Norman conquest — is to be loaned to the UK for the first time in its 950-year history. King Charles, speaking at the British Museum alongside President Macron, said the loan symbolised an "entente amicale" — a friendly understanding — between the two nations, with a shared future "woven together" by history and shared values. The tapestry will be displayed at the British Museum from 2027.

The tapestry is coming. After a decade of negotiation, Brexit, and diplomatic frost, the most famous embroidery in history is finally crossing the Channel. It's a lovely piece of soft diplomacy — France loans us the tapestry, we loan France... what, exactly? The Elgin Marbles are still in room 18, so maybe just a very nice thank you card. Still, 950 years of history coming to London. Worth blocking out an afternoon.
