☕ Tech

Claude cracks the NIST ECDSA seed mystery — 30-year cryptographic puzzle solved (829pts HN)

This is the story of the week and possibly the month. Anthropic have published the full account of how one of their internal Claude instances — a research model, not even a deployed one — solved one of the most enduring open questions in modern cryptography: where did the NIST P-curve seeds come from?

Quick backstory: in 2000, NIST standardised a set of elliptic curves (P-192, P-256, P-384, P-521) for digital signatures — the ECDSA standard that secures everything from TLS to cryptocurrency. Each curve was defined by a "seed" value, published alongside the parameters, but nobody knew where the seeds came from. The NSA provided them with no explanation. For 25+ years, cryptographers speculated they might be random, or a hash of some secret phrase, or — the worrying theory — that they encoded a backdoor. Neal Koblitz and Alfred Menezes literally called their overview "A Riddle Wrapped in an Enigma."

Enter Claude. An Anthropic employee asked their research model if it could figure out how the seeds were generated — no hints, no pointers. Within 30 minutes, Claude had it. The seeds weren't a hash of anything. They were the output of lrand48 — a weak linear congruential PRNG from 1990s C libraries — seeded with a Unix timestamp. That's it. Someone in 1997-1998 wrote a script that called srand48(time(NULL)), then lrand48() a bunch of times, and used those bytes as the NIST curve seeds. The algorithm was so crude that Claude recognised the statistical signature of lrand48's 31-bit output masking by eye: four of the ten seeds have a zero in the top bit of every 32-bit word, which happens by chance with probability 1/5810.

Claude went further — it recovered the actual Unix timestamps. P-192 was generated in May 1997. P-256 in April 1998. The binary curves date to February 1984 (with a timestamp doubling quirk suggesting the program halved the clock on that run). The base points? Also recovered — from the FreeLIP long-integer arithmetic package's random generator.

The beautiful punchline: this is good news. The curves were not backdoored. They were generated by a bored engineer in the late 90s with a timestamp and a PRNG that anyone could reproduce. The mystery was never malice — it was carelessness. A 30-year cryptographic anxiety resolved by an AI that noticed a pattern in the zero bits.
