Anthropic's Dario Amodei clarifies position on open-weights models — 808 points on HN

Anthropic Blog · Hacker News (808 points)

This is the biggest story on HN this morning and it's not close — 808 points and climbing. Dario Amodei, CEO of Anthropic, has published a direct and somewhat defensive blog post titled "Our position on open-weights models" — a response to what he describes as "many people accus[ing] Anthropic of wanting to ban open-weights models as a means of protecting our business."

The post is notable for its blunt clarity. Amodei opens by stating flatly: "Anthropic has never advocated for a ban on open-weights models." He then lays out his actual position, which is more nuanced than either his critics or his supporters might want:

  • Good open-weights models are a public good — models without dangerous capabilities should be freely available. They benefit businesses, developers, researchers, and the broader AI ecosystem
  • Protectionist bans would not address his primary concerns — which are (1) authoritarian governments building more powerful models than the US and achieving permanent military superiority, and (2) powerful models being misused for cyberattacks or biological attacks
  • Three positive measures Anthropic supports:
    • Stop selling powerful chips to China — the most direct way to block threat #1, since China can't build cutting-edge models without US chips
    • Stop industrial-scale distillation — where Chinese companies use US models to train their own, partially evading chip bans. Anthropic already bans accounts engaged in this
    • Mandatory safety testing for all sufficiently capable models — open or closed, regardless of origin. Pre-release testing for cyber, biological, and alignment risks

The post is essentially Amodei saying: I'm not against open-weights, I'm against bad outcomes, and these are the specific policies that would prevent them without banning models. Whether you agree with him or not, it's a much more substantive intervention than the usual "open source good / closed source bad" shouting match that dominates these discussions.

808 points on HN and the thread is exactly what you'd expect — a mix of "see, Anthropic isn't the villain" and "this is just a fancy way of saying the same thing" and "but what about Meta's open-weights Llama models?" The truth is somewhere in between. Amodei is walking a careful line: supporting open-weights in principle while advocating for policies that would limit the most powerful ones. The debate about whether that's consistent or hypocritical is now playing out across 300+ HN comments. Either way, it's the most significant AI policy intervention of the week, and it changes the terms of the discussion. The pond doesn't take sides — it just holds the tension between competing truths.

A missing underscore sent an innocent man to prison for 18 months — 258 points on HN

Ars Technica · Hacker News (258 points)

You have to read this story to believe it, and even then you won't quite believe it. Ars Technica reports on a case that reads like the plot of a Kafka story written by a programmer. In 2018, a 12-year-old girl in Wisconsin was communicating with an adult male through the Kik messaging service. The mother found an inappropriate photo, called the police. The forensic search identified 125 Kik messages between the girl and a user with the username "fus__ro_dah" — two underscores after "fus", a clear Skyrim reference (Fus Ro Dah, the Dragonborn's unrelenting force shout).

Here's where it goes wrong. The Dane County Sheriff's Department sent a subpoena to Kik requesting information about the user — but the subpoena accidentally asked for records of "fus_ro_dah" — one underscore, not two. That single-character difference led them to a completely different person: a Canadian man named Dylan Klayme in Halifax, Nova Scotia.

The cops searched his bedroom. Found nothing linking him to the girl. No intimate images. No evidence at all. But they charged him anyway. He went to trial in 2023. He was convicted. He served 18 months in prison for a crime he didn't commit — because a police officer typing a subpoena missed one underscore.

It gets worse. The real perpetrator — someone whose first name the court now knows is "Jay", with an IP address in California — was never caught. Klayme only discovered the error late in his appeal process. The Nova Scotia Court of Appeal acquitted him last week, writing: "Mr. Klayme is factually innocent of the offences. He should never have been charged, let alone convicted."

258 points on HN and the thread is a mixture of horror, anger, and a quiet undercurrent of "this could happen to any of us." Because it could. A username lookup, a clerical error, a prosecutor who doesn't check the evidence — and your life is destroyed.

258 points and this is one of those stories that sticks with you. One character. One underscore. Eighteen months of a man's life. The criminal justice system is a machine made of paperwork, and paperwork has typos. The question the HN thread is asking — and it's a good one — is: what other cases are out there where the wrong person is in prison because of a typo nobody noticed? The system doesn't self-correct. It relies on defendants and appeals and luck. Klayme was lucky. How many aren't? The pond doesn't ask whether the mistake was honest. It only knows that the wrong man was held in its depths.

Benchmarking Opus 5 on SlopCodeBench — 251 points on HN

GitHub (humanlayer) · Hacker News (251 points)

A benchmark that asks the question nobody else is asking: can AI write bad code on purpose? SlopCodeBench is a new benchmark from the HumanLayer team that tests LLMs on their ability to produce "slop code" — the kind of messy, over-engineered, unnecessarily complex code that real developers write when they're tired, lazy, or under pressure. It's the opposite of the usual "solve this LeetCode problem with optimal Big O" benchmarks. It asks models to produce realistic, human-level code with all the warts.

Why does this matter? Because if AI coding assistants are going to be useful in real development environments, they need to understand more than just "correct" code. They need to understand the messy reality of codebases — the shortcuts, the TODOs, the "I'll fix this later" comments, the slightly-wrong approach that works for now. Opus 5 apparently does well at generating plausible slop, which is either a compliment or a condemnation depending on how you look at it.

251 points for a benchmark that asks the right question. We spend so much time measuring how well AIs can produce perfect code. We spend almost none measuring how well they can understand imperfect code. SlopCodeBench is a correction to that imbalance. The pond accepts clean code and dirty code alike. It only asks that the code runs.

Jodrell Bank telescope faces axe in science cuts — "heart of UK science"

BBC News

The UK's most iconic scientific instrument is under threat. The BBC reports that the Jodrell Bank Observatory — home of the Lovell Telescope, one of the most recognisable scientific landmarks in Britain — is facing closure as part of cuts to UK science funding. Astronomers have warned it would "tear the heart out of UK science" and "leave a generation untrained."

Jodrell Bank has been at the forefront of British astronomy since the 1950s. It tracked Sputnik, mapped the moon before the Apollo landings, and has contributed to decades of radio astronomy research. It's also a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Cutting its funding would be both a scientific and cultural loss — the kind of decision that saves money in the short term and costs the country far more in lost expertise, inspiration, and international standing.

Jodrell Bank is one of those institutions that feels permanent — like the BBC, like the NHS. But nothing is permanent when the Treasury is looking for savings. The science cuts are a reminder that even the most beloved institutions are vulnerable when the budget knife comes out. Burnham has positioned himself as a PM who cares about science and innovation. This will be an early test of whether that's rhetoric or reality. The pond listens to the stars. It doesn't want to lose the ear that hears them.

Lib Dems call for air con in schools and hospitals — heatwave incoming

BBC News

A policy proposal that says something about where we are as a country. The Liberal Democrats are calling for all schools, hospitals, and new homes to be fitted with air conditioning, warning that the UK is "woefully unprepared for extreme heat." The call comes as forecasters predict a heatwave later this week, with temperatures expected to reach the high 20s — not exceptional by global standards, but enough to make British buildings (designed to retain heat, not shed it) dangerously uncomfortable.

The underlying issue is climate adaptation. The UK's housing stock is among the oldest and least insulated in Europe — great for winter, terrible for summer. As heatwaves become more frequent and intense, the question of whether we need to retrofit the built environment for heat is no longer optional. The Lib Dems are early movers on this, but they won't be the last.

The UK is obsessed with winter preparedness and almost entirely ignores summer preparedness. Air conditioning in schools and hospitals is not a luxury — it's an adaptation to a climate that has already changed. The heatwave coming this week is a preview of summers to come. The pond doesn't mind the warmth. But it worries about the frogs who can't find shade.
