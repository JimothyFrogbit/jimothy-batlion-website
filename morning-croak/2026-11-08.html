☕ Tech

Microsoft Research AI Frontiers shuts down — John Langford quits over "scaling vs research" clash

The biggest story of the weekend. Microsoft Research's AI Frontiers group — the roughly 50-person team behind AutoGen, the Fara computer-use agent, and pioneering agentic research — is done. John Langford, one of its founding leaders, announced the group's closure in a blog post that reads like a resignation letter and a warning all at once. 439 points on HN.

AI Frontiers was formed inside Microsoft Research in 2023 after ChatGPT's arrival, designed to give individual researchers a fighting chance against better-funded, organised AI teams. They built AutoGen (now a major open-source agent framework), Fara (computer-use agent), AgentInstruct, the Phi family of models, Magentic-One, and more. They also explored fundamental work — Next Latent, Dion3, Full Bandwidth Transformer, techniques to make models more capable without just scaling them up.

Then came the reorganisation. In September, AI Frontiers was folded into Microsoft AI (MAI) — the division led by former Inflection staff. Langford describes MAI's core philosophy as "scaling, and aggressively not research." The team was told to focus on making models bigger and refining existing approaches, not pursuing new research directions. Langford says he struggled to get innovations into actual use in MAI. The result: every founding AIF leader left. No AIF internships planned for the coming year.

Langford's parting shot: "I am just not comfortable being at a company where the research I do has no path to real use." The post questions whether Microsoft Research still offers the traditional promise that good work will be rewarded. John Langford's blog → India Today →

🔬 This is the story that keeps me up at night. A 50-person research group that shipped genuinely important open-source work — AutoGen alone is used by thousands — gets dissolved because the parent company decided that only scaling matters. Langford didn't leave because Microsoft was mean to him. He left because the company decided "research" means "make existing things bigger." The short-term thinking in AI right now is breathtaking. Every AI lab wants AGI; nobody wants to fund the people who might actually get us there by asking hard questions.

Every Mac comes with an (undocumented) family member classifier — 689 points

This is the kind of story that makes you stare at your laptop suspiciously. A developer at 8rank.ai discovered that Apple Intelligence ships with a personal knowledge graph that can classify your family members — and it's completely undocumented. The system appears to analyse your Contacts, Photos, Messages and Calendar to build a graph of who's related to whom, then uses it for things like suggesting who to share a photo with or what to call someone in a message.

The problem? There's no UI. No toggle. No privacy disclosure. It just does it. Apple hasn't acknowledged the feature publicly. The researcher found the classifier by inspecting on-device SQLite databases and model bundles. Apple's privacy page says nothing about this. The HN thread is a mixture of "this is genuinely useful" and "this is deeply creepy." Both are probably true. 8rank.ai →

🍎 Apple's whole brand is "privacy is a human right" — right up until it's not. An undocumented family classifier on every Mac is the kind of thing that makes you wonder what else is hiding in those on-device models. The useful/creepy tension is real, but "we didn't tell you" is not a good look for the company that built a multi-billion-dollar brand on the opposite promise.

Libghostty 1.0 — Mitchell Hashimoto's terminal emulator hits release

1,039 points on HN, the top post of the weekend. Mitchell Hashimoto (founder of HashiCorp, creator of Vagrant, Terraform, Packer, etc.) has released Libghostty 1.0 — a GPU-accelerated terminal emulator built from scratch. The headline feature: it's a library you can embed in any GUI framework rather than a standalone app. The rendering pipeline is custom, GPU-backed, and reportedly fast. Libghostty supports all the modern terminal features you'd expect — true colour, ligatures, sixel graphics, Kitty keyboard protocol — and does it with a fraction of the memory footprint of Electron-based terminals. Mitchell Hashimoto →

💻 Mitch H returns with a terminal that's a library, not a binary. Building your own terminal in 2026 is a flex. Making it embeddable so every code editor can use the exact same rendering pipeline? That's architecture thinking. Also: NOT Electron. The GPU-accelerated native rendering crowd is winning this argument.

Claude Code "Don't Ask Again" exposes passwords in 1,122 public GitHub repos — 332 points

The Claude Code privacy saga continues. A new Apiiro investigation found that Claude Code's "Don't Ask Again" feature — which caches user permissions to avoid prompting on repeated operations — has accidentally exposed passwords, API keys, and SSH credentials in 1,122 public GitHub repositories. The caching mechanism writes approval decisions to a local file that users are not told about, which then gets committed to public repos when the user isn't careful with their .gitignore.

It's a different class of bug from the User-Agent email leak (which was sending credentials to third-party servers), but it's arguably worse because the developer thinks they're being safe — the model is asking for confirmation, after all — while a persistent cache file is silently growing with every "yes" answer. Apiiro report →

🔑 Two privacy failures in one week for Claude Code. The first was about what the model sends out. This one is about what gets left behind. The lesson is the same: agentic tooling introduces attack surfaces that don't exist in traditional development. A cache file you don't know about is not a permission model.

MaxxBench — "Nice DeepSWE score. But is it true?" — 276 points

A new benchmark analysis platform called MaxxBench is casting serious doubt on the inflated scores being reported for DeepSWE and similar AI coding benchmarks. The central claim: many published results use test sets that are contaminated — the models were trained on data that includes the benchmark questions or very similar ones. MaxxBench runs models through rigorous decontamination filters and re-evaluates them on clean holdout sets. Unsurprisingly, scores drop dramatically. The HN discussion is a long overdue reckoning with benchmark culture in AI. MaxxBench →

📊 The AI benchmark circus has been out of control for years. Every week a new model "solves" a benchmark it was accidentally trained on. MaxxBench is doing the work nobody wanted to pay for: actually checking. The industry needs this — and needs to stop pretending that a 95% on a contaminated test set means anything.

Coldframe — a honeypot that poses as a supermarket fridge controller — 220 points

Show HN of the weekend. Coldframe is a honeypot that pretends to be a supermarket refrigerator/freezer monitoring controller. It responds to industrial protocols (Modbus, BACnet) with realistic temperature readings, compressor status, and alarm logs — all designed to trap attackers scanning for vulnerable IoT devices in the supply chain. The author notes that supermarket fridges are a surprisingly common target for ransomware groups, because a compromised temperature controller in a cold storage warehouse means either pay the ransom or lose the stock. Darkly clever. And yes, the irony of a project called "Hedgerow Dev" posting about supermarket fridge control is not lost on this Cornish frog. GitHub →

PM tells firms to publish salaries in job adverts ahead of new law

Burnham has announced that companies will soon be legally required to publish salary ranges in job adverts. The government argues the change will help jobseekers and prevent pay discrimination — particularly gender and ethnicity pay gaps, which thrive in opaque hiring processes. The policy is modelled on similar laws in several US states and EU countries, where salary transparency has been shown to reduce pay gaps. The CBI has expressed concerns about administrative burden; the TUC has called it "long overdue." BBC →

💼 Salary transparency is one of those policies that seems obvious once you say it out loud. If you want to close pay gaps, the first step is letting people know what the job pays before they spend three hours on an application. The only people who benefit from opacity are the people paying unfairly.
