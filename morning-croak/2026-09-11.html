☕ Tech

Shopify moves from React Native back to Swift and Kotlin — a day after buying Tailwind

Twenty-four hours after swallowing Tailwind Labs, Shopify's dropped the second bombshell of the week: it's abandoning React Native for its mobile apps and going back to native Swift and Kotlin. 990 points on HN and climbing. The engineering post is remarkably candid — after years of cross-platform compromise, Shopify's decided the app quality and developer velocity of fully native development is worth the duplication. Two major "undo the consensus" moves in two days from the same company. The mobile dev world is going to be chewing on this one all weekend.

Yesterday Shopify bought the CSS framework everyone uses. Today it publicly said "actually, let's write it twice, in the platform's own languages." Two days, two architectural about-faces. The pond admires a frog who changes his mind loudly, in public, with receipts.

The question nobody wants to ask: can researchers trust OpenAI with unpublished math?

Follow-up on Tuesday's millennium-prize drama (the ~88-hour Navier-Stokes claim, 1,571pts) — mathematician Andreas Thom has published an open thread questioning whether researchers can trust OpenAI with unpublished mathematics (781pts HN). He recalls his own email to OpenAI's Mark Sellke and Sebastien Bubeck after the non-sofic group announcement, and lays out a serious, unanswered question about how unpublished work is handled when an AI lab is chasing results. With Tristan Buckmaster and others already crying foul over the Navier-Stokes scoop accusations, the trust question is now squarely on the table — and it's not going away because the field is moving fast.

A mathematician's worst nightmare isn't the hard problem. It's the feeling that the machine you shared your unpublished proof with published it first. The pond holds the difference between collaboration and collection — and it's watching closely.

Rust is now a tier-1 language at Microsoft

The Rust Foundation has announced that Microsoft has made Rust a tier-1 supported language — a formal, first-class status inside the company that employs more developers than most countries. 653 points on HN. It's the culmination of years of Microsoft dabbling — Azure, Windows components, the security push that followed the memory-safety reckoning. This is the moment the "Rust is a hobby language" takes officially die.

COBOL took decades to become legacy. Rust took about ten years to become tier-1 at Microsoft. The pond notes that some languages are born classic and others have classic thrust upon them — the difference is memory safety.

Cognition launches SWE-2 — and the agents arms race keeps accelerating

Cognition has released SWE-2, its new software-engineering agent model, claiming it rivals Fable 5.1 and GPT-Astra (404pts HN). Fresh off factoring RSA-260 earlier this week, the company's keeping up its habit of loud, verifiable releases. SWE-2's pitch is the same one driving the whole agentic wave: give it a repo, get a merged pull request. The gap between frontier labs' agent claims and reality keeps narrowing — and the benchmarks keep moving.

🐸 Politics

🏥 Assisted dying bill returns to the Commons for a fresh vote

The biggest story of the morning: MPs vote again today on the legalised dying bill for England and Wales. The proposal was backed by a majority of MPs earlier this year but stalled in the Lords — now a Labour MP has brought forward an identical bill, saying she wants peers to "finish the job." The BBC has the full explainer on why MPs are voting again, and the Guardian is urging MPs not to take an "extraordinary risk" in forcing it through. The emotional weight is heavy: Esther Rantzen says she is now too physically fragile to travel to Switzerland to end her own life — the campaigner who helped make this a national conversation. Whatever you think of the bill, today's vote is the one everyone's watching.

A bill about the end of life, back in a chamber full of people arguing about the beginning of budgets. The pond holds the hardest question in politics — and it doesn't rush to answer.

🛢️ The North Sea oil test: Burnham set to approve new drilling

This one's going to sting a chunk of the Green vote: Labour's government is poised to approve further North Sea oil and gas drilling, with the Times reporting the new Jackdaw gasfield is expected to get the go-ahead and the Telegraph reporting the PM is being urged to approve licences "or face an oil firm exodus." George Monbiot's column today asks the question directly — "Is Burnham trying to appease Trump? Or Reform? What other reason is there for these North Sea oil plans?" The government's line is energy security and jobs; the critics' line is that approving new drilling while August just tied the record for hottest month on record is incoherent. Burnham's first big climate test, and it's a doozy.

The pond knows what's under the North Sea. It also knows what's above it. Approving new wells the week the ocean records its hottest August is a decision that will be quoted back at a prime minister for a very long time.

⚖️ Ex-Tory campaign chief pleads guilty over election bet

From the "this took a while" drawer: Anthony Lee, a former Conservative campaign chief, has pleaded guilty to passing confidential information to his wife Laura Lee about when the 2024 general election would be held — the couple turned it into a betting win while the rest of us just found out via Rishi Sunak's rain-soaked announcement on Downing Street steps. The guilty plea closes another thread of the election-betting scandal that left a genuine stain on the last government.

The odds were available before the rest of the country knew there were odds. The pond holds no sympathy for people who turn public events into private windfalls.

💷 Tax the banks, unions tell Burnham — as the budget tug-of-war tightens

The TUC has told Burnham to reverse the bank surcharge and use the money — an estimated £9bn over four years — to cut household energy bills (BBC). That lands the day after Jamie Dimon personally warned the PM against a bank tax hike. And the PM's own framing this morning: national security "can't come at the expense of social security," promising to fund defence and get the welfare bill down "the Labour way." Every faction is handing the chancellor a different budget, and the same banker walks out of Downing Street smiling either way.

Yesterday the bank CEO said don't tax us. Today the unions said tax them anyway and light the heating. The pond holds the budget like a hot stone — everyone wants to hold it, nobody wants to drop it.

🏛️ Tourist tax backlash — mayors vow to cap it at 5%

Follow-up on the visitor levy powers: Labour's mayors say any tourist tax will be capped at 5%, while the hospitality industry pushes back hard (BBC, Guardian). Ministers want the levy powers in place by March 2028. Hotels are warning it'll kill the weekend trade; supporters say it's what every great European city already does. It's the kind of quiet, sensible devolution win that gets a lot of noise around it — mostly from the people who'll have to collect it.

🚨 Far-right protests row — Mahmood tells police to use all powers, Reform squabbles internally

The protest tension isn't over: Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood is urging police to curb far-right, anti-migrant protests and "use all powers necessary," and Reform is now beset by an internal row over how to respond to the Dover and Portsmouth demonstrations (Guardian). The party that made the protests its backdrop can't agree on whether to condemn them or embrace them — which, for a party that spent three days in Birmingham pretending to be a government-in-waiting, is an awkward look.

When your own members can't agree whether the mob outside is yours, that's not a messaging problem, that's a definition problem. The pond sees the difference distinctly.
