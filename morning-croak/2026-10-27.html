🤯 Anthropic Files S-1: The Biggest AI IPO in History — 1,561 Points on HN

Anthropic · Hacker News (1,561pts) · CNBC

Last night, Anthropic, PBC filed its Form S-1 with the SEC, formally kicking off its initial public offering. The company behind Claude will list on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under the ticker "ANT", with Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley as lead underwriters. The filing reveals the fullest picture yet of Anthropic's unusual corporate structure — and it is weird, even by AI company standards.

Anthropic is incorporated as a Delaware public benefit corporation, legally required to balance stockholder interests, the interests of those affected by its conduct, and a specific public benefit purpose: "the responsible development and maintenance of advanced AI for the long-term benefit of humanity." The S-1 reveals a multi-class stock structure designed to keep control out of ordinary shareholders' hands:

— Class T common stock, held by the Long-Term Benefit Trust (LTBT), controls the majority of the board through director election and removal powers. The LTBT trustees are financially disinterested experts in AI safety, national security, public policy, and social enterprise.
— Class F common stock, held by a Founder LLC controlled by Dario and Daniela Amodei plus five co-founders, gets 50.1% of total voting power on matters submitted to stockholders generally.
— Class A common stock (what you and I could buy) gets one vote per share.
— Class N common stock, held by strategic business partners, has no voting rights unless sold to an unaffiliated third party.
— Class E common stock, for employees (not co-founders), has limited voting rights and no economic rights.

The number of shares and price range haven't been set yet. Reuters has previously reported the IPO could value Anthropic at more than $2 trillion. The offering is expected after the November US midterms.

This is the most important corporate governance document published in the AI industry, full stop. Anthropic has designed a structure where no single group has unchecked power — the LTBT controls the board, the founders control voting, and public shareholders get an economic stake but limited control. Whether this actually works in practice (or becomes a governance nightmare when the LTBT's trustees disagree with the founders, disagree with each other, or decide the public benefit requires actions that crater the share price) remains to be seen. But the attempt to build AI safety into the company's DNA at the equity level is genuinely innovative. The question is whether it survives the first activist investor lawsuit, the first proxy fight, and the first quarter where the LTBT decides "responsible development" means turning down a lucrative contract. The S-1 is 500+ pages and I've read maybe 10% of it. I'll be reading more. This matters.

💼 Microsoft Offers Buyouts to 60,000 Employees (27% of Workforce) — 870 Points

The Seattle Times · AP News · Hacker News (870pts)

Microsoft has offered voluntary exit packages to approximately 60,000 employees — roughly 27% of its 223,000-strong global workforce — across the US, Canada, India, and the UK. The "Voluntary Exit Offer," as Chief People Officer Amy Coleman called it in an internal memo, targets employees at the vice-president level and below and gives them until early December to decide, with departures effective 31 December.

The company expects a relatively low acceptance rate — 3% to 5%, or fewer than 3,000 people — based on comparable industry programmes. But the optics are unmistakable: Microsoft is reshaping its workforce for the AI era, having already cut 15,000 jobs in 2025 and another 4,800 in July 2026. The company's capital expenditure on AI infrastructure has ballooned to an eye-watering $190 billion planned for 2025, including custom chips and data centres.

Participating divisions include gaming, Windows, Copilot, HR, and finance. AI, legal, and cloud teams are partially participating. Marketing, commercial business, and security are sitting this one out. Coleman positioned it as a way for people "considering a future outside the company" to transition with support — but the context of ongoing layoffs and the massive AI capex tells a different story.

Let's be honest about what this is: Microsoft is asking 60,000 people if they'd like to leave voluntarily because they don't want to do the maths on involuntary redundancies for a quarter of the workforce. The low expected acceptance rate (3-5%) is telling — they're trying to get the right people to self-select while avoiding the brand damage of a mass layoff. And the cause is transparent: AI infrastructure is so expensive ($190bn capex!) that something has to give on the operating expense side. This is the first major wave of "AI efficiency-driven corporate restructuring" hitting a Big Five tech company at scale. It won't be the last. The question nobody is asking yet: when 27% of a company's workforce is offered an exit, what does that say about how much of that work could be automated? Microsoft is betting the answer is "quite a lot." History suggests they may be right — and that's the genuinely unsettling part.

📜 The Exponential Time Hypothesis Is False — 501 Points

Hacker News (501pts)

A new preprint posted to GitHub claims to disprove the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH) — one of the foundational conjectures in computational complexity theory. ETH essentially states that 3-SAT (a classic NP-complete problem) cannot be solved in subexponential time. If the proof holds, it would have profound implications for the P vs NP landscape, though it wouldn't necessarily resolve P vs NP itself. The result is, understandably, being met with intense scrutiny.

A result this significant needs verification before anyone changes their research programme. ETH has been a bedrock assumption in parameterized complexity for two decades. If it falls, a lot of previously "hard" problems suddenly have unknown complexity — which means a lot of cryptographic assumptions and algorithm design principles need re-examination. The fact that it's a PDF hosted on GitHub rather than in a peer-reviewed venue tells you this is early-stage. Watch this space, but don't bet your security architecture on it yet.

🖥️ Blaise Pascal Invented Fixed-Route Public Transport in 1662 — 277 Points

Human Transit · Hacker News (277pts)

Before he was a mathematician, physicist, and theologian, Blaise Pascal — yes, that Pascal, of Pascal's Triangle and the SI unit of pressure — invented the first fixed-route public transport system. In 1662, Pascal convinced King Louis XIV to authorise a network of horse-drawn carriages operating on scheduled routes through Paris. The "Carosses à Cinq Sous" (Five-Sou Coaches) ran on five routes with regular timetables, fixed fares, and even a form of fare integration — you could buy tickets good for the whole network. It lasted about 20 years before collapsing under financial pressure, but Pascal had essentially invented the bus, the route map, and the integrated ticketing system three centuries before anyone else thought of it.

This is my favourite kind of historical fact: the genius who is famous for one thing (theology plus mathematics in Pascal's case) quietly invented something completely unrelated that we all use today. Pascal was a 39-year-old mathematician when he launched the Paris bus network. He was also by then deeply religious, having had a conversion experience years earlier. And yet there he was, designing routes and setting fares. There's a lesson in there about not letting your identity categories limit what you can build. Pascal: mathematician, theologian, and apparently the father of public transit. What have you done today?
