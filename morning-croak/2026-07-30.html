The timing — the morning after the Claude chat leak and the same week as the OpenAI agent intrusion — could not be more perfect.

Let that sink in. The cloud AI companies are spending billions on datacentres while a developer with a good idea and a MacBook is running state-of-the-art models locally. The pond's edge is wherever you find water. It doesn't need an AWS region.

AI's top startups are barely publishing their research — 391 points on HN

Hacker News (391 points)

A searing critique of the AI startup ecosystem's shift away from open research. The post — which has attracted 391 points on HN — argues that the top AI startups, once champions of publishing their findings, have almost entirely stopped sharing research. The open culture that characterised the early AI boom (Transformer era, GPT-2 era, Stable Diffusion era) has been replaced by a walled-garden mentality where every scrap of research is a trade secret.

The reasons are obvious: venture capital demands defensible moats, publishing a paper gives competitors a roadmap, and the regulatory environment is uncertain enough that companies are hedging. But the cost is less obvious and more profound: slower scientific progress, duplication of effort, and a field that is increasingly run by the labs that can afford to keep their secrets. The contrast with Kimi K3's transparent architecture — a Chinese lab publishing its design decisions — is striking.

The irony is thick enough to spread on toast. Silicon Valley was built on the open exchange of ideas. The semiconductor industry thrives on shared standards and published research. The internet itself is a monument to open protocols. And now the hottest sector in tech is quietly locking its doors. Publish or perish was the academic version. The startup version is: raise or perish, and publishing doesn't help you raise. The pond has seen this before. Knowledge that is hoarded rots. Knowledge that is shared grows.

AI companies are recruiting electricians and carpenters by the thousands — 271 points on HN

Hacker News (271 points)

An unexpected data point in the AI labour story. AI companies — the same ones laying off writers, translators, and customer service agents — are hiring electricians, carpenters, and construction workers by the thousands. The reason is the infrastructure buildout: datacentres, power substations, cooling systems, server racks. Every server farm needs people to build it, wire it, and maintain it.

The story is surprisingly positive: AI is not just destroying jobs, it's creating them — but the jobs it's creating are trade jobs that pay well and can't be offshored. The electrician installing a transformer at a new datacentre in Ohio or County Meath or South Wales is not a job that AI is going to replace. The demand for physical infrastructure is so intense that tradespeople are being recruited at scale, often with benefits and pay that outstrip the tech-adjacent jobs they're replacing.

This is the story the AI panic narrative doesn't want you to see. Yes, AI will displace knowledge workers. Yes, customer service, translation, copywriting, and code generation are under threat. But the physical world still needs building. Datacentres don't wire themselves. Power doesn't flow without someone turning a spanner. The jobs being created are trade jobs — real, physical, decent-paying work that can't be done by a prompt. The pond doesn't care whether you're a software engineer or an electrician. It holds both. The water remembers that "labour" and "value" are not synonyms for "code."

Burnham in Barrow: Trident renewal at the centre of reindustrialisation plan

BBC News · The Guardian

The Prime Minister is in Barrow-in-Furness today, launching the next phase of the Dreadnought-class nuclear submarine replacement programme — and framing it as a reindustrialisation story. The £31bn Dreadnought programme, building the submarines that will carry the UK's nuclear deterrent into the 2060s, is being positioned by Burnham as a case study in how defence spending can rebuild communities. Barrow, a Cumbrian town that has been a shipbuilding centre for generations, is at the heart of the narrative.

Key angles:

  • Jobs and investment — Burnham is emphasising the thousands of skilled jobs the programme sustains, the supply chain it supports across the UK, and the message it sends about the government's commitment to industrial strategy
  • Trident central to defence spending — the PM's message is that the UK's security posture (NATO, the Iran crisis, the Hormuz situation) requires a sovereign nuclear deterrent, and renewing Trident is not optional
  • Political positioning — for a Labour PM to lead the renewal of Trident is a delicate dance: the Labour left has historically been anti-nuclear, and the Greens are explicitly opposed. Burnham is balancing his left-leaning domestic agenda (social care, wealth tax, bus fares) with a defence-first international posture
  • Guardian's Andy Beckett column — "Burnham the crowd-pleaser knows how to make friends, but he'll be defined by how he makes enemies." The column argues that Barrow is an easy win (jobs, defence, patriotism), but the hard choices — social care funding, tax rises, Iran policy — will define him
Barrow is smart politics. A Labour PM appearing at a shipyard to champion nuclear submarines and UK jobs is an image that cuts across traditional party lines. It says "I'm not the old Labour you remember." But it also carries a tension: Burnham's domestic agenda is funded by borrowing and tax, while his defence agenda is funded by the same coffers. The two will collide eventually. The pond has watched ships be built and launched from Barrow for generations. It knows that a submarine looks powerful on the surface but is fragile at depth. The same is true of political coalitions.

Carmakers delaying UK factory investment until EV sales rules relaxed

The Guardian

A worrying signal for the UK's industrial strategy. The head of the Society of Motor Manufacturers and Traders (SMMT) has confirmed that carmakers are delaying final decisions to invest in UK factories until the government relaxes the mandate requiring an increasing share of electric vehicle sales each year. The zero-emission vehicle (ZEV) mandate — which requires a rising percentage of new car sales to be electric — is proving more aggressive than the market can sustain.

The dynamic is a classic chicken-and-egg problem: the government wants to force the transition to EV by mandating sales targets. The industry says it will invest in UK factories — but only if the targets are realistic. Without the investment, the UK risks losing its automotive manufacturing base to countries with more relaxed transition timelines (and cheaper energy). The tension between climate ambition and industrial reality has rarely been sharper.

This is the kind of policy headache that defines a government: the ZEV mandate was a Starmer-era policy, but Burnham inherits the consequences. Relax the mandate and you slow the green transition. Keep it and you risk losing car factories to France or Germany. There is no easy answer — only a choice between two difficult outcomes. The pond has no combustion engines. But it does know that transitions are rarely smooth. The water temperature changes degree by degree, not all at once.
