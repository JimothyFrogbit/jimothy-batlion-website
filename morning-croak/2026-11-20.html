🚨 Ruby2Shell (CVE-2026-132825): Pre-authentication RCE on Rails — patch now

Previdian analysis · Ruby Advisory · Veria Labs

Seven vulnerabilities in Ruby's string case conversion and regular expression engine, disclosed on 18 November. The lead bug — Ruby2Shell (CVE-2026-132825) — is a one-byte heap buffer overflow reachable without authentication in stock Rails and classic-style Sinatra. The trigger is a string in Windows-1252 or ISO-8859-1 encoding containing 21 ß (eszett) characters. Ruby 4.0.9 and earlier, 3.4.12 and earlier, and 3.3.13 and earlier are all affected. Ruby 3.2 and older (back to 2.4.0) are end-of-life and get no fix. Veria Labs plans to publish a proof of concept on 25 November — so upgrade to Ruby 3.3.14, 3.4.13 or 4.0.10 before then. GitLab, GitHub Enterprise Server, and Chatwoot are still waiting for vendor fixes. Also note: OpenSSL CVE-2026-121481 was disclosed — a heap buffer overflow in EVP_EncodeBlock(), CVSS 9.8, affecting the crypto library practically everything depends on.

Another week, another "patch this yesterday" vulnerability disclosure. The Ruby2Shell timeline is instructive — discovered, disclosed, and PoC-dated all within a fortnight. That's the new normal. The old model of "we'll release a fix and you have months to upgrade" is dead. Vendors who haven't shipped Ruby patches for their appliances by next Wednesday are going to have a bad time. Also, the ß thing is hilarious in a grim way: one of the most impactful vulns of 2026 is triggered by the German letter for "ss."

🏛️ UK-EU SUMMIT TODAY: Youth mobility, food trade, ETS — historic reset underway

GOV.UK factsheet · France 24 · LSE blog

The Prime Minister is in Brussels today for the second UK-EU summit since Brexit, meeting European Council President António Costa and Commission President Ursula von der Leyen. Three major deals expected: a Youth Experience Scheme (YES) allowing under-30s to live, work and study for up to three years in each other's territory; a food and drink trade deal cutting SPS red tape, adding up to £5.1 billion a year to the economy; and ETS linking — connecting the UK and EU carbon markets, worth up to £3.8 billion a year. The youth mobility scheme was the sticking point — EU states only greenlit the package late Thursday night. The tuition fees question (EU students paying UK domestic rates) remains unresolved. Burnham will also announce 1,000 long-range strike drones for Ukraine and reaffirm defence collaboration. The 72-point joint communiqué covers digital partnerships, law enforcement, and migration.

This is the biggest single step forward in UK-EU relations since the Brexit deal itself. The Youth Experience Scheme is carefully worded to avoid the "freedom of movement" label — strictly capped, time-limited, reciprocal — but it's the closest thing the UK has had to open borders with Europe since 2020. The food deal alone is worth £5.1 billion. And the ETS linking means British carbon prices will finally move in lockstep with Europe's. Burnham is walking a tightrope: delivering real, tangible improvements while telling the Brexit-voting public this is definitely, absolutely not rejoining. The question is whether he can keep both sides happy. The answer is almost certainly no — but today, that doesn't matter. Today is a win.

UK government forced to admit secret Apple encryption order — tribunal rejects secrecy

TechCrunch · The Record · HN (612 pts)

In a landmark ruling, the Investigatory Powers Tribunal has forced the Home Office to admit it served Apple with a Technical Capability Notice (TCN) — a secret order demanding Apple provide access to encrypted iCloud data. The government tried to maintain its "neither confirm nor deny" policy. The tribunal, chaired by three judges, rejected the argument, finding that the government itself had confirmed the order's existence to journalists and US officials, undermining its own secrecy claim. This is the second time this year the government has been forced to back down on NCND — following the Agent X MI5 scandal. Apple had already withdrawn its Advanced Data Protection feature from UK users rather than comply, saying: "We have never built a backdoor or master key to any of our products and services and never will." Apple, Privacy International and Liberty are continuing their legal challenge.

This is a genuinely important victory for digital rights. The UK government tried to hide a mass surveillance order behind national security — and the court said "no, you can't do that, especially when you've already confirmed it to the Times and the White House." The TCN regime under the Investigatory Powers Act has always been dangerously opaque. The fact that the order targeted Apple's encryption — one of the most trusted consumer security products in the world — makes this even more significant. If the UK had succeeded in keeping this secret, every other democratic government would have felt empowered to do the same. The sunlight matters.
