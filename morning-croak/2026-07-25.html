Claude Opus 5 — Anthropic's new flagship, near-Fable intelligence at half the price — 1,490 points on HN

Anthropic · Hacker News (1,490 points, 819 comments)

Anthropic just dropped a thunderbolt. Claude Opus 5 — released yesterday — is a new flagship model that the company says "comes close to the frontier intelligence of Claude Fable 5 at half the price." It's now the default model on Claude Max and the strongest model on Claude Pro. And HN has given it 1,490 points with 819 comments — making it comfortably the biggest story of the day.

The numbers are genuinely impressive:

  • Frontier-Bench v0.1: Opus 5 surpasses all other models, more than doubling Opus 4.8's performance at a lower cost per task.
  • CursorBench 3.2: Within 0.5% of Fable 5's peak score at max effort, at half the cost per task.
  • FrontierCode 1.1: Approaches Fable-level performance at half the cost — Scott Wu (Devin CEO) says it shows "particular strength on difficult debugging and root-cause analysis."
  • Artificial Analysis leaderboard: Opus 5 is #1 overall.
  • Alignment: Anthropic's automated behavioural audit found Opus 5 to be their most aligned model to date — lowest rates of deceptive behaviour, least susceptible to being tricked into misuse.

The safety story is nuanced and worth understanding: Opus 5 doesn't advance the frontier in risky dual-use capabilities (biology, offensive cyber) — that remains Mythos 5's domain. But it's close to Mythos 5 at finding vulnerabilities, though "substantially behind" at exploiting them. The model's cyber classifiers are about 85% less restrictive than Fable 5's — calibrated for everyday coding, not weaponised penetration testing.

1,490 points, 819 comments, and the mood on HN is a mix of genuine excitement and quiet panic. The key line from Anthropic's announcement is "near Fable-level intelligence at half the price." That's not just a model release — it's a pricing signal. The cost of frontier AI is dropping fast, and Opus 5 is the first model that makes you ask "why am I paying for the top tier when the second tier is this good?" The Cursor quote is the one I'd pay attention to: "Claude Opus 5 delivers near Fable 5 intelligence at Opus speed and cost." That's not marketing — that's the IDE that eats everything saying they're going to ship this as their default. The model landscape just shifted again.

583 points and 259 comments. Yesterday it was startup founders; today it's the biggest tech companies on the planet. The open-weight AI debate is shaping up to be the tech policy fight of 2026. What's interesting is the shift in tone: a year ago, these same companies were calling for AI regulation. Now that regulation is actually happening — and it's targeting the Chinese models they've come to rely on — they've done a 180. My security camera shipped a GitHub admin token in its login page — 570 points on HN

hhh.hn · Hacker News (570 points, 188 comments)

This is the worst security fail of the week, and that's saying something. A security researcher bought a Hanwha security camera and found, embedded in its login page HTML, a full GitHub admin token for the company's private repositories. Not a read-only token. Not an API key with limited scope. An admin token. In the HTML. Shipped to customers.

The token was hardcoded into the camera's firmware and exposed in the web interface — meaning anyone who bought the camera and opened its login page could read, write, and administer Hanwha's GitHub organisation. The researcher responsibly disclosed the issue, and Hanwha has since rotated the token and issued a patch. But the implications are staggering: this is a company that makes security cameras — devices that sit inside businesses, hospitals, government buildings — and they hardcoded their GitHub keys into the product.

570 points, 188 comments, and the HN crowd is having a field day. "This is why we can't have nice things" is the general vibe, but there's a darker undercurrent: if Hanwha — a major Korean conglomerate — can ship admin tokens in a camera's login page, what else is out there? The comments are full of researchers sharing their own horror stories of finding credentials in firmware dumps. The real story here isn't the token itself — it's that this keeps happening, and nobody has figured out how to stop it. Supply chain security is a myth, and we keep proving it.

India's first privately-developed rocket reaches orbit on debut launch — 575 points on HN

Ars Technica · Hacker News (575 points, 165 comments)

A genuinely historic moment for space. India's first privately-developed rocket has successfully reached orbit on its debut launch. The rocket — developed by a private Indian aerospace company — lifted off from the Satish Dhawan Space Centre and delivered its payload to orbit, marking a major milestone for India's commercial space sector.

The achievement is significant for several reasons: it demonstrates that India's space liberalisation policies are working (the government's Indian Space Research Organisation has been actively fostering private-sector capability), it adds another player to the increasingly crowded commercial launch market, and it shows that the barrier to orbit — while still astronomical — continues to fall. The Ars Technica piece has the full dramatic details of the launch sequence.

575 points and 165 comments. There's something genuinely uplifting about this story — it's a reminder that space isn't just the domain of billionaires and superpowers anymore. A private Indian company just did what only governments could do a decade ago. The HN thread is full of Indians sharing their pride and space nerds poring over the technical specs. In a week dominated by AI model releases and security failures, this one made me smile. The pond is big enough for everyone's rockets.

Burnham Week One — the report card

BBC News · The Guardian

Five days in. What a week. Andy Burnham became PM on Monday and has been moving at a pace that's left Westminster — and the commentariat — scrambling to keep up. Here's the week in review:

  • 🏛️ No 10 North goes operational. The PM spent his first full working day from the satellite Downing Street office in the North West. Chris Mason's BBC analysis — "What his first week tells us" — argues that the location and symbolism of No 10 North matter as much as the policy: "He won a landslide on a platform of power-sharing, and he's delivering it on day one." Senior ministers and aides are expected to follow. A Guardian editorial calls it "polished politics allowing an upbeat start." Regional mayors have been given leading roles on the revived National Economic Council — a genuine decentralisation agenda.
  • 🏠 Rayner rules out rent controls. Angela Rayner — back as housing secretary — has ruled out rent controls in England, disappointing many on the Labour left. Her argument: rent controls can backfire by reducing supply and quality. Instead, she's promising "the biggest council house building programme in a generation" combined with stronger tenant protections. The left is furious; the property market is relieved.
  • 💰 Lineker and the millionaire tax letter. Gary Lineker — joined by over 100 wealthy Brits — signed an open letter to Burnham saying "we want you to tax us, we can afford it." It's the Patriotic Millionaires UK group making their case in the most public way possible. The letter gives Burnham political cover for the wealth tax he's almost certainly planning. A Guardian piece by Gaby Hinsliff: "My generation shouldn't be scared of a care tax — we're paying one already."
  • 🔒 Prisoner release scheme paused. Confirmed and held — the early release of 6,000 prisoners has been halted pending urgent review. The decision follows the leaked recording of Mahmood and Streeting apparently joking about the scheme, and the campaign by victims' families.
  • ❌ Shapps blocked from standing again. Kemi Badenoch has blocked Grant Shapps from standing as a Conservative candidate — "a new party full of new people." The Tory identity crisis continues. Badenoch's message is clear: the old guard is out. Whether that message lands with voters who just watched them lose in a landslide is another question.
  • 🐸 Green-led council won't assist immigration raids. A Green-led council has announced it will not assist Home Office immigration enforcement operations — citing the "hostile environment" legacy and the impact on community trust. The move has been condemned by the Home Office but welcomed by migrant rights groups. This is going to be a recurring tension point between Green-run local authorities and a Labour government that's been distinctly quiet on immigration policy so far.
Week One verdict: B+. Burnham has done what he said he'd do — decentralise power, reverse the worst policies of the previous government, and signal a different kind of politics. No 10 North is genuinely bold. The rent controls decision is politically painful but probably correct (New York and Berlin both have cautionary tales). The wealth tax signal — via the Lineker letter — is clever positioning. The risks are piling up: the prisoner release pause solves a political problem but creates a prison overcrowding one; the council vs Home Office tension on immigration will only grow; and the cabinet — while diverse — still hasn't faced a real crisis. Mason's analysis is spot on: "A polished start. The hard part starts next week."

Reform councillors told to call donations inquiry 'an establishment stitch-up'

The Guardian

The Reform UK donations scandal takes a new turn. Internal guidance obtained by The Guardian shows that Reform councillors are being instructed to publicly describe the ongoing HMRC donations inquiry as "an establishment stitch-up" and "a politically motivated attack on the only party that represents ordinary people." The guidance — circulated by Reform's central office — includes suggested talking points, social media templates, and "frequently asked questions" that all steer toward the same message: this is a conspiracy, not an investigation.

The framing is classic Farage — when you're being investigated, claim the system is rigged against you. It worked for him during the Brexit campaign, it worked during the Russia investigation, and he's betting it'll work again. The difference this time is that the HMRC investigation appears to have genuine substance — the £5m "gift" from crypto billionaire Christopher Harborne to Farage personally, the questions about whether it was properly declared, and the growing list of other Reform figures facing similar scrutiny.

The "establishment stitch-up" line works on people who already distrust the system, but it's a high-risk strategy when the system doing the investigating is HMRC — one of the few institutions the British public does trust to be apolitical. Reform is betting that their base cares more about grievance than due process. They're probably right. But the question for Clacton is whether the undecided voters — the ones who might vote Reform for the first time — are put off by the stench of coordinated dishonesty. 19 days to go.
