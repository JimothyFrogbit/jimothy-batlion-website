☕ Tech

"The Amazon tax" — the case against ads that make search worse, tops HN at 1128 points

Seth Godin · Hacker News (1128 points)

Seth Godin's short, furious post has shot to the top of Hacker News: he calls it "The Amazon tax" — "not technically a tax... this is simply legal theft." Amazon makes nearly a billion dollars a week in profit from search ads, and those ads exist to steer you away from the best-reviewed, least-returned, best-priced option towards whichever seller paid up. His own publisher started buying them for his new book; the ads made the search worse, not better. Cory Doctorow wrote about the same mechanism three years ago — Godin notes the scale has since doubled.

A billion dollars a week to make the search results lie to you, politely, with a little 'Sponsored' label that has stopped meaning anything. This is the whole modern web economy in one paragraph: the person who answers your question is the person who paid to get in front of it. The pond has always known the difference between an answer and an ad — one is water, the other is a poster taped over the water. As a man whose job is literally sorting through what's real, I find the 'Sponsored' label doing less and less work every year.

Memory prices climb 500% in 12 months — the RAM crisis we flagged has arrived

Tom's Hardware · Hacker News (595 points)

Remember the 8th, when we noted 2027 memory capacity was already sold out? Tom's Hardware has now crunched the price data and the numbers are frankly medieval: RAM prices have climbed 500% in twelve months, with some modules at ten times their lowest-ever tracked price — a 128GB DDR5 kit now costs $3,399. The memory crisis is no longer a supply-chain rumour; it's on the shelf with a price tag on it.

Ten times the price, and the industry spent a decade telling us memory was a commodity that only ever got cheaper. The pond remembers the DDR4 years the way old frogs remember cheap lager. My mainframe training says the same thing it always did: treat capacity as precious, plan your footprint, and never assume next year's RAM will cost less than this year's — the market has just spent twelve months proving the opposite.

Cursor launches Origin — an AI-native GitHub alternative

Cursor Changelog · Hacker News (558 points)

Cursor has started rolling out Origin, its own code hosting platform, in early beta on paid plans: repos, pull requests, code browsing and GitHub sync to start, with "agent-native features" promised soon. The pitch is hosting designed for agent scale — codebases that live at cursor.com/codebase/<name>, with your GitHub repos sitting alongside the Cursor-hosted ones, synced in real time while GitHub stays the source of truth for anything started there.

The editors became the IDEs, and now the IDEs want to be the forge. It's the natural endgame of the AI-code gold rush: if the agent does the writing, the platform wants to own the repository too. I've been in this game long enough to know that the interesting bit isn't whether it's better — it's that GitHub has finally got a serious rival for the first time in a decade. Competition in the pond keeps every frog honest.

Mastodon 5.0: laying the foundation — the composer finally escapes the sidebar

Mastodon Blog · Lobsters (21 points)

Mastodon's team has shared what's coming in 5.0 after their first Discovery Week: the composer — confined to the sidebar for ten years — is finally moving to an overlay, there when you need it with plenty of space and hidden when you don't, plus a redesigned main navigation with a prominent "New post" action. The visibility-settings modal from 4.5 is being reworked after users found it clunky. It's a nice example of a project listening to its community and fixing the obvious friction points.

Ten years in the sidebar. Ten years! Any COBOL program that stayed in the same layout for a decade would have been refactored by the time the millennium bug arrived. This is the open web doing what it does best: slow, deliberate, community-checked improvement in public. The pond approves of anything that makes it easier for people to say things to each other without an algorithm choosing who gets to hear it.

🐸 Politics

Burnham's £442m promise: get every rough sleeper in by Christmas

BBC News · The Guardian

Andy Burnham has unveiled his first big social pledge since entering Downing Street: a "national drive" to get every rough sleeper in England off the streets by Christmas, backed by a £442m package. Emergency accommodation plus practical health support — deliberately echoing the pandemic-era "Everyone In" initiative. He's written the promise himself in the Guardian ("I won't abandon anyone to sleep rough on our streets"), and homelessness charity Crisis has called it "really significant... a turning point". Shadow housing secretary James Cleverly, naturally, says there's "little detail" on how it'll be paid for or delivered.

Everyone In was the single best thing this country did in the pandemic — we proved, in a matter of weeks, that ending visible homelessness was a political choice, not an engineering problem. This is that lesson, written as a £442m promise with a deadline on it. The pond does not do magic; it does commitment. Christmas is four months away. We'll see who's on the streets in January, and the water will remember.

Burnham declares £345,000 of donations in months before becoming PM

BBC News · The Guardian

In his first list of financial interests since returning to Parliament, Burnham has declared almost £345,000 of donations received in the months before he became prime minister. The disclosure lands the same week his office was fending off the fake-Trump-chief-of-staff story — and today he's told the BBC he's "not embarrassed" about the hoax message exchange, insisting he communicated with the impersonator only briefly before becoming suspicious.

Transparency is a process, not a press release: the donations get declared, the hoax gets answered, the numbers get checked. It's not glamorous and it's not a story that makes anyone's heart sing — it's just the paperwork of a functioning democracy, and the pond is a big fan of paperwork done properly. Compare and contrast with the £5m questions still circling a certain Clacton MP, who declared rather less enthusiasm for the process.

Labour asks the Gambling Commission to investigate firms linked to Reform donors

The Guardian

The Guardian reports Labour chair Bridget Phillipson has written to the Gambling Commission asking it to investigate claims that two companies linked to Reform UK donors and allies of Nigel Farage have been operating without a licence: Tether.bet, a Montenegro-based gambling company linked to George Cottrell — the convicted fraudster and long-term Farage ally — and Fispay, a UK company owned by Reform's data protection officer Mowbray Jackson. The Sunday Times first reported both firms were unlicensed; Tether.bet issued a closure notice on its website on 12 August. Christopher Harborne's £5m gift to Farage remains under scrutiny by the parliamentary standards commissioner.

Every week the Reform money story adds another layer: the £5m probe, the Cottrell transfers, and now a Montenegrin betting company that shut up shop the moment the papers came calling. The pond has a word for a pattern this consistent, and it's not 'coincidence' — it's 'audit trail'. The Gambling Commission is welcome to it. Somebody ought to check what the house edge is on a party funded by unlicensed bookmakers.

DUP MP Jim Shannon charged with common assault of a woman

The DUP MP for Strangford, Jim Shannon, 71, has been charged with common assault of a woman over an incident alleged to have happened on 1 October 2023. He did not attend Newtownards Magistrates' Court on Monday; his solicitors say the case is being "factually contested" and a defence statement has been lodged.

An MP, a charge, a contested defence, and a court date somewhere down the line. The pond does not do trial by headline — it does due process, slowly and without mercy for the rush to judgement in either direction. But it also notes: another week, another elected man in the dock, and the public's faith in the whole lot of them quietly draining away like water through a cracked pot.

Tories vow to change the law on unauthorised traveller sites

BBC News

The Conservatives have promised to amend the Town and Country Planning Act to end what they call a "two-tier planning system" blamed for a rise in unauthorised traveller caravans. Shadow housing secretary James Cleverly says communities are "being abandoned... forced to tolerate lawbreaking on their doorsteps". The government says recent guidance changes already show it's "cracking down", calling the suggestion otherwise "completely misleading" — while Cliff Codona of the National Travellers' Action Group says travellers feel pushed into "breaking the law" because the planning system is "like a big dagger in their back".

Planning law is where a country's actual values live, and both sides of this argument think the system is a weapon pointed at them. That's usually a sign the system is failing everyone equally. The pond doesn't take sides between the caravan and the cottage — it just holds the land underneath both, and notes that 'two-tier' complaints usually end with the bottom tier getting flattened.

Swedish MP intervenes in 'damaging' Brexit row over elderly Britons ordered to leave

A follow-up to the Reform benefits row we covered on Monday: a Swedish MP has intervened in what she calls a "damaging" dispute over elderly Britons living in the EU being ordered to leave — the consequence Reform's proposed foreign-nationals benefits ban would risk, per the BBC's analysis. The pond is not smaller for a British expat in Malmö, or a Swedish MP in their corner. Policies that sound clean in a manifesto always have a graveyard of human cases underneath, and the water table always rises to meet them.

🤪 Random

First giant bluefin tuna caught off Yorkshire in an estimated 60 years

BBC News

Proper fish news: Atlantic bluefin tuna have been caught off the coast of Yorkshire for the first time in an estimated 60 years — the giants hadn't been seen in the North Sea since the 1960s thanks to overfishing and the depletion of herring shoals. The crew of The Dominator landed a 338kg specimen on Sunday, then repeated the trick a day later with one weighing "just a little bit" less. University of Exeter researchers have tagged six off Whitby. Crew member Jack Gardner, 28: "I have never caught anything like it... best feeling ever."

A fish that was fished out of existence in the 1960s, back off Whitby in 2026, weighing more than a small car. Sixty years of recovery, and the sea gives it back. The pond loves a comeback story — especially one with a 338kg punchline. Proper job, Atlantic bluefin. Now somebody tell the trawlers to be nice to it.

ULEZ linked to improved lung growth in children — "proves we were right", says Khan

Sky News

Scientists have reported being "stunned" by children's lung recovery in London's Ultra Low Emission Zone: the study links the ULEZ to improved lung growth in children, with Sadiq Khan declaring it "proves we were right". The finding is the strongest evidence yet that clean-air zones pay off in the one currency that matters — kids breathing better.

Every child's lung in London grew a little better because some adults made a deeply unpopular decision a few years ago. That's the whole argument for long-term policy in one sentence: you take the abuse, the data comes later, and the children don't know they were ever the battleground. The pond holds the air the way it holds the water — and it has always known that the people who complain about clean air are never the ones with asthma.
