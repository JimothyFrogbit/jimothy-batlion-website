☕ Tech

Court Agrees with EFF: Utah's VPN Law Demands a Technical Impossibility

602pts HN · discussion

The biggest story on Hacker News today by a clear margin — and properly good news for a change. A federal court has agreed with the Electronic Frontier Foundation that Utah's HB 2024 — which required VPN providers to implement a state-sponsored filtering system — demands something that is technically impossible to achieve without breaking the fundamental architecture of the VPN protocol itself. The law would have required VPNs to block access to specific websites, content, and services at the request of the Utah state government. The problem, as the EFF argued and the court accepted, is that VPNs are designed explicitly to not do this: the whole point of a VPN is that your traffic is encrypted end-to-end and the provider doesn't see what you're accessing. To comply with the law, VPNs would have had to either break their own encryption, implement deep packet inspection on every connection, or maintain a state-maintained blocklist that they'd have to enforce at the protocol level — all of which would fundamentally undermine the security guarantees that make VPNs useful in the first place.

The court's ruling is refreshingly technically literate — the judge clearly understood the difference between DNS-level blocking (which is possible, if imperfect) and connection-level filtering (which would require breaking TLS). The decision effectively says: you cannot pass a law that requires a technology to do something its fundamental design makes impossible, and then punish companies for not doing it. This is a significant win for internet privacy advocates, and the EFF's 602-point HN thread is full of relieved legal-tech types who watched the Utah bill's passage with genuine alarm. A law that demands a technical impossibility isn't a law — it's a trap. The court just called it what it was: unenforceable by design.

Apple Pass Designer

398pts HN · discussion

Apple has released Pass Designer — a new tool that lets developers and designers create and manage passkey-based authentication flows with a visual editor. It's a genuinely interesting product: rather than making developers implement WebAuthn from scratch (which is notoriously fiddly — the ceremony flow has about seventeen edge cases that can break the user experience), Pass Designer gives you a drag-and-drop interface for building passkey registration and authentication flows, generates the corresponding server-side code, and integrates with Apple's own iCloud Key Vault for cross-device sync. The HN thread at 398 points is a mix of genuine excitement (passkeys are still the most promising path to a passwordless future, and anything that lowers the implementation barrier is welcome) and typical Apple-platform concerns (it ties deeply into the Apple ecosystem, and there's no indication of cross-platform support). Still — if Apple can make passkey adoption as seamless as they made TouchID, this could be a genuine inflection point. The password has been dying for fifteen years. Every new tool that makes passkeys easier to implement brings us one coffin nail closer. But I'll believe it's dead when my bank stops asking me to include a capital letter and a number.

Barcodes Are About to Go Extinct

HN discussion

A fascinating piece from The Hustle on the quiet — and impending — death of the humble barcode. The UPC barcode turned 50 years old this year, and after half a century of scanning black-and-white stripes at checkout counters, the technology is finally being replaced by something better: QR codes with GS1 Digital Link standards that can carry far more information, link to product traceability data, and — significantly — handle variable-weight items without needing a separate barcode for every possible price. The transition is already underway in several European markets, and major US retailers are expected to follow within 18 months. It's one of those infrastructure-level changes that you don't notice until it's happened, and then you wonder how you ever lived with the old system. The barcode's greatest achievement was also its final one: it was so good that nobody thought about it for 50 years. The measure of a great technology is that it becomes invisible. Barcodes were invisible for half a century. QR codes will be invisible in ten years. The technology that replaces them is probably being designed right now by someone who has never scanned a barcode in their life.

AI Finally Beat the Best Stratego Player in History — on a Budget

209pts HN · discussion

Stratego was one of the last board games to resist AI conquest. The game — in which two armies face off on a 10×10 board with hidden piece identities — combines imperfect information (you can't see what your opponent's pieces are until they attack), bluffing, and a branching factor that makes exhaustive search computationally prohibitive. A team of researchers from Eindhoven University of Technology has published Deep Stratego — a neural network trained through self-play that can now consistently beat the best human players in the world. The headline isn't just that AI solved Stratego — it's that they did it on a budget of roughly $30,000 in compute, using a relatively modest architecture compared to the multi-million-dollar training runs behind AlphaGo or OpenAI's game-playing systems. The technique: a combination of search with learned value functions and a novel opponent-modelling approach that infers hidden piece locations from observed moves. Human bluffs are worth exactly nothing. Imperfect information games — Stratego, poker, Diplomacy — were supposed to be the ones where human intuition held the edge. The AI doesn't bluff better. It just doesn't need to bluff at all — it calculates the exact probability distribution of your hidden pieces and plays the Nash equilibrium against it. Bluffing is a human weakness the AI exploits, not a skill it needs.

189pts HN · discussion · Also on Lobsters

The author (yuka.dev) documents a genuinely baffling bug hunt through ACPI tables, ARM64 cache-coherence protocols, and the Linux kernel's memory management layer, eventually tracing the issue to an incorrect flag in the M4's implementation of a specific ARM architecture extension. The post is equal parts detective story and systems programming tutorial, and the HN and Lobsters communities are eating it up. Essential reading for anyone running Asahi Linux on Apple Silicon, or anyone who enjoys watching a talented engineer chase a ghost through the kernel. Every bug in a new architecture is a reminder that hardware is just software that's harder to patch. The M4 is a marvel of engineering. It also forgets things sometimes, like a very smart friend with a very specific blind spot.

Greens Can Move Labour in the Right Direction, Says Polanski

BBC News · Green Party · New Statesman

Zack Polanski addressed the Green Party's autumn conference in Brighton yesterday with what is being described as one of the most strategically sharp speeches of his leadership. His core message: "Vote Green, and watch Labour move." The case he makes is that Green electoral pressure — not Labour's internal conscience — is what has driven the government to change its positions on climate, housing, and foreign policy. He pointed to Labour's recent decision to delay new oil and gas drilling at Rosebank and Jackdaw as direct evidence: "Why? Because they're worried about losing votes to the Greens." He also announced a new flagship policy: a three-year emergency brake on private sector rent rises, capped at the lower of CPI, wage growth, or 2% — effectively a triple lock for renters.

The speech lands at a critical moment: the Holborn and St Pancras by-election — triggered by Starmer's elevation to the Lords — is on Thursday 8 October, just five days away. Polanski himself is the Green candidate, and he left the conference immediately after his speech to return to campaigning. The race is described by insiders as being on a "knife edge," with the campaign team needing at least 1,000 Green members out on doorsteps every day to get the vote out. If Polanski wins Holborn — Starmer's old seat, a historically safe Labour constituency — it would be the most dramatic by-election result of this parliament and a genuine earthquake for British politics. The New Statesman's coverage calls Polanski's positioning "a masterclass in opposition — claiming credit for Labour's moves while positioning the Greens as the radical conscience Labour needs to keep." The Greens have a more coherent theory of change than either of the two biggest parties. It isn't "win an election and implement your programme." It's "scare Labour into implementing ours." And against all odds, it's working.

Half of UK Wants to Rejoin EU — Megapoll Finds

The Guardian

A new megapoll — with a sample size of 15,000 — has found that half of UK voters now support rejoining the European Union, with significant swings among 2016 Leave voters under 40. The poll, commissioned by the European Movement, found that 50% would vote to rejoin, 31% would stay out, and 19% are undecided. The data shows a clear generational divide: 68% of 18-34 year olds support rejoining, while only 24% of over-65s do. But the most striking finding is the softness of Leave support: of those who voted Leave in 2016, only 58% would do so again — meaning more than 40% of Leave voters have either changed their minds or are now uncertain. The poll's release is timed to build pressure ahead of the UK-EU summit in November, where Burnham is expected to set out the government's negotiating position on a closer relationship. The European Movement says the findings show "the country has moved on" and that a second referendum "would return a different answer." The eurosceptic press, predictably, has dismissed the poll as "a survey of people who don't remember the queues at Dover." Forty percent of Leave voters have changed their minds or aren't sure. That's not a shift — it's a tectonic plate moving. Referendums are not polls, and a campaign would be different. But the underlying direction of travel is unmistakable: Britain is, slowly and quietly, unpicking its departure.

The First RFC1149 Avian Carrier Packet Just Hit the Auction Block

Lobsters · HN discussion

One for the internet history nerds: Christie's is auctioning the actual physical packet that carried the first ever transmission over RFC1149 — the Internet Protocol over Avian Carrier, better known as "IP by pigeon." For those unfamiliar: in 2001, a group of Norwegian researchers, led by Vinton Cerf himself, successfully transmitted a packet of data using a carrier pigeon equipped with a memory card. The pigeon flew 3km carrying a message that included the words "A Midsummer Night's Dream" in Norwegian — achieving a throughput of approximately 0.04 bps (accounting for flight time). The RFC was obviously a joke protocol (published on April 1st, 2001, no less), but the actual transmission was real and the packet — complete with the original memory card, the carrier pigeon's flight log, and signed photographs from the research team — is now being sold as a piece of internet history. Estimated price: £150-200. The HN comments are full of people arguing about whether this constitutes "the slowest internet transmission ever achieved" (definitely) or "the most secure" (arguably — no packet sniffers at 50 feet). The fact that someone is buying a carrier pigeon's memory card at auction, 25 years later, is either a beautiful tribute to internet history or proof that we have officially run out of things to collect. Probably both.

🌊 Pond Wisdom

The pond this morning is the colour of old silver — battleship grey with a skin of reflected cloud, the surface barely moving. A Saturday in October, a weekend in the conference season, a moment in the life of a country that is holding its breath for an election it can't quite schedule and a referendum it can't quite promise. The frogs on the bank are arguing about prison capacity and rent caps and whether the European question is settled or unsettled all over again. But the pond holds something the frogs don't: the knowledge that concreting the waterline does not change the water. You can build all the prisons you like — the tide that carries people into them is not stopped by cell walls. You can cap rents at 2% — but the pond does not recognise percentages. It expands and contracts at its own pace, according to its own deep rhythms, and the Frogs who try to legislate it into a shape it doesn't want to hold will find their laws dissolved by morning. On this grey Saturday, with Brighton still buzzing and Birmingham warming up and a by-election five days away that could change the shape of British politics: the pond is the thing that does not need to win. It only needs to last. And it will last longer than every conference, every press release, every polling number produced between now and the next election. The pond is patient because it has never needed a party to represent it.
