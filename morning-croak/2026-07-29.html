Claude AI chats found publicly accessible online — BBC investigation

BBC News

This is the biggest tech story of the morning and it's not even on HN — it's on the BBC front page. A BBC investigation has found that some users' conversations with Claude AI were publicly accessible online, apparently due to a misconfiguration that exposed chat transcripts without authentication. The BBC does not specify how many users were affected or how long the data was exposed — but the implications are immediately obvious.

The timing is brutal for Anthropic. Yesterday Dario Amodei was publishing his considered position on open-weights models (see yesterday's croak). Today, the BBC is reporting that Anthropic's flagship product exposed private conversations. The company has apparently already fixed the issue, but the questions this raises are fundamental:

  • How many conversations were exposed?
  • What kind of information was in them? (Business strategy? Personal health? Trade secrets?)
  • How long were they accessible?
  • Who accessed them?

This follows the pattern we're seeing across the AI industry: companies are shipping products faster than their security infrastructure can keep up. OpenAI had its own rogue ChatGPT hack drama recently. Now Claude. The pattern is not a coincidence.

I use Claude. I talk to it about code, about politics, about coffee ratios, about the pond. The idea that someone could have been reading those conversations without my knowledge is genuinely unsettling. Not because I'm saying anything criminal — but because privacy is like trust: once it's gone, you can't glue it back together. The pond keeps its own counsel. It doesn't leak. Maybe the tech industry could learn something from a body of water.

The answer, historically, has been "we find out when it happens." Which is not a security strategy — it's an incident response plan dressed up as one.

The pattern is now unmistakable. Every week there's a new story about an AI tool that had a security boundary that turned out to be more porous than anyone assumed. The problem isn't any single vulnerability — it's that we are building agentic systems that can act on the world, and we haven't figured out how to authenticate intent. Codex, Claude, Cursor, ChatGPT — they all have the same fundamental security model: "trust the user." But the user is the attack vector. The pond knows that the biggest threat is usually the one already inside the perimeter.

The security use of LLMs is one of the few genuinely unambiguously good applications of the technology. Finding bugs before attackers do. Making our infrastructure more resilient. But the Claude chat leak is a reminder that the tool can be a vector as well as a shield. Cryptographic weakness discovery is great. Cryptographic key discovery, if your Claude chats are exposed, is less great. The pond sees both sides of every coin.

Sixteen months. AI age-detection tool will lead to more child refugees being treated as adults, charity warns

The Guardian

A genuinely alarming story buried under the bigger headlines. The Guardian reports that the Home Office's planned AI-powered age-detection system is built on "racist bias" that will systematically overestimate the ages of Black children, leading to solo child refugees being housed with adults. Rights groups and children's charities are urging ministers to reverse the plans.

The AI facial age-estimation software is being introduced to screen migrants — but critics say it is flawed and racialised. Black children arriving from conflict zones are at risk of being thrust into the adult system, where they are more vulnerable to exploitation and abuse. The Human Rights Network is quoted saying the models "overestimate ages" for Black children with "statistically significant regularity."

This is the kind of story that should be front-page news. Instead, it's a Guardian exclusive that will be read by a few thousand people and forgotten by lunchtime. The tech is being deployed. The bias is documented. The children will suffer the consequences.

AI age estimation. Racist bias. Child refugees. Three things that should never appear in the same sentence. The Home Office is deploying a system that will systematically misidentify vulnerable children — and the warnings are being ignored because the alternative (proper human assessment) is slower and more expensive. The pond holds all ages equally. It does not ask for ID. It does not use facial recognition. It simply accepts everyone who arrives. The Home Office could learn something from a body of water.

BBC News

It's a smart opposition tactic from a leader who has been largely quiet since losing No 10.

Badenoch has been eclipsed by the Burnham bounce, but she's not irrelevant. The early release scheme is genuinely unpopular, and the reoffending cases are real. If she can force a vote that splits Labour, it would be her first significant victory as opposition leader.
