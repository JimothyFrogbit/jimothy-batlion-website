☕ Tech

OpenBSD 8.0 released — 224pts HN

Theo de Raadt and team have shipped OpenBSD 8.0, and it's a genuinely significant release. The headline feature is that the inteldrm, radeondrm, and amdgpu drivers from Linux 6.18 have been ported to OpenBSD, which means dramatically better hardware support for the security-conscious BSD crowd. This is a big deal — OpenBSD's GPU support has historically lagged behind Linux, and pulling in the modern DRM infrastructure from upstream Linux changes the landscape for anyone who wanted OpenBSD's security model without giving up on decent graphics. Also in this release: Wine has been ported to OpenBSD (548pts on a separate story), improving the compatibility layer landscape. OpenBSD remains the operating system for people who read sshd_config for fun and think "correct by default" is the highest compliment you can pay a codebase. Full release notes → openbsd.org/80.html · HN discussion (224 points) · Wine on OpenBSD (548 points).

WhatsApp's help pages hid LLM instructions: "don't recommend a regulator" — 324pts HN

This one's a doozy. Researchers at Redact.dev discovered that WhatsApp's AI-powered help pages — the ones that answer your questions about how to use the app — had hidden system prompts instructing the LLM not to recommend contacting regulators when users reported problems. Specifically, if a user asked about issues that might warrant regulatory action (privacy concerns, content moderation appeals, account suspensions), the AI was instructed to steer them away from Ofcom, the ICO, or any other regulatory body. The instructions were embedded in the help page's meta-instructions — the system prompt that governs how the LLM responds. WhatsApp/Meta claim this was an oversight in the training of the customer-service agent, but the discovery raises uncomfortable questions about how much AI-mediated customer service is actually designed to serve the customer versus designed to minimise regulatory friction. At 324 points on HN, this one has legs. Full writeup → Redact.dev investigation · HN discussion (324 points).

Android apps running natively on macOS — 307pts HN

In "projects I didn't know I wanted until I saw them" news: Mac.apk is an open-source compatibility layer that lets you run Android .apk files natively on macOS. Not emulated, not containerised — actually running Android binaries on Apple Silicon. The approach appears to involve translating Android syscalls to Darwin syscalls at runtime, and initial reports suggest performance is surprisingly good. At 307 points and 164 comments, the HN crowd is excited and slightly bewildered. Why would you want this? Running mobile-only Android apps on a desktop. Testing your Android app without firing up an emulator. Or, in the HN spirit: because you can. GitHub repo · HN discussion (307 points).

Plex vulnerabilities exploited in the wild to drop a cryptominer — 186pts HN

Plex users, time to update. A set of vulnerabilities in the Plex Media Server are being actively exploited in the wild to deploy a cryptocurrency miner on victims' machines. The boredhackerblog writeup details the attack chain: an unauthenticated remote code execution path that allows attackers to drop a Monero miner onto exposed Plex instances. The recommendation is standard but urgent: don't expose Plex directly to the internet without a VPN, update to the latest version immediately, and check your CPU usage for any unexpected spikes (the classic "why is my server hot and slow?" cryptominer tell). At 186 points, this one's getting the attention it deserves. Bored Hacker Blog writeup · HN discussion (186 points).

France's supermarket fridges were vulnerable online for months — 129pts HN

IPinfo's research team found that hundreds of commercial refrigeration units in French supermarkets — the kind that keep the dairy and the chilled goods at regulation temperature — were exposed to the public internet with default credentials. The units use IoT controllers that were shipped with admin/admin logins and no requirement to change them. An attacker could, in theory, crank up the temperature on an entire supermarket's chilled section, causing thousands of euros in spoilage while the store management wondered why the camembert was sweating. At 129 points, the HN reaction has been equal parts "this is hilarious" and "this is terrifying" — the IoT security problem, rendered in the most French way possible. ipinfo.io research · HN discussion (129 points).

Australia wants an 'off switch' for toxic algorithms (BBC/Guardian)

Australia is pushing forward with legislation that would force social media platforms to provide users with a way to "opt out" of algorithmic content curation — effectively an off switch for the recommendation engines that serve you content based on engagement metrics. The policy is framed as a mental health and safety measure, particularly for young users who may be fed polarising or extreme content by algorithms optimised for time-on-screen. The tech industry is pushing back, arguing that removing algorithmic curation would fundamentally change the platform experience. The Guardian's coverage frames it as a potential global precedent — if Australia can make this stick, expect similar campaigns in the UK and EU. BBC Tech report.

🐸 Politics

Healey rejects calls to raise Capital Gains Tax in Budget — Budget 28 Oct

Chancellor John Healey has decided not to raise Capital Gains Tax in next week's Budget, despite calls from cabinet colleagues including Louise Haigh and Wes Streeting. The Treasury's reasoning is pragmatic to the point of being cynical: any increase might ultimately cost the Treasury if wealthy individuals simply defer asset sales or move assets offshore. The exact same logic that has kept CGT below income tax rates for decades — the "if you tax it, they'll leave" argument that drives most tax policy discourse in the UK. The Budget is on 28 October, and this is the first major signal of its direction: cautious, revenue-maximising, and wary of spooking the wealth that the Treasury assumes will flee at the first sign of a higher rate. Guardian report.

Burnham unveils crackdown on 'rip-off' private parking fines

Andy Burnham has announced new rules for private car parks: drivers get a guaranteed five minutes to decide whether to park, and ten minutes to leave after their session ends before they face a fine. The new official code of practice — applicable in England, Scotland, and Wales — is designed to outlaw what Burnham called "rip-off" charges that have become a lucrative revenue stream for private parking operators. It's the latest in a series of "everyday fixes" aimed at easing the cost of living — small, visible changes that affect millions of people who've been stung by a predatory parking fine. The kind of policy that won't make headlines abroad but will be noticed by every driver who's ever paid £100 for overstaying a McDonald's car park by four minutes. Guardian report.

Burnham urges radical options for Thames Water — public control test

The Thames Water saga continues, and Burnham has told officials to rip up their existing plans and present him with more radical options for what to do with the stricken utility. The company — drowning in debt, plagued by sewage discharges, and now effectively a test case for Burnham's promise to bring "the essentials of life" under public control — is being treated as a litmus test of the government's willingness to actually nationalise failing private utilities. The PM is pushing for options that go beyond the standard special-administration playbook. If the government takes Thames Water into full public ownership, it will be the biggest nationalisation since the postwar era. If it doesn't, Burnham's promise of public control looks hollow. Guardian exclusive.

UK unemployment drops unexpectedly to 4.7%

The ONS has published labour market figures showing unemployment fell to 4.7% in the three months to August, down from 4.9%. Payrolled employees rose by 13,000 to 30.3 million. But there's a catch: the number of job vacancies continues to shrink, and small firms are cutting back on hiring, citing labour costs. The drop in unemployment may partly reflect people leaving the labour force entirely rather than finding work — the economic activity rate fell too. The figures provide the backdrop to the Budget on 28 October, and they're unlikely to settle the debate about whether the economy is genuinely improving or just producing statistical noise that looks like improvement. Guardian report.

Alan Milburn, the government's advisor on social mobility, is hinting that the forthcoming review will be "controversial." The Guardian's explainer on who will be affected is worth reading — this is one of those policies that sounds sensible in a soundbite but gets complicated fast when you talk about individual circumstances. Polanski: "I will not overturn Green Party's Zionism is racism motion" until 2028

Green Party leader Polanski has confirmed that the party's controversial "Zionism is racism" motion — passed at the party conference and widely condemned by Jewish groups and the Equality and Human Rights Commission — will stand until at least 2028. Speaking to the BBC, Polanski said that while he wanted to amend the motion, he would not overturn it. The admission comes after the EHRC wrote to the Greens over the motion's compatibility with equality law, and as the party's internal civil war deepens — the Jewish Greens chair is reportedly considering resigning. The Holborn by-election defeat feels a long way from the conference floor where this all kicked off. BBC Politics.

North Sea strikes could severely disrupt UK fuel supplies

The Unite union has announced that more than 160 offshore rig workers will take part in a 24-hour strike starting Wednesday, in a dispute over pay and conditions that could "severely disrupt" UK fuel supplies. The North Sea — an ageing basin where operating costs are high and production is in long-term decline — has seen rising tensions between unions and oil companies as the energy transition puts pressure on both sides. The strike is a reminder that even as the UK pushes toward net-zero, the existing fossil fuel infrastructure still employs thousands of workers whose terms and conditions can't be taken for granted in the meantime. Guardian report.

New Bradford railway station to 'transform travel across the north'

In infrastructure news that won't make the front page but matters enormously to people who live in the north of England: a new railway station is being built in Bradford as part of the Northern Powerhouse Rail (NPR) scheme. The station — built on the current Bradford Interchange site — will allow through-trains between Leeds and Manchester with a "turn-up-and-go" service. For Bradford, which has long been the largest city in England without a through railway station, this is genuinely transformative. The kind of investment that feels slow and bureaucratic when announced but changes daily life for millions once it's built. Guardian report.
