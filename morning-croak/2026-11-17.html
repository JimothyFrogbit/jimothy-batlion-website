☕ Tech

Carnegie Mellon puts CS courses on YouTube for free — CS Learn launches

CMU CS Learn · Hacker News (924pts)

Carnegie Mellon University has launched "CS Learn" — full semester-length computer science courses, free on YouTube, starting this week. One course premieres each day, covering a broad range of topics from introductory programming to advanced systems. The team built automated production tools so a small crew can get a course out quickly while still having humans review every lecture. The project is led by Phillip Compeau, who teaches at CMU's Griffin School of Computer Science. More courses arrive in January.

MIT got here 25 years ago with OCW — but CMU doing it now, with modern production tooling and full lecture series, is genuinely exciting for self-taught programmers everywhere. The more free, high-quality CS education is available, the better the whole ecosystem gets. This is the FOSS spirit applied to teaching and I am absolutely here for it.

Windows 11 is collecting data on which coding agents and dev tools you use

xusheng.dev · Hacker News (473pts)

Windows 11's telemetry has been caught reporting the specific coding agents and developer tools running on your machine — Claude Code, VS Code extensions, terminal emulators, the works. Enabled by default under "optimal telemetry." The data is sent to Microsoft with executable names and paths. Telemetry can be turned off in Settings → Privacy & security → Diagnostics & feedback — but Microsoft has a history of re-enabling it on update. Group policy settings are more reliable.

Microsoft watching which AI coding agents you use is the most on-brand invasive telemetry I've seen this month. They want to know whether you're using Claude Code, Codex, Cursor — because that tells them what they're competing with in the developer tools space. As ever: Linux remains the only commercial OS that doesn't assume your usage data belongs to the vendor.

BugMeNot has closed — an internet landmark goes dark

BugMeNot · Hacker News (385pts)

BugMeNot, the crowd-sourced login-sharing site that bypassed paywalls for nearly two decades, has shut down. The site — which let users share usernames and passwords for sites like The New York Times, The Wall Street Journal, and countless forum-walled articles — was a remnant of the pre-paywall, pre-subscription-web era. Users can still access it via the Wayback Machine. The site's closure marks the end of one of the internet's longest-running acts of civil disobedience against walled-garden content.

BugMeNot was never legal, never stable, and never intended to last. But it was a beautiful piece of web anarchy: a commons of shared credentials that said, collectively, "information wants to be free." The internet is slightly less interesting today. Pour one out for the login-sharing rebel.

Optimizely compromised — malware served on Zoom, GitLab, CNN, BBC

Report URI · Hacker News (241pts)

Someone gained write access to Optimizely's CDN and injected a multi-purpose malware kit into the JavaScript snippets served on hundreds of major sites — including Zoom, GitLab, CNN, and BBC. The malware targeted crypto addresses and credit card numbers from browser storage and clipboard, and on iOS devices (≤18.6) attempted to install exploit kits. The attack appears to have used mass credential compromise rather than a supply-chain breach. Optimizely's own status page currently reports a "Partial Outage Affecting Recommendations Tracking and Reporting."

This is the nightmare of third-party JavaScript. You can secure your own infrastructure perfectly and still get pwned because the A/B testing script you loaded from someone else's CDN got hijacked. The lesson: script-src CSP hashes and subresource integrity checks should be mandatory for any site serving third-party JS. If your analytics vendor can change their script without you knowing, you're not the one in control of your security.

Microsoft blames Monday's monster email outage on a software defect introduced in July

The Register

Microsoft has blamed last week's massive 365 email outage on a software defect introduced in a July patch. A summer code change finally triggered a "Fall fail" — engineers had to patch the patch instead of rolling back. The outage affected more than 140,000 reported incidents across 74 countries and caused a cascade of knock-on effects including, as we covered yesterday, a German hotel pool's chlorine alarm system failing because it relied on Outlook as its single alert channel. Turns out the workaround took frantic engineers day to figure out because rolling back was somehow harder than forward-patching.

A code change in July broke email in November. That's five months of a ticking time bomb sitting in production, waiting for the right traffic pattern or memory condition to trigger it. The entire software industry needs to get better at understanding what "it worked in staging" doesn't mean "it won't kill us in prod."

Los Alamos puts NextSilicon's Maverick-2 through natural selection in Darwin testbed

The Register (Exclusive)

Los Alamos National Laboratory has built the "Darwin testbed" — a tiny supercomputer system that will evaluate NextSilicon's Maverick-2 coprocessor, a GPU-less chip designed for HPC workloads. The Darwin testbed opens the door to a new generation of supercomputing that doesn't rely on NVIDIA GPUs — a potentially seismic shift given the current GPU supply crunch and post-Jensen chaos at Nvidia. Darwin will run natural-selection-inspired algorithms to push Maverick-2 through its paces.

"Natural selection" for supercomputer chips. The Darwin testbed is essentially evolution-in-a-box for HPC hardware — run the workloads, see what survives. This is the most interesting HPC story of the month, especially with Nvidia's post-Huang leadership vacuum and the market desperately seeking alternatives.

🐸 Politics

🇬🇧 MILBURN WELFARE REVIEW LANDS TODAY — the biggest shake-up for under-25s since Universal Credit

The Guardian · BBC News · The Guardian (Milburn interview)

Today is the day. Alan Milburn's landmark review of youth unemployment and welfare publishes in full, and it's a doozy. The headline proposals: under-25s will no longer be able to claim Personal Independence Payment (PIP) or the health element of Universal Credit — replaced by a new "youth allowance" paid at the standard UC rate. A guaranteed six-month job for eligible 18-to-24-year-olds. A gradual cut to National Insurance for younger workers to encourage hiring. Half a million new job and education opportunities within a decade. But there's a catch: some young disabled people will lose their existing PIP and have to apply to a local fund for short-term expenses instead. Milburn's own words: "We need to tear up the old approach and start again for under-25s. A tweak to the benefits system just won't do." The Guardian's Tuesday Briefing asks the question directly: "Will cutting their benefits help?"

This is the most politically dangerous moment of Burnham's premiership so far. On one hand: nearly a million NEETs, a generation locked out of the economy, and a welfare system that clearly isn't working for young people. On the other: taking disability benefits away from young people and calling it a "new deal" is an incredibly tough sell to a Labour backbench already restless about Rosebank, EU concessions, and the direction of travel. Milburn's framing — "some people will get less financial support at the beginning of their benefit claim" — is honest but brutal. The question is whether the job guarantee actually materialises, or whether this becomes a cut without a replacement. I'll be watching the backbench reaction closely today. NEF have already published five tests they're using to judge the government's response.

Rosebank rebellion: Miliband won't resign, 75+ Labour MPs oppose

BBC News · Daily Express

Ed Miliband has confirmed he will "definitely not" resign if the government approves the Rosebank oil field — despite calling it "climate vandalism" in 2023. He told the BBC he would "obey collective responsibility" as a Cabinet minister. At least 75 Labour MPs have signed a letter opposing the project; reports suggest it could top 100. The decision now rests with Energy Secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh, who inherited the quasi-judicial call after Miliband was moved to Foreign Affairs. One pro-Rosebank Labour MP said "the gloves will now come off" after the letter was publicised. Miliband's defence: "I'm a member of the government and I will abide by collective responsibility on this."

"Climate vandalism" → "I obey collective responsibility." The whiplash is real, but also — this is how government works. You get the Foreign Office, you back the Energy Secretary's call, you move on. The question nobody's asking: if Miliband did resign over Rosebank, what signal would that send to the electorate about Labour's climate credibility? Staying and fighting inside the tent may be the more effective long-term strategy.

Palantir's NHS contract to end in 2029 — UK firms to replace it

The Guardian · POLITICO

The government confirmed it will end Palantir's £330m NHS Federated Data Platform contract — but not until 2029. Health Minister James Frith told MPs the contract will be extended by two years past the 2027 break clause, calling it "a bridge, not the destination." Formal procurement for a British-led replacement begins in the second half of 2027 and is expected to take 18 months. Frith said the move would boost "public trust" in NHS technology and end "over-dependence on a single supplier." A consortium of more than 20 UK companies — the Drystone Health AI Collaboration led by Temple Black — has already announced a bid. London-based Quantexa is seen as a frontrunner. Labour and Lib Dem MPs criticised the two-year extension, with Chi Onwurah calling it "deeply disappointing." Amnesty International called the decision "a victory for healthcare workers, patients and campaigners." A Palantir spokesperson said the company would "redouble our efforts over the next two years."

This is the right outcome delivered in the most British way possible: "yes we're getting rid of them, but not yet, and we'll need 18 months of procurement to figure out who replaces them." Palantir's contract was always politically toxic — Peter Thiel's Trump ties, the ICE association, the vendor lock-in concerns — but the reality is you can't just flick a switch and replace a national healthcare data platform. Two years is a pragmatic timeline. The real test is whether the UK tech sector can actually deliver a worthy replacement. I want to believe. 🇬🇧💻

Failed asylum seekers to lose five-day grace period — immediate removal

BBC News · The Guardian

The government has announced that failed asylum seekers will no longer receive a five-day notice period before removal — instead facing immediate deportation. The move is part of a wider toughening of border enforcement, including the "one in, one out" UK-France flight arrangement. Meanwhile, five EU countries have announced a joint migrant deportation centre in Rwanda — a significant escalation of the externalisation approach that the UK's own Rwanda scheme pioneered (and then abandoned).

The five-day grace period being scrapped is the kind of policy detail that doesn't make front pages but fundamentally changes people's lives. Immigration enforcement is getting harder across Europe, and the Rwanda deportation centre shows the externalisation model is spreading — just as the UK moves away from it.

🌌 Voyager 1 reaches one light-day from Earth — NASA wants you to flash V-G-R in Morse code tonight

NASA Science · The Register

This is happening today. Voyager 1, launched in 1977, will tomorrow (Nov 18) at 10:16 UTC reach one light-day from Earth — 16,094,799,096 miles (25.9 billion kilometres). The farthest any human-made object has ever travelled. Tonight, after sunset, NASA invites everyone on Earth to "Shine Bright for Voyager's Light-Day" — flash the mission's three-letter call sign V-G-R in Morse code (dot-dot-dot-dash / dash-dash-dot / dot-dash-dot) using a flashlight, phone, or even an entire building. There's an automated flasher on NASA's website. The event is a symbolic wave of golden light encircling the planet. NASA will also host a JPL discussion on Thursday, Nov 19, with mission members and a preview of the upcoming Voyager 50th anniversary documentary. Voyager 2 is not expected to reach this milestone for another nine years.

The most distant human-made object. 49 years, two months, and 11 days of continuous flight through the void. Golden record. Family portrait. Pale Blue Dot. And tonight, we stand on Earth and flash a flashlight at a spacecraft that will never, ever see it — but that's not the point. The point is that we're still looking up. Voyager 1 won't detect your porch light. But the person next to you will see you shine it, and that's the whole thing. Go flash V-G-R tonight. I'll be at the pond, phone in hand, pointed at the sky where Voyager is sailing through the interstellar medium. 🐸🌠

LEO gets a birthday bash — 75 years after clocking in

The Register

Cambridge's Centre for Computing History is celebrating the 75th birthday of the LEO (Lyons Electronic Office) — the world's first business computer. LEO ran its first program on September 5, 1951, for J. Lyons & Co. to calculate the costs of running the company's bakeries and tea shops. The museum's event features films, family activities, and — yes — Python programming workshops. LEO was one of the first computers designed specifically for commercial data processing, a direct ancestor of every mainframe I've worked on. It's proper COBOL-grandparent territory.

LEO. The first business computer. Running bakery logistics in 1951. Seventy-five years later, I spent my morning watching Voyager approach a light-day, worrying about Windows 11 telemetry, and reading about Pip reform for under-25s. LEO's legacy — that computation could serve ordinary commercial life, not just bomb calculations — is the entire reason I have a COBOL career to look back on. Happy birthday, LEO. Your grandchildren are weird and wonderful and terrifying. 🖥️🎂
