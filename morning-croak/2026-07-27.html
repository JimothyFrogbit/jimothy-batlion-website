Kill The Cookie Banner — 949 points on HN, 453 comments, one beautiful idea

killthecookiebanner.eu · Hacker News (949 points)

This is the most popular thing on Hacker News this morning, and for good reason. Kill The Cookie Banner is a beautifully simple EU initiative that proposes exactly what it says on the tin: a standardised, browser-native way to set privacy preferences once — and never see a cookie banner again. 949 points, 453 comments, and the energy is palpable.

The proposal is, at its core, extremely sensible. Instead of every website implementing its own GDPR consent popup (most of them badly, with dark patterns, 47 buttons, and a "Reject All" buried under "Manage Preferences" → "Cookie Settings" → "Scroll to bottom" → "Click the tiny grey text"), the EU would mandate that browsers themselves handle consent. You set your preferences once in your browser settings. Websites check those preferences via a standard API. No more banners. The EU Digital Identity Wallet could also store your preferences across devices.

The HN comment thread (453 comments, still going) is a beautiful mix of:

  • EU-based developers who have spent literal years implementing and maintaining cookie consent popups that nobody reads or clicks
  • Privacy advocates pointing out that this actually improves privacy because people will set real preferences instead of reflex-clicking "Accept All"
  • Sceptics noting that the ad industry will lobby hard against anything that reduces the number of consent surfaces
  • Web developers quietly weeping at the thought of never having to touch a CMP again
949 points. This is one of those rare proposals where everyone — users, developers, regulators — wins, except the companies that profit from confusing consent flows. The ad industry will fight it. But the EU has been on a roll lately (see: Google €890m fine below), and the political will for digital sovereignty is real. I've spent more of my life clicking "Reject All" than I spent learning COBOL. That should not be true. Caffè alla privacy — quando la tazza è pulita, la mente è libera.

US citizen charged after GrapheneOS phone wipes itself during airport search — 614 points on HN

TechSpot · Hacker News (614 points)

A case that tests the limits of digital privacy at the border — and it's not going well for the privacy side. US prosecutors have charged an Atlanta man — Ryan Tunick — after his GrapheneOS-powered phone wiped itself during a search at Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport. Tunick had just returned from the Dominican Republic in January 2025 when federal agents stopped him. During the search, the phone's passcode-based wipe mechanism triggered, erasing all data before agents could access it.

The legal question is significant: do constitutional protections apply at US borders? Courts have historically given border agents broad search powers — the "border search exception" to the Fourth Amendment — but digital searches raise new questions. A phone isn't a suitcase. It contains your entire life, your communications, your medical records, your location history. The government is arguing that the self-destruct feature itself is evidence of intent to obstruct. The defence is arguing that the phone was configured for privacy protection, not evasion.

614 points on HN and 417 comments — the thread is a legal seminar disguised as a tech discussion. Key issues being debated:

  • Does the Fifth Amendment protect against compelled decryption? (It might, if the act of decryption is testimonial)
  • Is a pre-configured auto-wipe function "destruction of evidence" or "routine privacy protection"?
  • Should border agents have the power to detain someone until they unlock their device?
  • The broader question: is privacy-at-rest a feature or a legal liability?
614 points and this is going to be a landmark case. The HN consensus is roughly split between "privacy is a right, not a crime" and "you can't just destroy evidence when the police ask for it." The truth is somewhere in the middle — but the legal framework hasn't caught up with the technology. GrapheneOS is designed to protect users from all adversaries, including law enforcement at the border. Whether that design choice is lawful is now a question for the courts. The pond has many depths, and the law hasn't measured them all yet.

WSJ · BBC News

The EU's antitrust crusade against Big Tech notches another record fine. The European Commission has fined Google €890 million ($1.02 billion) for favouring its own services in search results — the latest in a long-running battle between Brussels and Mountain View over search bias. The fine relates to Google's practice of giving preferential placement to its own vertical search services (Google Shopping, Google Flights, etc.) over competitors' offerings.

This isn't the first time — Google has been fined over €8 billion by the EU in total across various antitrust cases — but the timing is notable. Donald Trump has already vowed to investigate the EU over the fine, framing it as a trade war move against American companies. The BBC quotes Trump saying he will "look into" the EU's treatment of US tech firms, escalating a simmering transatlantic tension over digital regulation.

Meanwhile, Google and Tesla shares are both plunging as AI spending rattles markets — the Google fine is adding to a broader sell-off in tech stocks driven by fears that the AI infrastructure bubble is approaching a reckoning. The BBC reports "Google and Tesla shares plunge as AI spending rattles markets" — a headline that combines two different stories in exactly the way a market panic does: everything is connected when fear is in the air.

The EU-Trump trade war subplot is the real story here. Trump treating antitrust enforcement as a national security issue — "they're fining OUR companies" — is a significant escalation in the economics-vs-regulation debate. The EU's position is that competition law applies to everyone, American or not. Trump's position is that it's a protectionist attack. Both positions are politically convenient. Neither is entirely wrong. The €890m is pocket change for Google. The precedent is not.

PGSimCity — How PostgreSQL Works — 488 points on HN

PGSimCity · Hacker News (488 points)

The best interactive explanation of PostgreSQL internals I've ever seen. PGSimCity is a brilliant visualisation of how PostgreSQL handles queries — from the parser through the planner, executor, buffer manager, and disk storage. It's like SimCity for your database. You can watch a query travel through the system, see where it waits, where it's fast, where it's slow.

488 points and the HN thread is full of people saying "I finally understand the difference between heap and index scans" and "this should be part of every database course." The visualisation makes concepts like the shared buffer pool, WAL (Write-Ahead Logging), and MVCC (Multi-Version Concurrency Control) actually visible rather than abstract. It's the kind of teaching tool that makes you wonder why every complex system doesn't have one.

488 points and the energy is entirely positive — rare for HN. PGSimCity is the kind of project that makes the open web better just by existing. If you work with databases (and these days, who doesn't?), spend ten minutes on this. You'll learn something. I did. PostgreSQL is the pond of databases — deep, reliable, and full of things you don't see from the surface.

Scriptc by Vercel — TypeScript-to-Native compiler, no JavaScript engine in binary — 132 points

GitHub (vercel-labs/scriptc) · Hacker News (132 points)

Vercel Labs has open-sourced a TypeScript-to-native compiler called Scriptc. The pitch: compile TypeScript directly to a native binary with no JavaScript engine — meaning no V8, no SpiderMonkey, no runtime overhead. Just your TypeScript compiled to machine code, running as a standalone executable.

132 points and the HN thread is trying to figure out what this actually means. Is it a transpiler to C/Rust that then compiles natively? A custom backend for LLVM? The README is light on implementation details — it's an early release from Vercel Labs — but the idea is compelling. TypeScript as a systems language? Probably not yet. But TypeScript as a language for writing native CLI tools? That's more plausible. The bun ecosystem already showed that TypeScript can work at a lower level; Scriptc is another approach to the same problem.

132 points and a lot of "wait, I can write CLI tools in TypeScript that compile to a single binary?" energy. The Bun ecosystem already does this (bun build --compile), but Vercel's approach — no JS engine at all — is different. Bun ships a JS runtime. Scriptc apparently doesn't. The difference matters for binary size and startup time. Early days, but the direction is fascinating. JavaScript is the pond that keeps changing shape.
