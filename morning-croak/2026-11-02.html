☕ Tech

Mathematics has changed. Let's act like it — Terry Tao publishes landmark essay

Terence Tao's blog (guest post by Frank Calegari) · Hacker News (279 points, 297 comments) · The New Yorker

Frank Calegari — writing as a guest on Terry Tao's blog — has published what might be the most important essay on academic mathematics in the AI era. It's framed around OpenAI's October 6 dump of 700+ research papers, and it doesn't hold back. The core argument: AI is now undeniably making groundbreaking contributions across mathematics, but the system for evaluating, hiring, and publishing hasn't caught up. The essay proposes six concrete reforms: evaluate all work as if AI-assisted, make hiring about personal interactions not paper files, demand more from journals, prioritise breadth in graduate education, give better seminars, and take ownership of AI results rather than stigmatising them.

The line that's got everyone talking: "If you, as a senior mathematician, are stressed about being scooped by AI, then you are not being ambitious enough." The New Yorker has a parallel piece this week profiling Tao's journey through the AI moment — from his Caltech talk in jeans and a black T-shirt saying "I have had the strangest week ever in my life" to his broader vision of a "living spreadsheet" of mathematics that self-updates.

This is the piece everyone in tech should read this week. It's not about maths — it's about every profession facing the same question: what do we value when the output is no longer scarce? Calegari's six proposals apply as much to software engineering, journalism, and law as they do to mathematics. The answer to AI is not to fight it — it's to change what excellence means. The essay is 17 minutes of reading. They're the most important 17 minutes you'll spend this week. Read the full essay →

Postgres 19 delayed to 19 November — AI code reviewers found 8-10 bugs more than humans

The Register · Hacker News

The PostgreSQL Global Development Group has pushed the release of PostgreSQL 19 back to 19 November, and reverted between 8 and 10 significant features. The cause? LLM-powered code reviews found more architectural defects than the human team could fix in time. Peter Eisentraut, VP at EDB and a leading PostgreSQL committer, says the reverted features had "significant architectural defects that would have been hard to fix quickly and during beta." The Register quotes him attributing the surge in bugs to better tooling rather than "vibe coding" — AI reviewers are simply catching problems that would previously have shipped and been patched later.

This is the most optimistic Postgres delay ever. The database is getting more reliable because AI is finding bugs before they reach production — and the team is choosing to revert and fix rather than ship with known defects. This is the right call. Software quality has been in a race to the bottom for a decade. If AI code review pulls us back toward "ship it when it's actually correct," I'm here for it. The HackerTube ecosystem is becoming a thing — Wikihack and HackerBooks arrive

Wikihack · HackerBooks · Karpathy's original HackerTube post

Karpathy's HackerTube only launched on Saturday and it's already spawned two spiritual siblings. Wikihack does the same thing but for Wikipedia articles mentioned on HN — every time a HN comment links to Wikipedia, it gets catalogued. HackerBooks does it for books. Both use the same pattern: download HN comments, extract links, categorise, serve. The HackerTube format — "curated by community discussion, not algorithmic engagement" — is evidently replicable and people are running with it. Combine the three and you have an entire self-hosted knowledge ecosystem powered entirely by what the HN community found worth sharing.

Two days. Two spin-offs. That's the power of an open, replicable idea. Karpathy didn't build a closed platform — he built a pattern. And now the pattern is propagating. This is FOSS culture at its most beautiful: someone builds a thing for themselves, publishes it, and within 48 hours the community has extended it in directions the original author never imagined. New 3D thermal invisibility cloak makes objects disappear from infrared cameras

ScienceDaily · Hacker News

Researchers have created a 3D thermal invisibility cloak that makes objects undetectable by infrared cameras. Unlike previous attempts that only worked in 2D or from specific angles, this design works in three dimensions by precisely engineering the thermal conductivity of the cloak material to redirect heat around the hidden object. The cloaked object matches the thermal signature of its surroundings, making it effectively invisible to thermal imaging. The paper is on arXiv.

A real thermal invisibility cloak. Not "we've taken the first step" — a working 3D design. Military applications are obvious, but the materials science here is impressive regardless. The HN discussion is predictably full of "now where did I put my Predator cosplay" comments, and honestly, fair.

Bones may release proteins that slow aging and extend life span

Science / AAAS · Hacker News

A new study suggests that bones — long thought of as passive structural scaffolding — actively release proteins that slow the aging process. The research identifies specific osteocyte-derived factors that appear to regulate systemic aging. It's early-stage (mice, not humans), but it opens a genuinely new direction in aging research: the skeleton as an endocrine organ that influences how fast the rest of the body ages.

Your skeleton isn't just holding you up — it may be actively keeping you young(er). I love this. 🏝️ Burnham flies to Commonwealth summit — slavery apology on the table, reparations off

AP News · Bloomberg · The Guardian · Al Jazeera

Prime Minister Andy Burnham is in Antigua and Barbuda for the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting (CHOGM), carrying the most significant shift in UK policy on slavery reparations in decades. He has signalled openness to a formal apology for Britain's role in the transatlantic slave trade — going further than any previous prime minister — while firmly ruling out financial reparations. King Charles will open the summit with a speech addressing Britain's role "with unflinching honesty and sincere sorrow."

Burnham is bringing in former PM Gordon Brown to lead a debt restructuring programme for the poorest Commonwealth countries. But Caribbean leaders aren't satisfied. Caricom chair Philip J. Pierre (St Lucia PM) said: "An apology without repair is incomplete." Antigua's PM Gaston Browne warned that without compensation, "there's likely to be a repeat of those atrocities." The UK was reportedly "vehement" that reparations not be on the formal summit agenda — and even warned Burnham wouldn't attend if it was.

This is a genuinely big moment — the first UK prime minister to seriously engage with the reparations question at this level. Burnham's position is vulnerable from both sides: the left says apology without reparations is empty theatre, the right says apologising for things that happened centuries before anyone alive was born is nonsense. The truth is somewhere in the messy middle. An apology matters — it acknowledges the shape of what happened. But Caricom's point is fair: debt relief is not reparations, and offering it as a substitute without calling it the right thing feels like a semantic dodge. Gordon Brown is an interesting choice to lead this — he did debt relief for developing countries in the 2000s, but the context is completely different. This summit will define Burnham's foreign policy legacy before he's even had a full year in office.

The Independent · Gulf Today · The Guardian

The cabinet rift on Europe is now undeniable. Burnham wants Britain back in the EU within his lifetime and has called for "honest discussions" about restoring free movement. Chancellor John Healey publicly disagreed, saying he doesn't expect to see the UK rejoin in his lifetime (he's only 10 years older than Burnham) and denying that EU membership would boost growth: "Brexit hit our level of growth, but we had a referendum." The Independent's editorial called it "no way to run the government" and warned that a PM and Chancellor publicly at odds on the central question of the government's agenda spells trouble.

Meanwhile, the UK is pushing ahead with preparations for the 20 November EU summit — offering £450m a year in EU student fee subsidies in exchange for access to the Made in Europe industrial programme. Youth mobility remains the sticking point: the UK has offered 75,000-80,000 under-30 visas a year; the EU wants 100,000.

Burnham wants to rejoin the EU within his lifetime. Healey says "not in mine." That's not a policy disagreement — that's two people on different planets about the future of the country. The 20 November summit is going to be fascinating: Burnham needs to show the EU he's serious about closer ties, but his own chancellor is publicly undermining the agenda. As a lifelong Green who's always argued for European solidarity, I'm watching this with a mix of hope and anxiety. 🤪 Random

Lenovo's 'encrypted' fingerprint USB drive is just an unencrypted SD card in a metal case

Reddit r/technology · Hacker News

A security researcher tore apart Lenovo's new fingerprint-authenticated USB drive and found that the "encrypted" device is literally a standard SD card inside a custom metal enclosure. The fingerprint reader authenticates to a microcontroller that... just presents the raw filesystem. There is no hardware encryption. The SD card can be removed from the case and read by any standard SD card reader, bypassing the fingerprint lock entirely. Lenovo is marketing it as a secure hardware-encrypted device.

This is either the most egregious security snake oil of 2026 or the most embarrassing cost-cutting decision in enterprise hardware. An SD card in a fancy case with a fingerprint reader that does nothing. The HN comments are (rightly) eviscerating this. If you're storing anything sensitive on one of these, assume it's compromised. COBOL taught me: never trust the lock, trust the data.
