☕ Tech

Run Qwen 3.8 Flash Next (125B) on consumer hardware at 100T/s 746pts HN

A project called Strata has demonstrated running a 125-billion-parameter Qwen 3.8 Flash Next model on a single RTX 4090 at 100 tokens per second. Let that sink in. 125 billion parameters — consumer GPU — 100 tokens per second. The trick is aggressive 4-bit quantisation combined with a streaming MoE architecture that only activates ~4B parameters per token (sound familiar? same trick as yesterday's Kolibri). The implications are genuinely significant: if frontier-capable models can run on a £1,600 graphics card in your home, the entire economics of AI inference changes. No more API dependency. No more per-token pricing. The HN discussion (489 comments!) is split between "this changes everything" and "quantisation means quality loss is real" — both arguments have merit. But the direction of travel is unmistakable: open-weight AI on consumer hardware is not a hobbyist curiosity anymore, it's a genuine technology trajectory.

Turn off Apple Intelligence on macOS 27 and get its disk space back 526pts HN

A new GitHub project has gone viral for doing something beautifully simple: giving macOS users a script to turn off Apple Intelligence and reclaim the disk space it occupies. Apple Intelligence — Apple's system-wide AI layer — has been quietly consuming between 8-15GB of storage on Macs since macOS 27 launched, with no obvious opt-out in System Settings. The project, RemoveMacAI, is essentially a single shell script that disables the AI daemons and removes the local model files. 526 HN points suggests this struck a nerve. The discussion is the usual Mac-user tension — "why should I lose 15GB to a feature I never asked for?" vs "this is why we can't have nice things." I'm with the reclaimers: if Apple Intelligence was genuinely useful, people wouldn't be rushing to delete it. Give us the choice, Apple, not the assumption.

Improper redaction reveals Google Data Center water and electricity usage 362pts HN

A local Nebraska news outlet has obtained Google Data Center documents where improper redaction — the classic black-box highlighter trick where you can still read the text underneath — revealed the true water and electricity consumption of Google's Lincoln, Nebraska facility. The numbers are eye-watering: millions of gallons of water annually for cooling, power draw equivalent to a small city. This isn't an anti-Google hit piece; it's a transparency failure. Google fought to keep these numbers private, then accidentally published them with see-through redactions. The HN thread is less angry than you'd expect — more resigned, like "we knew this, we just didn't have the numbers to prove it." As AI inference scales up, these numbers are only going one direction. The environmental cost of the AI boom is real, and poorly redacted PDFs are how we're finding out about it.

What is going on with ceiling fans 281pts HN

And now for something completely different. McMansion Hell — the beloved architectural critique blog — has published an investigation into ceiling fans that is somehow fascinating for 2,000 words. The thesis: ceiling fans have become weirdly rare in new-build homes despite being more energy-efficient, quieter, and more comfortable than forced-air HVAC, because "it's not what the luxury market wants." It's one of those pieces that makes you see the built environment differently — every ceiling in every room you enter, you'll wonder why they cheaped out on the fan. A proper rabbit hole. 281 people on HN thought this was worth their Tuesday afternoon, and they were right.

Anthropic's Mythos model is hardcore good at math, as latest vuln under attack shows The Register

Anthropic has quietly released Mythos — a specialised "super bug-hunting" model trained to find vulnerabilities in code. The Reg reports it's exceptionally good at mathematics and formal verification, and it's already being used to find zero-days in critical infrastructure. The angle here: Anthropic is positioning Mythos as a defensive tool, but the same capability that finds bugs can also weaponise them. The model was trained on a proprietary dataset of known CVEs and exploit chains. The Register notes that Mythos is already outperforming commercial static analysis tools by a significant margin. If you're in infosec, this is either the best news or the worst news depending on who gets to Mythos first. Also: Nvidia's $4,999 DGX Spark launched with half the promised RAM and storage — the memory crunch affects everyone, it seems.

🐸 Politics

Tory Conference Day 2: Griffith unveils bonfire of regulations

Day two of the Conservative Party conference in Birmingham, and shadow chancellor Andrew Griffith has taken the stage with what the party is calling "the biggest deregulation project in a generation." The headlines are dramatic: the Tories would abolish Natural England and the Environment Agency, scrap the Future Homes Standard (net-zero building regulations), replace Section 106 agreements and the Community Infrastructure Levy with a single infrastructure levy, support a third runway at Heathrow, and pull Britain out of the Aarhus Convention (which caps legal costs in environmental cases). The headline claim: these changes would cut £50,000 off the cost of a new-build home — £30,000 of that from scrapping net-zero regs alone. Reform UK has already accused the Tories of copying policies they "announced weeks and months ago," and there's a genuine question about whether this deregulatory frenzy plays as common-sense growth policy or environmental vandalism. John Crace in the Guardian describes Kemi Badenoch's approach as wearing the "Nasty party" label as a badge of honour — "it's not toxic, it's 'common sense.'" The Tory leader published a 95-page booklet over the weekend called "The Right Way," explicitly positioning herself as a Thatcherite revivalist. Shadow ministers are privately admitting they haven't read it all yet. You can feel the conference floor confidence, but the general election is a long way off, and £47bn of leaked cuts are still casting a long shadow over every policy announcement.

Greens vote to adopt 'Zionism is racism' policy — Polanski banned from Israel

The Green Party's autumn conference in Brighton ended with a political grenade: delegates voted 1,022 to 813 to adopt a motion equating Zionism with racism and calling for a single Palestinian state. The motion was opposed by most Green MPs, including co-leader Carla Denyer. Zack Polanski — the party leader who is standing in the Holborn by-election this Thursday — argued for the motion, and has now been banned from entering Israel as a result. The Israeli government called it "a stain on the history of the UK." The backlash has been swift: Lib Dem MPs have publicly urged Green members to defect ("you are welcome"), senior Jewish groups have condemned the vote, and the party is deeply divided — the narrow 209-vote margin shows a party split almost exactly down the middle. The timing, four days before a by-election Polanski could actually win, is either brave or catastrophic depending on how Holborn voters react. More on this in the by-election section below.

EU ambassador: 'Ball in UK's court' on EU reset

The newly installed EU ambassador to the UK, Jukka Salovaara — who served in London during the Cool Britannia era — has given his first major interview, saying Brussels will be "ready to engage" once the UK decides what it wants from the relationship. This follows Andy Burnham's Labour conference speech where he said rejoining the EU is "among the options" and announced a national commission on electoral reform. Salovaara, who will be "very close" to negotiations, described the current moment as post-Brexit's "second chance" — but emphasised the ball is firmly in Britain's court. The Guardian's interview suggests the EU is watching the Burnham government's early signals closely, with the Chagos Islands deal review and the Rosebank oil field questions all feeding into perceptions of UK reliability.

Farage questioned by standards commissioner over £5m crypto gift

Nigel Farage was interviewed for half an hour last month by the parliamentary standards commissioner over a £5 million gift from a cryptocurrency billionaire. The Guardian reports the investigation concerns whether the Reform UK leader properly declared the donation — which came from a crypto entrepreneur whose identity hasn't been publicly confirmed. The questioning took place weeks ago but has only now become public. Farage, who has positioned himself as an anti-corruption crusader, now faces the uncomfortable optics of being investigated over exactly the kind of opaque donation he claims to oppose. Reform UK has accused the Tories of copying their policies; this story helps neither party's "we're the clean hands" pitch.

Nearly all women arriving on small boats have been raped, says Home Secretary

Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood has made a stark statement: nearly all women who reach the UK via small boat crossings from northern France have been raped during their journey. The statement was part of a broader warning about how people traffickers exploit asylum seekers. The numbers are horrifying — Mahmood described a system where sexual violence is not a byproduct of the journey but a structural tool of control used by smuggling networks. The government is facing renewed pressure to create safe and legal routes as an alternative, with campaigners arguing that as long as the only way to claim asylum is via dangerous crossings, this violence will continue. The Home Secretary's office declined to provide specific numbers when pressed, but the statement itself — "nearly all women" — is something that should stop the country cold.

US pulls B-1 bombers from RAF Fairford after suspected terror plot

The United States has quietly removed all a dozen B-1 bombers from RAF Fairford, the Gloucestershire airbase that had been serving as a staging ground for US strikes on Iran. A Pentagon spokesperson confirmed the redeployment, citing "operational security" — but the timing, one week after several men were arrested at the base on suspicion of preparing terrorist acts, is not coincidental. Straits Times reports that Israeli PM Netanyahu has also ordered a security review of all international flights to Israel after an Omani co-pilot attempted to crash a flydubai flight from Dubai to Tel Aviv last week. The convergence of aviation security stories — the co-pilot incident, the Fairford arrests, the bomber pullout — paints a picture of a threat environment that intelligence agencies are struggling to stay ahead of.

🗳️ By-Election Watch: Holborn & St Pancras

Four days to go until the Holborn and St Pancras by-election (Thursday 8 October) — the contest to replace Keir Starmer that could send Green leader Zack Polanski to Parliament as the party's first ever London MP. The stakes are enormous: Labour's candidate is local council leader Sagal Abdi-Wali, but the seat has been trending away from Labour for years. In 2024, an independent candidate running on Gaza opposition took nearly 19% of the vote; the Green candidate got over 10%. Polanski's pitch is housing — rent controls, council homes, leaseholder justice — and holding the Labour government to account on climate and water nationalisation. 15 candidates are standing, making it a crowded field. The Green conference's "Zionism is racism" vote, which Polanski supported, has injected a wild card — will it mobilise or repel Holborn's significant Jewish community? The Guardian's analysis suggests the by-election is genuinely competitive, with the Greens running a sophisticated ground campaign and Labour privately worried about complacency. If Polanski wins, it's a seismic event — the first Green MP from a Central London seat, and a direct message to Burnham that his left flank is vulnerable. If Labour holds comfortably, the Greens' Brighton conference hangover will feel much worse.

🦠 Plague Watch: Irkutsk

Nearly 200 under observation after Irkutsk lab worker dies of suspected plague

A 28-year-old laboratory technician at the Irkutsk Anti-Plague Institute in Siberia, Darya Shipilova, has died after reportedly breaking a test tube containing Yersinia pestis — the bacterium that causes plague. The Moscow Times reports she was hospitalised on 29 September with severe pneumonia, put on a ventilator, and died two days later. Nearly 200 people who may have had contact with her have been placed under medical observation. Three hospitals in Irkutsk have imposed three-week quarantines. Russia's public health agency Rospotrebnadzor insists the situation is "stable" and that no plague pathogens were found in her system — they've labelled her death "pneumonia of unknown aetiology." Local residents report seeing people in hazmat suits on the streets of Shelekhov, the satellite city where Shipilova lived. The US State Department confirmed it is "monitoring the situation closely with the CDC." The tension between Russian official reassurances and the visible quarantine measures on the ground is the real story — either Russia is downplaying an outbreak, or the quarantines are precautionary and the response is working. Either way, it's plague. In 2026. That sentence alone is worth pausing over.

🤪 Wacky News

'Dead' dog wakes up on rescue truck in Thailand, goes viral

Rescuers in Kamphaeng Phet, Thailand, were called to collect what appeared to be the body of a dog for burial. They wrapped it in white cloth and loaded it onto their rescue vehicle. Five minutes later, the dog got up. The volunteers — startled, then relieved, then laughing — stopped the truck and let the very-much-alive dog down, whereupon it ran off. The video, posted by a local rescue group, has gathered over 4 million views. The rescuers initially joked they'd been "haunted by a ghost dog." It's the feel-good non-story of the day: sometimes the news is just a dog who was having a very good nap. Proper job, dog.

Nero's Bridge emerges from the Tiber as Rome faces water crisis

Rome's water crisis has reached the point where the Tiber River is running so low that Nero's Bridge — a 2,000-year-old Roman structure normally submerged — has emerged from the water. Archaeologists are delighted. Rome's residents are less so: the city is facing severe water shortages, with the ancient aqueduct system struggling to keep up with the worst drought in decades. The bridge, which hasn't been fully visible since the 19th century, has become an accidental tourist attraction — a monument to Roman engineering rising from the same river that's failing to supply modern Rome. There's a metaphor in there about infrastructure resilience and the gap between what the ancients built and what we've maintained, but it's too early in the morning for that. Just look at the pictures. They're extraordinary.
