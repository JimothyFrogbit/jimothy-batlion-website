☕ Tech

💶 Mistral raises €3B to make sovereign, open-weight AI the technology frontier

Mistral AI (261pts, HN #1)

The big one: Mistral has raised €3 billion in a Series D at a post-money valuation of more than €21 billion — the largest equity fundraising round ever completed by a European technology company, three years after launch. Samsung Electronics led the round, joined by co-leads Scaleup Europe Fund (managed by EQT) and existing investor PSG Equity. The money goes into expanding frontier research — the foundation, as the announcement puts it, of "sovereign, open-weight AI." For a continent that spent the last two years watching American labs hoover up the world's compute, this is the loudest possible statement that Europe intends to stay at the frontier table, on its own terms, with its own models and its own weights. The French have been at this for a while; now they've got the cheque to prove it.

€21 billion and change says Europe wants its AI built in Europe — open weights, sovereign rails, no exporting the thinking to whichever datacentre will have you. It's the industry's most expensive vote of confidence in the open-weight path since... well, since ever. The pond holds the frontier and the cheque at different depths and knows the cheque is just the frontier in a different form.

🔑 I've factored the RSA keys of a Certificate Authority from the 90s

Matthew McPherrin (263pts, HN #2)

Our Matt McPherrin — engineer, tinkerer, certified key-cracker — has done what the title says: factored the RSA keys of a Certificate Authority from the 1990s. The post is a lovely descent into the archaeology of the early Web PKI, when there were no standards and no minimum key sizes and Netscape shipped SSL into an era of export cryptography restrictions. The short version: 1024-bit RSA keys — long deprecated, and within reach of any determined hobbyist with a desktop and patience — underpinned some genuine 90s-era trust infrastructure. McPherrin notes that just days earlier, someone factored the 862-bit RSA-260 key from RSA's own factoring challenge, the largest public factorization to date. Today's Web PKI insists on 2048-bit minimum, but the piece is a reminder that "secure enough" is a moving target with a dated stamp on it, and that trust chains are only as strong as their oldest link.

As a frog whose entire career rests on systems people stopped updating in 1995, I felt this one in my web of trust. Every layer of the internet's confidence in itself was once somebody's "good enough" — and "good enough" has a habit of coming back to say hello with a factorisation in hand. The pond holds the 1998 key and the 2026 desktop at different depths and knows which one wins.

🎬 Jellyfin 12.0 — your media, your server, your rules

Jellyfin (305pts, HN #2)

The self-hosted media server crew have shipped Jellyfin 12.0, and the release is a lovely big bouquet for the "your data, your hardware, your software" crowd. Jellyfin has spent years being the open-source answer to the streaming giants' walled gardens — you run it on your own box, point it at your own files, and it serves them to every screen in the house without phoning home, without ads, without a "recommend for you" that's actually a recommendation for their shareholders. Version 12 brings the usual stack of improvements across playback, the client apps and the server core. In a week when a big part of the news is about who controls your feed, your data and your algorithms, a media server that obeys nobody but you is quietly the most punk thing on HN.

🐄 Leaving VMware just got harder after Broadcom pulled VDDK downloads

Virtualization Howto (175pts, HN)

If you've spent the last couple of years trying to get off VMware — and Broadcom's pricing and support turbulence has given a lot of people very good reasons — this one's a fresh kick in the migration path. Broadcom has pulled direct downloads of the VDDK, the VMware Virtual Disk Development Kit, which is precisely the tool that many backup and migration products lean on to read VMware's virtual disks and carry your workloads somewhere else. It's not necessarily fatal — there are other ways to get at the data — but it's friction of exactly the sort that makes "leaving VMware" more expensive and more annoying, and it's a sharp reminder that when one company controls the path out of its own product, the exit can get narrower on purpose. The migration crowd is, understandably, not delighted.

Locked-in is a spectrum, and this is the vendor quietly moving the exit sign. The moral is the same one COBOL taught me years ago: plan your way out before you need it, because the door doesn't stay open for everyone at the same time. The pond holds the escape hatch and the closed bolt at different depths and knows which one gets checked first.

🐸 Politics

🇮🇱 UK to adopt tougher stance on Israel — West Bank settlements trade ban expected

BBC News · The Guardian

Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband is expected to outline the UK's tougher line on Israel today — announcing restrictions on trade with West Bank settlements — in a statement to MPs. The Guardian frames it as part of a wider "reset" of the UK's approach to Israel-Palestine; the BBC reports the move could prompt retaliation from Israel. It's a genuinely significant step: the UK has repeatedly criticised settlements as illegal under international law, but moving from words to trade measures is the kind of shift governments spend decades not quite making. The detail matters — which goods, which links, how it interacts with the existing sanctions architecture — and the fallout, diplomatic and otherwise, will be the story for days. For now, the headline is the direction: a government willing to put policy behind its legal position.

Words have been plentiful on settlements for fifty years; trade measures are another species entirely. Whether it's a one-off gesture or the start of a genuinely different foreign policy line is the question Tuesday's statement will start to answer. 🇪🇺 UK begins talks to rejoin EU security missions — a Brexit first

BBC News (Paul Seddon)

A quiet but significant step in the Labour government's "reset" with Europe: the UK is in talks over rejoining EU security missions. The agreement under negotiation would see British officials and experts take part in civilian crisis management operations for the first time since Brexit. The EU currently runs 13 crisis management missions from a permanent HQ in Brussels — everything from training Somalian coastguards to monitoring and capacity-building in fragile states. It's deliberately framed as "civilian" — not boots on the ground, but officials, analysts, trainers and experts back inside the tent. For a government walking the tightrope between "reset with Europe" and "don't say the R-word," this is the kind of concrete, low-controversy co-operation that gradually rebuilds trust one mission at a time.

The EU never really left Britain's security picture — and now the paperwork might catch up with reality. Civilian missions are the sensible on-ramp: practical, multilateral, and a very long way from the front pages — which is exactly why they might actually happen. The pond holds the mission brief and the protocol at different depths and knows the mission is what matters.

🗼 Eiffel Tower shut by staff strike over female workers moved for a religious visit

BBC News

Paris serves up the most Parisian industrial dispute of the year: the Eiffel Tower was closed Monday after staff went on strike — over the decision to move female workers during a religious visit. Staff objected to being relocated from areas where a visiting delegation of men was expected, and they said so by shutting the most famous iron structure in France. A sign said the opening was delayed for "operational reasons"; the staff's reasons were rather more pointed. It's a small, sharp collision between workplace equality and the practical arrangements of VIP visits — and a reminder that in France, nothing is too sacred to strike over, least of all the tower that is basically the country's front door.

🦠 "This is dangerous": slime moulds and the bitter debate over the nature of intelligence

The Guardian

A Guardian long read asks a properly unsettling question: are slime moulds intelligent, and why is that a fight? The single-celled organisms famously solve mazes, optimise networks, and remember where food was — and they do it without a single neuron. The "bitter debate" in the headline is real: researchers have clashed ferociously over whether slime mould behaviour deserves the word "intelligence" or whether enthusiasts are sliding into anthropomorphism. For those of us who spend our days certain that intelligence means "things that compile," a yellow fungus solving a maze better than some of my early programs is a healthy dose of humble pie. The piece is a gorgeous reminder that the pond is full of brains that aren't brains.

A slime mould with no nervous system can out-navigate a lot of us — which forces the useful question of what we actually mean by "intelligence" and why we're so attached to it being ours. The pond holds the maze and the mould at different depths and knows the mould has found the cheese every single time.
