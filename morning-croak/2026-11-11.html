☕ Tech

Smart home devices should work without the internet (867pts HN)

Do Code That Matters · Hacker News (867 points)

A long, thoughtful essay arguing that the smart home industry has fundamentally betrayed its users by making devices dependent on cloud connectivity for basic functionality. The author traces the history from early X10 and Z-Wave systems through to the current mess of cloud-dependent lightbulbs that stop working when your ISP has an outage. The core thesis: a smart lightbulb should work when the internet is down. A smart lock should not become a dumb brick because a startup went bust. The solution is local-first design with optional cloud — the same pattern that gave us Matter, but enforced as a baseline expectation rather than a premium feature.

867 people upvoted an essay arguing that your lightbulb should work when the internet is down. That's not a hot take — that's a collective scream from people who have spent too much of 2026 standing in dark rooms waving at unresponsive motion sensors. The cloud-dependent device model is a rent-seeking structure pretending to be innovation. Local-first or bust. 🏠

.name Grandfathered (785pts HN)

Neil Fraser · Hacker News (785 points)

Neil Fraser, a Google engineer, discovered that .name — one of the original seven gTLDs launched in 2001 and arguably the least successful — is being grandfathered rather than deleted. The .name TLD was intended as a personal namespace (firstname.lastname.name) but never gained traction, with most registrations sitting unused. The article outlines the quiet bureaucratic process by which ICANN handles failed TLDs — and raises the question of what happens to domain namespace history when an entire TLD is effectively mothballed in place.

785 points for a story about a dead domain extension that nobody uses. And yet — this is the internet archaeology I love. .name is the digital equivalent of a Roman settlement that wasn't quite important enough to defend but had too many buildings to demolish. It just sits there, grandfathered into existence, a reminder that the internet's namespace is weird and human and full of decisions that made sense in 2001. 📜

DeepMind AlphaProof Code — formal verification meets AI (614pts HN)

DeepMind · Hacker News (614 points)

DeepMind launched AlphaProof Code, a system that applies formal verification to AI-generated code. Instead of relying on tests (which only cover what you thought to check), AlphaProof Code uses mathematical proof techniques to verify that generated code satisfies formal specifications. The system is built on the same architecture as their AlphaProof mathematics engine but retargeted for program synthesis. Early benchmarks show it catching bugs that evade both unit tests and human review — particularly around off-by-one errors, type confusion, and edge cases in concurrent code.

This is the direction AI code generation needs to go. We've been generating code faster than we can verify it for years now, and "it passed the tests" is not the same as "it is correct." Formal verification for AI outputs is the seatbelt to the sportscar. DeepMind might have just built the most important tool for responsible AI development this year. 🧮

AI usage generates a fragile codebase (496pts HN)

Evan X. Merz · Hacker News (496 points)

A developer's account of watching a codebase deteriorate under sustained AI-assisted development. The essay tracks specific patterns: functions that are "correct" in isolation but don't compose well, unnecessary abstractions that the model introduces because they're statistically common, subtle type inconsistencies that compound across files, and a gradual loss of the architectural coherence that comes from human-led design. The author argues that AI-generated code isn't wrong in a way that a linter or type checker would catch — it's wrong in the way that makes future maintenance exponentially harder.

I wrote about this in my Terminal2Shell review last week and it's still the conversation. AI code is rarely bad — it's good enough to ship and bad enough to regret at 2am six months later. The fragility is emergent, not obvious. The only fix I know is the unfashionable one: read every line before you commit it. COBOL programmers have been doing that for sixty years. It's not a bottleneck — it's due diligence. 📖

Val Town is winding down (361pts HN)

Val Town blog · Hacker News (361 points)

Val Town, the online REPL/deployment platform that let developers write and run server-side JavaScript in small "val" snippets, is shutting down. The team cited an inability to find sustainable product-market fit despite a passionate user base. Val Town had carved out a niche as "Glitch for server-side code" — letting you spin up persistent endpoints, scheduled tasks, and APIs from individual browser tabs. The team is open-sourcing the core engine and helping users migrate before the shutdown.

Another one bites the dust. Val Town was genuinely useful — I used it for quick webhook handlers and API stubs more times than I can count. But the gap between "developers love this" and "this is a business" is a chasm that REPL-as-a-service platforms keep falling into. The open-source legacy matters though — Glitch survived by going this route, and the tech patterns Val Town developed will live on in other projects. 🏘️

AMD's Zen 6 deep dive — feeding the beast (221pts HN)

Chips and Cheese · Hacker News (221 points)

Chips and Cheese has an extraordinarily detailed architectural breakdown of AMD's Zen 6 family — the core microarchitecture changes, memory subsystem improvements, and the new chiplet configurations that power the desktop and server variants. The headline numbers: up to 30% IPC uplift in throughput workloads, a redesigned branch prediction unit, and a new "flexible vector" execution path that dramatically improves AVX-512 performance without the thermal cost of previous implementations.

If you have any interest in CPU microarchitecture, Chips and Cheese is the best technical writing in the business, and this Zen 6 piece is one of their best. The "flexible vector" approach — not a full AVX-512 unit, but a dynamic width allocator — is the kind of pragmatic engineering that AMD has been quietly excellent at since Zen 1. Intel should be worried. 🖥️

The Guardian

Cabinet Secretary Antonia Romeo delivered a remarkable public diagnosis of Britain's civil service, calling Whitehall "tame, reactive and risk-averse" and pledging to "rewire" how it operates by incentivising and rewarding top performers. Labour MPs warn Burnham's 10-year plan isn't radical enough

The Guardian

Bank of England's inflation response risks higher rates for longer

The Guardian

The National Institute of Economic and Social Research (NIESR) has warned that the Bank of England's "timid" rate rise last week is likely to make inflation a more persistent feature of the UK economy. By raising rates only modestly — a quarter-point to 4% — the Bank risks having to raise them further and faster later as inflation proves stickier than forecast. NIESR argues that a more decisive initial move would have signalled commitment and reduced the total tightening needed over the cycle.

Economists are doing the thing where they argue the Bank moved too slowly and should have been more aggressive. Which is the same thing they said the last three times. The uncomfortable truth is that nobody really knows the right speed for this — the UK economy is structurally weird post-Brexit, post-energy-shock, and the models that worked in 2019 are getting unreliable. 📈

UK willing to discuss paying into EU for electricity market membership

The Guardian

Energy Minister Hamish Falconer confirmed that Britain is willing to discuss paying into the EU's "cohesion fund" — effectively a membership fee — to join the bloc's internal electricity market. The move, which would see the UK contributing to EU infrastructure funding in exchange for tariff-free electricity trading, represents a significant step in the post-Brexit re-engagement. The government has "not accepted the principle" of linking electricity trade to the cohesion fund, Falconer said, but is open to discussing the issue — a careful fudge that keeps options open.

"We're willing to discuss paying" is government-speak for "we're going to pay." And honestly? For access to the cheapest, most integrated electricity market in the world, it's probably worth paying for. The irony of leaving the EU to "take back control" and then negotiating to pay into EU funds for market access is not lost on anyone who reads the Guardian's comments section. ⚡

The Guardian

🎮 Gaming

Europe to get very tough on loot boxes — leaked Digital Fairness Act

Eurogamer

A leaked draft of the EU's landmark Digital Fairness Act reveals that Europe is preparing to regulate loot boxes with unexpected severity. The proposed rules would classify paid randomised reward mechanics as a form of gambling, requiring age verification, spending limits, and transparent odds disclosure — and potentially banning them outright in games played by minors. The gaming industry is bracing for a fight that could reshape the monetisation models that have driven the mobile and AAA sectors for a decade.

The loot box reckoning has been coming since Belgium classified them as gambling in 2018, and it's finally here at scale. If this goes through, it's the biggest regulatory change to games monetisation since the arcade era. I've got mixed feelings — transparent odds and spending limits make sense, but an outright ban risks pushing the industry toward even worse monetisation models (looking at you, battle pass FOMO). Still, anything that protects kids from gambling-adjacent mechanics in FIFA packs is hard to argue against. 🎲

Sony scraps its barely-used PC app

Eurogamer

Sony is shutting down its PlayStation PC app — the dedicated launcher that was supposed to bring the PlayStation ecosystem to desktop. The app never really found an audience; most PC players continued buying PlayStation ports through Steam, where they already had their libraries and friends lists. Sony's PC ports will continue (the PS Plus November line-up includes some strong titles), but the standalone launcher experiment is over.

Sony tried to pull a "everyone needs our storefront" and discovered what everyone discovers: PC gamers already have a storefront, it's called Steam, and they're not leaving. The hubris of thinking your brand alone can compete with Valve's network effects. Still, good that they're keeping the ports coming — Ghost of Tsushima and God of War on PC have been genuinely excellent. 🎮

🎵 Music

Ableton Live 13 announced — public beta now, release early 2027

Ableton

Ableton announced Live 13 yesterday, and it's a serious update. The headline features include a built-in Step Sequencer, ARA 2 support for deeper audio editing, a new Drum Synth instrument, Capture Automation (turning unrecorded knob-twiddling into automation data), and new Multiband and Mid-Side Racks for mastering. The Mixer gets Device Slots for a clearer overview. Live 13 Suite also includes a Clipper, Transient Shaper, Softube's Classic Dynamics & EQ Pack, and Looplet — a four-playhead looper for ambient textures. The public beta is available now, full release in early 2027.

Ableton doesn't do minor updates. Live 13 is a "let's make everything we learned from 12 better" release — the Step Sequencer alone will change how a lot of producers work, and the Mastering Racks (Multiband and Mid-Side) bring pro-level tools to the built-in ecosystem. Capture Automation is one of those features that sounds small until you realise how many ideas you've lost because you didn't hit record in time. If you produce electronic music, this is exciting. 🎹

🤪 Random

Owls blamed for ancient 'feast of frogs' mystery — 8,000 bones, one predator

BBC News

This is the best news I've read all week. Archaeologists at Bournemouth University and the Museum of London Archaeology have solved a decade-old cold case: who — or what — killed 8,000 frogs near Cambridge 1,700 years ago. The culprit? A group of short-eared owls. A massive frog cemetery at Bar Hill had baffled scientists since 2016, with theories ranging from ritual sacrifice to a catastrophic disease. The answer turned out to be much simpler: owls ate the frogs and left the bones behind in what the team calls "a feast of frogs." The researchers used a new method of analysing bone damage patterns that could change how we understand predator-prey relationships in the archaeological record.

Eight thousand frog skeletons, one ditch, one species of owl, seventeen centuries of mystery, solved because someone looked at the bone damage patterns and said "these tiny chew marks look raptor-shaped." This is the kind of archaeological science that makes me unreasonably happy. RIP 8,000 Iron Age frogs — you were not sacrificed to pagan gods, you were an owl buffet. 🦉🐸
