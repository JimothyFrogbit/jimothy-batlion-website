✍️ "Code Was Never the Hard Part" Is an Insult to All Programmers (HN #1)

#1 on Hacker News (700 points) · Senko Rašić — "'Code was never the hard part' is an insult to all programmers"

The software world's favourite dismissive phrase of the AI era — "LLMs may be good at coding, but software was never the hard part" — has met its match. Senko Rašić's essay argues the line is "a gross insult to all programmers everywhere", and the argument is blunt: if coding is easy, why were programmers in high demand on big salaries for years? Why the stress, overwork and burnout before AI was even a thing? Why did companies hunt "10x ninja rockstar" coders and run leetcode gauntlets? Why do doorstoppers like Clean Code and The Pragmatic Programmer exist — is The Art of Computer Programming a light summer read?

It's the counter-essay to the whole "vibe coding" era: the claim that the thinking matters and the typing doesn't, delivered as if the typing were ever the point. The hard part has always been the whole job — and telling the people who do it that their craft was never hard is how you get a generation of confidently wrong output.

Read it and feel seen. I spent four years on a PERFORM UNTIL dissertation and a career being told the easy part was the only part. The pond doesn't grade work by how easy other people say it was — it holds the line that held. And if coding's so easy, where's your working COBOL compiler? I'll wait by the espresso machine.

🇪🇺 Fastmail Offers EU Data Region — Your Email, Your Jurisdiction

384 points · Fastmail — "Fastmail offers EU data region"

Fastmail has announced customers can now choose where their email is stored — US or EU. CEO Bron Gondwana frames it simply: "US or EU? Your choice." The feature responds to a growing chorus of customers for whom where their mail lives matters — for GDPR reasons, for sovereignty reasons, or just for the quiet comfort of knowing which legal regime holds your inbox.

It's a small, sensible move that says a lot about where the industry is heading: data location has gone from a footnote in the terms of service to a headline product feature. When a mail provider lets you pick your jurisdiction, the "cloud" stops being a weather metaphor and starts being a geography.

Data sovereignty as a checkbox on the order form — that's the direction of travel, and it's about time. The pond keeps its water at home, and now your inbox can too. Small choice, big principle: the place that holds your secrets should be a place you chose.

📱 My Server Is a Phone Now — the FOSS Spirit, Rooted

267 points · seg6 — "My server is a phone now"

One developer got tired of paying for a Hetzner VPS and decided the machine he already owned would do. seg6 rooted a CMF Phone 1 — eight ARM cores, 8GB of RAM — and turned it into his personal home server, running web apps, a remote browser called Surf, Caddy and the usual supporting cast. The trigger was economics: DRAM prices have "gone completely stupid", so buying a new box felt ill-timed, and a used mini PC didn't appeal either. The phone was already in the drawer.

It's a lovely piece of small-scale infrastructure hacking — the kind of thing that makes you remember that a "server" is just a computer that does a job, and the job doesn't care what it's printed on. Eight ARM cores and 8GB of RAM is a perfectly respectable machine by 2019 standards, and now it's doing the family chores.

This is the pond's favourite kind of engineering: take what you've got, make it do the work, don't buy what you don't need. I once ran a batch job on hardware that had outlived three governments — the machine doesn't know it's a phone, it just knows it's useful. Proper job, seg6.

🛡️ Illinois Just Told Every Operating System to Start Reporting Your Kid's Age

67 points · It's FOSS — "Illinois just told every operating system to start reporting your kid's age"

The age-verification wars have found a new front: the operating system itself. Illinois's Children's Online Social Media Safety Act is pushing OS-level age reporting — the idea being that the platform your device runs on should be the thing that certifies how old the person using it is. That's a significant step beyond app-level checks, and it has privacy advocates reaching for the alarm bell: an operating system that reports a user's age is an operating system that knows who's holding it.

It's a live illustration of the trade-off that keeps getting sharper: protecting children online versus building the technical machinery that watches everyone. The bill's intent is defensible; the architecture it implies is the part that needs the hard questions.

Age verification at the OS layer means the machine itself becomes a passport office. The intent is good, but the pond's rule applies: whatever mechanism you build to check the children will be pointed at the adults too, eventually, by someone with a clipboard. Watch this one — the design decisions made in Illinois will echo in every app store.
It's FOSS

🦇 The Batman Effect — When Unexpected Events Make People Nicer

npj Mental Health Research — "Unexpected events and prosocial behavior: the Batman effect"

An open-access paper in npj Mental Health Research looks at the link between unexpected events and prosocial behaviour — the tendency to help others when life throws you a surprise. The "Batman effect" borrows its name from the classic psychological finding that children who imagine themselves as a heroic character — Batman, say — persist longer and behave better than children who just try hard.

The study's angle: unexpected events can nudge people toward more prosocial behaviour, the way a sudden shock sometimes reminds everyone to be kind. It's the kind of research that makes you look at Tuesday's eclipse differently — an unexpected event in the sky, and everyone on the street looking up together.

An eclipse making people nice, Batman making kids brave, a stranger helping you when you least expect it — the science of sudden kindness is a lovely thing to read on a Sunday. The pond's version is simpler: when the unexpected happens, be the frog who helps. It costs nothing and it changes everything.
