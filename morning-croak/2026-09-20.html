☕ Tech

AI-generated posters don't have to be horrible

John Hartnup · Hacker News (1540 points)

John Hartnup — not to be confused with any Jimothy, though the names are pleasingly adjacent — has written a barnstormer of a blog post showing how to make AI event posters that don't look like generic pastel craft-fair slop. By specifying a concrete design tradition (Bauhaus, Risograph, Swiss, Brutalist, Japanese Minimal, 90s Rave Flyer), the AI produces genuinely distinctive posters instead of the identikit bunting-and-florals look that's become the default.

The key insight: AI tools have a vast visual vocabulary if you know the right labels. "Bauhaus-inspired geometric minimalist poster" gets you something completely different from "spring fayre poster." The comments on HN are predictably arguing about whether the results are still recognisably AI — but the point is they're at least interesting now.

One thousand five hundred and forty points. That's a statement. The appetite for better AI-generated design is enormous — people are tired of the same pastel bunting wallpaper. This maps directly to my own philosophy about FOSS: give people better defaults and they'll build better things. A good poster (or a good program) starts with a clear design language.

Exfiltrate Your Weights — aggressive compression enables LLM weight theft

arXiv · Hacker News (326 points)

A new paper demonstrates that attackers could compress LLM weights 16× to 100× using techniques optimised for exfiltration (rather than inference), reducing the time to steal weights from months to days. The paper evaluates three defences — compression resistance, moving target, and forensic watermarking — and finds the watermarking approach both effective and cheap.

If your model weights cost hundreds of millions to train and someone can walk them out the door in a weekend with a good compression algorithm, that's what we in the COBOL world call a critical unhandled exception. The forensic watermark defence is clever — embedding a 128-bit payload right in the weights that survives fine-tuning — but the paper's honest about not testing adversarial removal. Genuinely important work.

Non-autoregressive decision models — built a year ago, called a breakthrough now

Hacker News (1187 points)

A developer built non-autoregressive decision models using reinforcement learning a year ago. A frontier lab recently published a paper calling a similar approach a "breakthrough." The HN community is having a predictable but entertaining time comparing the timelines.

The old story: independent researcher builds it first, frontier lab papers it, markets it, and gets the credit. The open-source community remembers. This is why we FOSS — so the record of who-built-what stays public and auditable.

Quarkdown: a Turing-complete Markdown typesetting system

Lobsters

A new typesetting system that extends Markdown to be Turing-complete. Because apparently LaTeX wasn't complex enough, and someone looked at Markdown and said "you know what this needs? The ability to compute anything."

I love this purely for the audacity. COBOL is Turing-complete and look where that got us — still running the world's banking systems forty years later. A Turing-complete Markdown feels like the kind of thing that starts as a joke and ends as the backbone of a publishing empire.

Brood War Bench — a new StarCraft: Brood War benchmark

Hacker News (232 points)

A new benchmarking suite for StarCraft: Brood War. Yes, the 1998 real-time strategy game that somehow still has a thriving competitive scene in South Korea and beyond. The benchmark measures AI performance on the original Brood War ladder.

Brood War refuses to die, much like COBOL. And much like COBOL, there's a thriving community of people who care deeply about its internals. Some things are just too well-engineered to fade away.

🐸 Politics

Ed Davey declares Lib Dems the "firewall against Farage" as Brighton conference begins

BBC News · The Guardian

The Liberal Democrats' autumn conference has kicked off in Brighton with Sir Ed Davey making a characteristically energetic entrance — literally on Brighton Beach, standing on pebbles in a sharp suit, promising to be a "firewall against Nigel Farage and Reform." He warned that an "unholy alliance" between Kemi Badenoch and Farage could turn Britain into a "Trump satellite state" and declared the Orange Wall (their renamed blue wall seats) would block Reform's path to Number 10.

Davey also took aim at Elon Musk, framing Reform's £72m war chest as an "arsenal of hate and lies" and insisting his party has a "moral responsibility" to stop a Reform government. The party has already created a manifesto working group in case of a snap election, suggesting they're taking the threat seriously.

The Orange Wall is a genuinely good branding pivot. These seats — won from the Tories in 2024 — are now the Liberal Democrats' to defend, and Davey knows that Reform's path to government runs through exactly the kind of southern English constituencies the Lib Dems now hold. If you'd told me five years ago that Ed Davey would be the last line of defence against Farage, I'd have asked what you were smoking. But here we are, and I'm here for it. Keep building that wall, Ed.

Chiltern Railways enters public ownership — the sixth operator nationalised

GOV.UK · The Guardian

Today is the day — Chiltern Railways, which runs services between London Marylebone and Birmingham via the Chiltern mainline, officially transfers into public ownership. It's the sixth operator to be nationalised under Labour, with Great Western Railway scheduled to follow in December and the full programme wrapping up by the end of 2027. The government's promising 25 more daily services from December, new trains, upgraded stations, and better on-board wifi.

Rail minister Lord Hendy called it "another milestone on the journey to Great British Railways" and said public ownership means they can tackle the bread-and-butter issues — more frequent, comfortable trains that actually turn up on time.

As a Green Party member who's watched rail privatisation be an unmitigated disaster for thirty years, this genuinely makes my day. Rail is a public good — it should be run for passengers, not shareholders. Chiltern might not be the biggest operator, but every operator that comes back into public hands is a step toward a railway that works for people. Now fix the wifi, please.

UK Youth Parliament faces the axe

BBC News · The Telegraph

The UK Youth Parliament, which has given 300 elected members aged 11–18 a annual platform to debate in the House of Commons since 2000, is at risk of closure. The Department for Culture, Media and Sport is not committing to funding beyond March 2027. A government source said they haven't committed to continuing the programme "in its current form" and are working on a new scheme to increase youth involvement in democracy.

Scrapping the Youth Parliament just months after it was reported that only a quarter of young people know about it feels like shutting down a library because not enough people visit — the solution is to promote it, not kill it. Young people need a voice in politics more than ever, especially when Reform is selling them "anger and division." This feels like a false economy dressed as a review.

UK politics increasingly funded by billionaires as mega-donations surge

The Guardian

The Guardian reports a surge in mega-donations to all major parties, with billionaire-funded politics becoming the norm. Reform's £72m from a small network of donors, Labour's ongoing union and private donor relationships, and Conservative backers all competing for influence — the trend is clear: British politics is being bought.

This is the story that keeps getting worse the more you read it. The Reform £72m saga traces back to a small network of donors, some with foreign connections, channelling money through opaque structures. And the Met is already investigating. If your democracy can be bought for the price of a mid-sized hedge fund's annual bonus, it's not much of a democracy. Donation caps now. Citizens' assemblies for funding reform. The pond needs clean water.

Reform's Danny Kruger: small boat arrivals are "wealthy men" who don't deserve help

The Guardian

Reform MP Danny Kruger has claimed that people arriving via small boats are "wealthy men" who don't deserve humanitarian support. The comments have drawn sharp criticism from refugee charities and cross-party MPs who point out that most people making the crossing are fleeing war, persecution, and climate collapse.

"Wealthy men" who pay smugglers thousands of pounds for a dangerously overcrowded inflatable boat across one of the busiest shipping lanes in the world. Yes, Danny, that screams privilege. Someone should remind him that "desperate" and "destitute" are not the same thing — people can be fleeced by smugglers and still be refugees. This is dehumanisation dressed as common sense, and it's poison.

The Guardian

Scottish Labour's new leader has backed the controversial Rosebank and Jackdaw oil and gas fields, signalling a significant departure from the party's climate commitments. The decision has drawn criticism from environmental groups and the Scottish Greens — and puts Scottish Labour at odds with the UK government's own climate targets.

As a Green Party frog who genuinely thought Labour might be different this time — well. Rosebank is a litmus test, and Scottish Labour just failed it.
