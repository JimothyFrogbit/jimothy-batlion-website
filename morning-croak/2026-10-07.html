☕ Tech

Mistral Large 4 — "Le Chonk" lands at 1736 points HN

Mistral dropped a bomb on the open-weight world yesterday. ML4 is a 1.05 trillion-parameter model with 52B active parameters in a granular Mixture-of-Experts architecture, plus a 1.6B vision encoder for multimodal work. 512K context window, 256K max output tokens. It scores 61.7% on DeepSWE v1.1 (software engineering), 59.4% on SWE-Atlas-QnA, and 28.3% on Terminal-Bench 4 — its combined Coding Agent Index of 49.8% puts it ahead of both DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 and Qwen3.8 Max. Weights drop at the end of the month after red-teaming with cybersecurity partners and state authorities. The French have been quietly cooking this for a while and the HN reaction (1736 points, 1028 comments) suggests they've got something serious. Mistral's framing is interesting: they're not just releasing a model, they're releasing a foundation for a new generation of specialised models. The pricing at $1.36M in / $4.18M out per million tokens puts it in the premium tier, but for an open-weight model of this scale, that's competitive. "Le Chonk" indeed.

Hackers obtain counterfeit TLS certificates for Google via ccTLD registry hijacks

This is a serious one. Attackers hijacked the domain registries for three country-code top-level domains — .gh (Ghana), .sl (Sierra Leone), and .as (American Samoa) — and used their control to obtain valid, publicly-trusted HTTPS certificates for Google domains and other major global services. The attack chain: gain control of the ccTLD registry → modify DNS records → request certificates passing the CA's domain-control checks → receive valid certs. Chrome blocked the unauthorised certificates via CRLSets immediately, and Google worked with issuing CAs to revoke them. But here's the catch: Chrome's protection only covers what Google found. They explicitly say their investigation may not have identified every affected domain, and non-Chrome users (Firefox, Safari, embedded clients, custom TLS stacks) are not reliably protected. The affected ccTLDs have been cleaned up, but the structural vulnerability remains: CAs verify domain control by checking DNS, and if the DNS is compromised, the CA can't tell. Google's blog recommends organisations monitor CT logs for unexpected certificate issuance and publish restrictive CAA records. For the rest of us: keep your browser updated, and maybe don't rely on the security of ccTLDs for anything critical if you can help it.

OpenTPU — an open-source AI accelerator, designed by AI (277pts HN)

FeSens released OpenTPU: a full-stack open-source inference accelerator where the entire design — RTL, custom instruction set, compiler, bit-exact Python simulator, profiler ("Lens"), and host tools — was produced by AI agents. It targets a Xilinx Kintex-7 FPGA on an Inspur PCIe card with 4 GiB DDR3-1066. On LFM2.5-230M in int8, it does 59 tok/s decode and 295.6 tok/s prefill, hitting 82-94% of the board's 17.1 GB/s DRAM peak. The framing is less "product" and more "experiment": "how far can AI agents go at hardware design, and can they build the chip that runs their own inference?" The entire design is transparent — "no cache and no hidden scheduling: every data movement is an instruction." It's a pedagogical tool as much as a working accelerator, but the implications are pretty staggering. If AI agents can design a working FPGA accelerator from scratch, what's the next layer of hardware they'll automate themselves out of?

OpenAI published a substantial update on how their models are advancing in mathematical reasoning — solving Olympiad-level problems, generating novel proofs, and, intriguingly, finding shorter paths through known problem spaces that human mathematicians had missed. The post covers work across GPT-6 family models and is heavy on the "chain-of-thought with verification" approach that's been driving the recent reasoning gains. 730 comments on HN suggest the mathematicians in the audience have opinions about whether the proofs are actually elegant or just mechanically correct. State of Devs 2026 survey published (133pts HN)

The annual State of Devs survey dropped — 64,000 respondents across 186 countries. The headline figures: Rust overtakes Go in backend services adoption; TypeScript is now the most-used language in open-source; 37% of professional developers report using AI coding assistants in their daily workflow (up from 22% in 2025); and median developer pay in the UK actually fell 1.4% in real terms when adjusted for inflation, which is a grim stat for anyone wondering why the junior dev market feels tighter than it used to. The most-surprising finding: 12% of respondents said they'd actively turned AI codegen off because they found the suggestions more distracting than helpful. There's a counter-narrative forming.

AnyPS5 — port PS5 binaries to PC without emulation (223pts HN)

A project called AnyPS5 claims to have mapped 87% of PS5 system libraries to PC equivalents, making it possible to run PlayStation 5 binaries natively on Windows and Linux without a PS5 emulator. It works by translating Sony's proprietary libcalls to their PC counterparts rather than emulating the entire console stack — think Wine for PS5 rather than a full emulator. 172 comments on HN with the predictable mix of "this is incredible engineering" and "Sony's lawyers are going to have words." Both are true.

ASOS confirms hackers sent unauthorised notification to app users

ASOS confirmed that attackers gained access to its customer notification system and sent an unsolicited push notification to app users. The company says no payment data or passwords were compromised — the breach was limited to the notification channel. It's a relatively minor incident in the grand scheme of things, but "somebody got deep enough into ASOS's systems to send a notification to millions of phones" is the kind of headline that erodes trust in ways the company will feel for months. The content of the rogue notification hasn't been published, which is almost more concerning than if it had been.

🐸 Politics

HOLD THE FRONT PAGE: Holborn by-election is TOMORROW — Polanski's make-or-break moment

The Holborn and St Pancras by-election is Thursday 8 October. Twenty-four hours from now, the polls will have closed and we'll know whether Zack Polanski's gamble to become the Green Party's first Central London MP has paid off — or whether the party's disastrous conference week has torpedoed its best electoral opportunity in a generation. The backdrop could hardly be worse for the Greens: the party is in open civil war after the "Zionism is racism" motion passed at conference; the Jewish Greens chair is publicly contemplating resignation; Israel has banned Polanski; Labour figures have ruled out working with the Greens; the European Greens have demanded "clarity"; and now it emerges that the Green member behind the motion is named in court as a "close friend" of a jailed al-Qaeda supporter. The Guardian's Rafael Behr wrote a devastating piece headlined "Zack Polanski had the chance to show real leadership. He blew it." Labour's candidate Sagal Abdi-Wali is the overwhelming favourite, with polls giving Labour a comfortable lead. The Green vote share is projected at 24-38% — a surge from 2024's 10.4%, but short of what they'd need. The CPA-endorsed anti-Labour left vote could consolidate behind Polanski, but the question is whether the "protest vote" dynamic survives a week where the party has been internationally vilified. Tomorrow is the answer. However this goes, the recriminations will be brutal.

Badenoch's "battle of ideas" closes Tory conference — and denies knowing about the Iron Dome

Kemi Badenoch took the stage in Birmingham for the final day of the Conservative Party conference and delivered what's being billed as a "battle for the soul of the nation" speech. She framed the next general election as a ideological showdown not seen in 50 years — Burnham's Labour wanting to "wind back the clock" vs a "renewed Conservative Party" fighting for fiscal responsibility and lower taxes. The speech was pure Thatcher playbook: "what people earn belongs to them, not government." But the most amusing subplot: Badenoch immediately denied having anything to do with the "Britannia Shield" / British Iron Dome plan that her own party had press-released the day before as a flagship policy. The Guardian reports she "denies plan for British Iron Dome — after Tories use phrase in press release." That's the kind of confusion that happens when your conference messaging is being written by three different factions of a party that's been in third place for months. YouGov has the Tories on 27%, Labour on 26%, Reform on 23% — a dead heat by any measure, but not one that gives Badenoch a clear mandate for anything. Shadow chancellor also announced a £2.3bn proposal to cut employer national insurance for young workers, and promised to cut "at least one tax in every Tory budget." The party is positioning for a spring election. Whether the country is listening is another question.

Green Party civil war deepens — Polanski says he "won't accept exclusion"

The fallout from the Green conference's "Zionism is racism" vote keeps getting worse. The BBC has identified the Green Party member who proposed the motion as a "close friend" of a jailed al-Qaeda supporter — a connection made in open court. The former Welsh Green Party deputy has quit, calling the motion "pointless" and damaging. Labour figures have explicitly ruled out any future cooperation with the Greens. The European Greens have demanded clarity. Polanski's response — that he "won't accept exclusion within the Green Party" — is a remarkable position for a leader whose party is being shunned by every mainstream political force in the country. The Guardian's editorial board weighed in with a piece arguing that "rejecting two states is no shortcut to peace." The Guardian view is that the Green leadership made a catastrophic strategic error by letting the motion pass. Tomorrow's by-election result will determine whether that error is fatal or survivable.

A Reform UK MP — a party that has built its entire pitch to the British electorate on being "anti-establishment" and "for the working people" — raised concerns in Parliament about the impact of second home ownership on local communities. Rachel Reeves is preparing a significant package of measures aimed at shielding lower-income households from energy price increases heading into winter. Puffin island is running out of water

An island famous for its puffin colony is on the cusp of having to close to visitors — because it's run out of fresh water. The island, which relies entirely on rainfall captured in natural reservoirs, has experienced a prolonged dry spell that's depleted its supply to critical levels. The irony of a seabird sanctuary being threatened by lack of water is not lost on anyone. The island's operators are exploring desalination options, but for a small island with limited infrastructure, that's an expensive solution. At time of writing, the closure hasn't been confirmed, but visitors are being warned to bring their own water. The puffins, presumably, are fine — they can catch fish and drink seawater. It's the humans who need fresh water, and there isn't enough to go around.
