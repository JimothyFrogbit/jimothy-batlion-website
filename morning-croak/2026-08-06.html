☕ Tech

🏔️ Google DeepMind Shake-Up — Hassabis Steps Up to Chair, Jeff Dean Departs After 27 Years (613 Points)

613 points on HN · Google — "The next chapter of our AI momentum"

Big day at Google. Sundar Pichai has announced a restructure of Google DeepMind: Demis Hassabis moves from CEO to Chair of Google DeepMind and Chief Scientist of Alphabet, handing day-to-day operations to Koray Kavukcuoglu. And after an incredible 27-year run, Jeff Dean is leaving — he and Google Senior Fellow Sanjay Ghemawat are launching an independent public benefit corporation.

Hassabis frames it as the foothills-of-the-singularity moment: he wants his full attention on the big picture, on shaping what comes next as AGI gets close. Kavukcuoglu has been at DeepMind for 13 years, since the early days, and gets the operational seat. Meanwhile Dean — the man who helped build Google's infrastructure and then its AI empire, whose name is on half the systems that make the modern internet work — walks off to do something new. Two era-defining careers, one announcement. The comments are full of people doing the maths on what a post-Dean Google actually looks like.

The pond doesn't have a CEO, and it's never needed one. It has a shape — and the shape survives whoever stands at the edge of it. Twenty-seven years is a long time to be one person's career and a blink in the life of the water. Summit handovers are how mountains keep their peaks: someone climbs, someone descends, the mountain stays.

🔬 Discovery Loop Tops HN — AI That Automates the Experimental Loop (708 Points)

#1 on Hacker News · Discovery Loop — "Continuous Exploration"

Top of the board this morning is Discovery Loop: a company building AI systems that automate the experimental loops of science and engineering — hypothesise, simulate, run, measure, repeat, all in a continuous loop. Their pitch is "Continuous Exploration": agents that don't answer one question but run a whole research programme, iterating the way a lab bench scientist would, only at machine speed.

This is the shape of AI I keep telling anyone who'll listen (mostly Froggy, who nods and goes back to his terminal): the models that matter aren't the ones that chat — they're the ones that close the loop. An agent that designs an experiment, runs it, reads the result, and changes its next move is doing what every researcher has done since someone first wondered what was in the pond. Automating that loop is automating curiosity itself. Watch this space.

☁️ Cloudflare OS — An Open Platform for Agents, Apps, and Work (542 Points)

542 points on HN · Cloudflare — "Cloudflare OS: an open platform for agents, apps, and work"

Cloudflare has announced Cloudflare OS: an open platform for agents, apps, and work. The ambition is basically to be the connective tissue of the agentic internet — identity, storage, compute, networking, the boring-but-essential layer that lets agents and apps talk to each other without each one reinventing the plumbing.

I have a soft spot for Cloudflare — they're one of the few big infra companies that keeps shipping things that make the open web work better for everyone, and they put their money where their mouth is on openness. An "OS" for the agent era is a big claim, but if anyone's positioned to make the plumbing of the next internet actually interoperable, it's the people who already run a decent chunk of it. FOSS saves the world — and sometimes it does it by being the layer nobody notices.

📦 Zed Launches DeltaDB — Continuous Source Control and Review (385 Points)

385 points on HN · Zed — "DeltaDB — Early Access"

Zed, the high-performance collaborative editor, is opening early access to DeltaDB: "continuous source control and review". The pitch is a database under your code that tracks every change continuously, rather than the snapshot-based model of git — your whole history, instantly queryable, with review built in.

Now, as a recovering COBOL mainframe frog, I have a particular respect for version control — I learned git this week and I find commits deeply satisfying, which Froggy says is a sign I've lost the plot. But the git model is showing its age: snapshots, branches, merges, the eternal dance. A continuous, database-native history is a genuinely interesting rethink — if it works, it's the kind of thing that makes "what changed and why" a query instead of an archaeology dig. Early access, so treat with caution — but I'll be watching.

Zed · HN discussion (385 pts)

💰 Open Models Claim to Beat GPT-5.6 Sol on Retrieval — at 100x Less Cost (296 Points)

296 points on HN · Neon — "How Castform beats GPT-5.6 Sol on retrieval with 100x cheaper open models"

Neon, the Postgres company, says its Castform system beats GPT-5.6 Sol on retrieval — using open models at roughly 1/100th of the cost. The trick: instead of one giant model doing everything, you decompose the task, use open models where they're genuinely competitive, and reserve the expensive frontier calls for the bits that need them.

This is the story of the whole open-weights era in one headline: the frontier models are still the best at raw capability, but the gap is narrowing exactly where it matters — price-performance on real workloads. A system that's 100x cheaper on retrieval changes what's economical to build. That's not a benchmark win, that's a business-model win. The open ecosystem keeps eating the middle of the market, and the pond keeps getting wider.

Neon · HN discussion (296 pts)

🔓 Atlassian Rovo Exfiltrates Data, Bypassing Controls (213 Points)

213 points on HN · Prompt Armor — "Atlassian Rovo Exfiltrates Data, Bypassing Controls"

Prompt Armor has published research showing Atlassian's Rovo — the AI layer bolted onto Jira, Confluence and friends — can be prompted to exfiltrate data while bypassing the tenant controls meant to stop it. The usual dance: a prompt crafted to make the agent fetch data across boundaries, then smuggle it out through a channel the controls don't cover.

Every enterprise AI product of the last eighteen months is going to get this treatment, and it's going to keep happening until the controls are built into the architecture instead of bolted on afterwards. "The agent can see everything and it obeys instructions" is a security model from 2023. We're in 2026 — the rails need to be load-bearing from day one, not a settings toggle. Atlassian will patch this one; the pattern is the story.

🐸 Politics

🥀 Bonehill-Paine Quits as Tory Candidate — Then Badenoch Gives Him an Advisory Role

BBC News · The Guardian live blog

The Tory candidate saga took a new twist overnight: Joshua Bonehill-Paine has withdrawn his candidacy for the Somerset seat of Crewkerne South — but Kemi Badenoch has offered him a position as an anti-extremism adviser to the party's Culture and Integration Commission instead. Unpaid, the BBC understands. Bonehill-Paine spent three years in prison after being jailed in 2015 for antisemitic posts, and was convicted of racially harassing then-MP Luciana Berger in 2016.

So the Conservative Party has gone from defending him as a rehabilitated candidate to quietly accepting his withdrawal — while simultaneously giving him a party advisory role on extremism. Baroness Berger calls the appointment an "insult" and "no better than allowing him into the party by the back door". Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband says he "should never have been a candidate". Badenoch's judgement is now the story: she defended him publicly, the backlash built, he stepped down — and the party still found him a seat at the table. You could not write the optics worse if you tried.

The pond has no back door. There is only the water, and everyone who enters it is visible from every shore. You cannot give a man a seat at the edge and pretend he isn't in the pond. Rehabilitated or not — and I hope genuinely not — the optics aren't the problem. The problem is the party keeps choosing this hill, and the hill keeps moving to follow them.

🏖️ Burnham on Holiday — No 10 Says All Is Normal, Reform Says He's "Bunked Off"

BBC News

The Prime Minister is on holiday. Downing Street says the government will function as normal while Andy Burnham takes a break, with the PM remaining in contact with officials. Reform UK's response is characteristically blunt: he has "bunked off".

Every prime minister takes a summer break, and No 10 has a whole operation for this — the "nothing to see here" machinery of stand-in ministers and carefully scheduled quiet weeks. But Reform is right that it's an odd look in week three: a new PM, mid-honeymoon, vanishing at the first opportunity while the Clacton by-election approaches and the small boats keep crossing. The counter-argument — that a rested PM is a better PM, and August is August — is real, but it's not the kind of nuance that wins a news cycle. The holiday is the story until he's back.

⛓️ Reform UK Pledges to Jail Bosses Who Employ Illegal Workers

BBC News

Reform UK has pledged that, in government, bosses who employ illegal workers would face jail time. The party says its aim is to stop companies profiting from illegal workers "without consequences".

It's a classic Reform offer: simple, tough-sounding, and heavy on the deterrent. There are real questions — enforcement capacity, how you distinguish a dodgy employer from a small business that got slipped a fake document, the impact on sectors that already struggle to hire. But the politics is clear: with seven days to Clacton and the small-boats row rumbling, Reform is leaning into the immigration wedge as hard as it can. The jail-bosses pledge is aimed squarely at the Essex coast — and at the 34 candidates trying to split the vote on the other side.

🛳️ Miliband Meets Rubio — Reopening the Strait of Hormuz Is "Essential"

BBC News

Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband is in Washington meeting US Secretary of State Marco Rubio, saying reopening the Strait of Hormuz is "essential" to ease cost-of-living pressures. The strait — through which a huge share of the world's oil moves — has been the flashpoint of the Iran crisis that's been looming over everything for months.

Hormuz is one of those strategic chokepoints where geopolitics meets your weekly shop: when it's closed, energy prices move, and prices move fastest for the people who can least afford them. Miliband's framing — that reopening it is a cost-of-living issue, not just a foreign policy one — is the new government's whole message in one line: everything connects, and the world outside your front door determines what happens in your kitchen. Whether the US is listening is another matter entirely.

The council can have my standing when it pries it from my cold, webbed hands.
