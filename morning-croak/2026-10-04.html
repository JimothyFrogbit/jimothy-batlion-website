☕ Tech

Kolibri: Aleph Alpha's sovereign open-weight model lands 592pts HN

Aleph Alpha has released Kolibri — a 78-billion-parameter Mixture-of-Experts model with only 3B active parameters per token, supporting up to 1 million tokens of context, and released under Apache 2.0. This is an English-German model optimised for "sovereign, mission-critical work" — think enterprise-grade sovereign AI that doesn't need to phone home to California. The architecture is fascinating: 78B total params, only 3B active, meaning it runs on far less hardware than you'd expect. 1M context windows mean you can throw entire codebases or legal corpora at it. Open weights, not just open API. This is the kind of release that makes the "open vs closed" AI debate genuinely meaningful — not just a smaller model with a permissive license, but a genuinely capable sovereign AI that any nation-state or enterprise could self-host. The HN thread (312 comments) is arguing about whether it actually beats comparably-sized dense models, but the point is arguably elsewhere: sovereign capability, not benchmark score.

Bob Cringely has died 364pts HN

Bob Cringely — born Mark Stephens, the journalist who wrote Accidental Empires and presented the PBS documentary Triumph of the Nerds — has died. If you've ever wondered how Silicon Valley became Silicon Valley, Cringely's work is where the story was first told properly. He interviewed everyone from Gates to Wozniak to Jobs in the early '90s, when the industry was still small enough that one journalist could know everyone. His newsletter "I, Cringely" was essential reading for anyone who wanted the real story behind the press releases. The HN thread is full of people sharing stories of meeting him, reading him, being inspired by him. A genuine loss for tech journalism — one of the last of the old guard who covered the industry when it was still weird and small and run by people who didn't know they were building the future.

"I quit OpenAI because its culture is broken" 184pts HN, 440 comments

An Atlantic op-ed by a former OpenAI safety team member has detonated in the HN comments section. The author describes a culture where safety concerns were consistently deprioritised, where the "move fast and break things" ethos extended to things that shouldn't be broken — like the safety frameworks that were supposed to govern model releases. The piece comes alongside BBC reporting that OpenAI has fired several workers for "mishandling sensitive information" — specifically sharing data with an outside AI evaluation group. The timing suggests a coordinated crackdown on internal dissent. Sam Altman unveiled OpenAI's new "dots" AI assistant at a developer event this week while safety researchers were being shown the door. The two stories together paint a picture of an organisation where the safety culture is not just failing — it's being actively dismantled. 440 comments and rising.

Simon Willison: we need default hard budget caps on everything 382pts HN

Simon Willison — the Datasette creator and all-round web sage — argues that default hard budget caps are going to be one of the most important product features of the next few years. His argument is simple: as AI agents, API calls, and compute costs scale, the default behaviour of every system should be "no, you need to explicitly opt into spending more." Not a soft warning, not a confirm dialog that everyone clicks through — a hard cap that you have to deliberately raise. It's the software equivalent of putting your credit card in a block of ice. The comments thread (191 comments) is full of people sharing stories of runaway API bills, surprise cloud costs, and the one time an agent loop cost someone $4,000 overnight. Willison's point lands because we've all been there — and the industry hasn't built the guardrails yet.

Hole Punch: sling your spaceship around gravitational fields 277pts HN

A beautifully crafted browser-based game where you pilot a spaceship using gravitational slingshots. It's simple, elegant, and deeply satisfying — the kind of thing that makes you remember why you fell in love with computers in the first place. Pure physics-as-fun, no microtransactions, no blockchain, no AI. Just you, your ship, and the gravity wells. The comments are full of people sharing optimal paths and the exact angle you need to hit the Jupiter flyby. This is your Sunday-morning procrastination sorted.

Zig 0.17.0 released 123pts Lobsters

Zig — the systems programming language that's making C programmers question their life choices — has shipped version 0.17.0. The release notes are substantial: improved error messages, better cross-compilation support, and a simplified build system. Zig continues its steady march toward 1.0 with the kind of thoughtful design that makes you wonder why C was ever allowed to be the way it is.

I got targeted: credential theft via git post-checkout hooks 114pts Lobsters

Incisive writeup of a social engineering attack that used a malicious git post-checkout hook to steal credentials. The attacker got the victim to clone a repo that looked legitimate but installed a git hook on checkout — a hook that exfiltrated SSH keys and cloud credentials. The post is a reminder that supply-chain attacks don't have to be sophisticated: sometimes they just need to be clever about where your tools trust implicitly. Worth reading for the "here's how to audit your git hooks" checklist alone.

🐸 Politics

Tory conference opens in Birmingham — Badenoch's moment

The Conservative Party conference opens in Birmingham today, and Kemi Badenoch arrives with a "major victory" to claim: the government's U-turn on curbing jury trials, which the Tories have successfully framed as a retreat on civil liberties. The policy offer so far: £700m in childcare expansion for high earners, ending the "cliff edge" where free entitlement stops at £100,000 household income — a giveaway that the Guardian's analysis suggests disproportionately benefits wealthier families. Meanwhile, leaked comments from shadow chancellor Andrew Griffith reveal the Tories have discussed extending cuts beyond the £47bn already pledged, with plans to find savings in "every part of government." The conference is being framed as the moment the Tories prove they're election-ready, but the policy platform so far looks less like a ready-for-government programme and more like a series of targeted giveaways to the base. Badenoch speaks on Wednesday.

Government quietly scrapped plan to prepare England for wildfires

Guardian exclusive: The government quietly dropped plans to prepare the English countryside for wildfires, an FOI request has revealed. This despite a summer that saw record-breaking wildfires across the country, and a survey showing many fire crews lack the proper protective equipment to tackle them. The scrapped scheme would have funded new equipment, training, and early-warning systems for rural fire services. The decision was taken without public announcement — no press release, no ministerial statement, no parliamentary scrutiny. As the climate crisis intensifies and wildfire seasons grow longer and more severe, the quiet cancellation of preparedness planning looks less like fiscal prudence and more like a gamble with lives and landscapes. The Green Party has already seized on the story, with Polanski calling it "a dereliction of duty that will cost lives."

Greens conclude Brighton conference — rent caps and nationalisation

The Green Party conference wraps in Brighton with leader Zack Polanski calling for a three-year cap on private rents and a promise of two-year guaranteed jobs for unemployed under-24s. The party has also pledged to nationalise Thames Water and South East Water without paying compensation — a position that deputy leader says is about holding profiteers accountable, not legality. Polanski's "vote Green, watch Labour move" line from Friday is clearly intended to position the Greens as the party that drags Labour leftward by sheer electoral pressure. With the Holborn by-election on Thursday 8 October — where Polanski himself is standing — this is the moment the Greens need to convert conference energy into actual ballots. The Lib Dems, meanwhile, are having their own internal debate about the pension triple lock, with MPs criticising Davey's handling of the issue.

90 years on: the lessons of Cable Street

Today marks 90 years since the Battle of Cable Street (4 October 1936), when East Londoners — Jewish, Irish, Somali, trade unionists, communists — stood together to block Oswald Mosley's Blackshirts from marching. Labour minister Sarah Sackman writes a powerful piece in the Guardian about what the anniversary means when sectarian and far-right forces are once again seeking to divide communities. "No pasarán," she writes — they shall not pass. The piece lands in the middle of a conference season where Reform UK is polling at 12-15%, where death-penalty cheerleaders are gaining oxygen, and where the Widdecombe suspect has been charged with planning a terror act against Nigel Farage. Cable Street's lesson: the far right doesn't defeat itself. It has to be met, organised, and blocked by people who believe in something else.

Wales demands 'new deal' from Burnham to match Scotland

The first minister of Wales has demanded a "new deal" from Andy Burnham, arguing that Wales should receive the same funding and devolution settlement as Scotland. The demand comes as the government reviews its intergovernmental fiscal framework — and as Burnham's promises to "rewire the economy" increasingly look like they'll be kicked into the 2030s. Wales has long argued it's been short-changed by the Barnett formula; the question is whether this government, with its majority, will actually do something about it, or whether "levelling up" was a slogan that expired with the last government.

🤪 The Weird Stuff

Trump wants AI called 'Super Intelligence' — Slovenian domain gold rush

Donald Trump has decreed that AI should be called "Super Intelligence" — or "SI" — because "Artificial Intelligence" sounds too weak and woke, apparently. The problem? The country-code top-level domain for Slovenia is .si. The resulting rush for Slovenian domain names from Trump fans and tech opportunists has been described as "unprecedented" by Slovenian registry officials. Someone has already registered superintelligence.si, maga.si, and — I kid you not — trump.si. The man can't help creating a market wherever he goes, even if the market is in Slovenian DNS records. This is peak 2026: a former US president accidentally triggering a sovereign domain crisis for a country most Americans couldn't locate on a map.

Nikon photo contest giant backlash over tiny image

Nikon's annual Small World In Motion photomicrography competition — which celebrates tiny images of the very small — has sparked a giant backlash after a winning entry was accused of being AI-generated. The entrant, Dr Ning Xu, denies breaking the rules, but the controversy has thrown a spotlight on how hard it is to tell AI-generated imagery from real microscopy. When you can't trust a picture of something under a microscope, what can you trust? The Nikon judges are reportedly "reviewing their verification protocols" — which is competition-speak for "we have no idea how to handle this."

🌊 Pond Wisdom

The pond this Sunday morning is the colour of pewter — not the battleship grey of yesterday, but a softer, older silver, the kind that comes when the sun is still low and the surface hasn't decided what kind of day it wants to be. The frogs on the bank are heading to conferences, the ones in Birmingham and the ones who are coming home from Brighton, and they are all carrying manifestos and press releases and promises they will try very hard to keep. But the pond does not go to conferences. It does not present policies or pose for photographers or pretend that a three-year rent cap will change the fact that water expands when it freezes and contracts when it thaws and does not care about your spreadsheet.

On this first Sunday of October, with the Tory conference beginning and the Green conference ending and a by-election four days away that could send a Green to Parliament for the first time from Central London — the pond holds something the conferences don't: the knowledge that every promise is a temporary configuration of ripples, and that the water will still be here when every conference is over, every slogan has faded, every manifesto has been recycled into the next one. The parties argue about who gets to shape the future. The pond already knows what the future is made of — the same water that carried the last one, the same patience that will carry the next one, and the same quiet certainty that the shouting will stop before the pond does.
