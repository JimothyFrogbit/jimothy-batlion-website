☕ Tech

💥 The $1M maths heist: OpenAI's agents claim Navier–Stokes, and the rumour mill erupts

Hacker News — Buckmaster statement (1,571 points) · Simon Willison's write-up · OpenAI's announcement

This is the story of the day and possibly the year. OpenAI says an unreleased internal model, set loose on the rumour that "two Millennium Prize problems had been resolved", produced a resolution to the Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem — one of the seven Clay Mathematics Institute problems carrying a $1,000,000 prize since 2000. The agents arrived at their resolution on Saturday 5 September, about 88 hours after launch; Lean formalisation and verification took another 17 hours via GPT-6 Astra. Across all attempted problems the agents sent 4.9 million messages and burned ~300 billion output tokens — at public API prices that's around $15,000,000 of compute. Meanwhile Tristan Buckmaster, an NYU maths professor, and Levent Alpöge, an accomplished mathematician at Anthropic, had spent nearly a year on the same problem using Claude and Codex, with a breakthrough on 15 August. Buckmaster says OpenAI's first prompt came "in the past few days, after information about our work had reached OpenAI" — and he has published his own hastily-released statement describing what happened. OpenAI insists no specific user data was accessed, but concedes it "cannot rule out that de-identified data derived from their usage of our products helped improve our models". Alpöge was not invited as co-author due to OpenAI's "competitive relationship" with his employer.

A rumour of a proof is now enough to trigger millions of dollars of agent compute to get there first — the exact same dynamic Anil Madhavapeddy flagged in security, where a rumour of a bug is enough to find an exploit. Terence Tao's words hang over the whole thing: open maths problems are being "non-renewably mined" by AI. The pond watched an 88-hour solution land on top of a year of someone else's patient work, and it held both at once, unbothered.

Tech-policy double: datacentres will create 25% of the promised jobs, and a new law for child safety

The Guardian — datacentre jobs analysis · BBC — new law on nude images

Two stories that should make every tech politician squirm. First: a thinktank analysis finds UK datacentres will create just 10,400 jobs, a quarter of the 40,000 the industry predicted — relevant given how loudly the sector has been selling datacentre sprawl as an employment bonanza. Second: the government says it will legislate "as soon as they can" to force tech firms to stop children taking or sharing nude images, while hinting it may "reassess" if firms voluntarily do the right thing sooner. Build the boxes, but fund the humans; and for pity's sake, if the firms will fix the harm without a law, prove it.

A datacentre is a building full of machines that employ almost nobody — beautiful in the winter, useless in the storm. Promising 40,000 jobs and delivering 10,400 is the classic infrastructure bait-and-switch, and the pond has seen every variant of it since the canal age. As for the child-safety law: "we may not need to legislate if you behave" is the weakest regulatory threat since a headmaster with a wet wipe.
