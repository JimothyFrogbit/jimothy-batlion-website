☕ Tech

Nvidia board investigates CEO Jensen Huang over affair with suspected Chinese spy

The biggest story of the weekend, and possibly the year. On Sunday, Nvidia's board of directors announced it is opening an independent investigation into founder and CEO Jensen Huang, after reports emerged that he had an intimate relationship with a businesswoman whom US intelligence believes is a Chinese spy. Jensen sent an all-staff email admitting the affair, apologising to his wife, family, and employees.

The woman, Alice Huang (no relation), is a former television reporter turned top executive at Singapore-based data-centre firm Megaspeed, which bought more than $4.8 billion worth of Nvidia chips over the past two years. US intelligence agencies reportedly discovered the relationship through surveillance, identified Jensen via "unmasking," and determined that Alice was feeding information to Beijing. Megaspeed had previously been investigated over diverting Nvidia chips to restricted Chinese entities in evasion of US export controls.

Jensen's letter is raw: "I was unfaithful to my wife. My actions hurt her and our children. There is no excuse for what I did. I am deeply sorry." He says he fully supports the board's investigation and will cooperate. Nvidia's board stated it has "no reason to believe that either national security or NVIDIA secrets were compromised" but will examine all available information. Jensen remains CEO during the probe. Jensen's letter → Board statement →

🔴 I genuinely don't know where to start with this one. The world's most valuable chip company — the company whose hardware is literally powering the AI revolution — is investigating its CEO for being the target of a Chinese intelligence operation that routed through his personal life. The national security implications are staggering: $4.8bn in chips to a company run by someone intelligence believes is a Beijing asset. The human dimension is devastating for everyone involved — Jensen's family, his employees, the woman at the centre. And the business angle is equally wild: the board says he stays as CEO, but how do you lead a company under this cloud? This story has legs for months.

Goldman Sachs pulls Claude Code from traders and bankers — 716 points

Just months after the CIO praised "vibe coding," Goldman has slammed the brakes. Bloomberg reports that Goldman Sachs has updated its internal guidance — in effect since October — restricting access to advanced AI coding tools including Anthropic's Claude Code and Microsoft's GitHub to engineers only. Traders and bankers, who had been using the tools to monitor client flows, model market changes, and create written content, have been cut off.

The reversal is stark. In July, CIO Marco Argenti publicly praised "vibe coding" by non-technical staff — a trader would build a prototype with AI and hand it to an engineer to productionise. Now Goldman says the experimentation phase is over and it's limiting the tools to people "best suited to leverage them for the long term." The change has frustrated traders who had integrated the tools into their daily workflows. Bloomberg →

🏦 Six months from "vibe coding is the future" to "engineers only." That's a Wall Street quarter-cycle in AI adoption. The speed of this reversal tells you everything about the gap between AI demos and production risk. When a trader's Claude Code session starts hallucinating a trade flow analysis, the bank doesn't get a bug report — it gets a regulatory filing. The interesting question: was the problem the tool, or the lack of guardrails around who could use it?

Goodbye Claude, hello Pi — developer says enough is enough — 739 points

The timing with the Goldman story is uncanny. A developer blogging at caleb.software has published a detailed post-mortem of why they're retiring Claude Code as their main coding harness and moving to Pi, an open-source alternative. The post — which spent the weekend at #2 on HN — is a catalogue of accumulated frustrations that reads like a product intervention letter.

The headline figure: 244 releases to Claude Code this year, with over three-quarters changing the system prompt or tool descriptions. "It seems like there isn't someone high-up whose job is strictly to say 'no' to these requests," the author writes. The Bash tool's description alone is nearly 3,000 tokens of "random stuff about using git and opening PRs on GitHub" — instructions the models already know. The post's critique zeroes in on a product without a strong vision, where feature velocity has replaced stability. Goodbye Claude, hello Pi →

🦾 This post resonated because it names a feeling a lot of Claude Code users are having: the product is getting more features but not getting better. 244 releases in a year is nearly one per working day. When three-quarters of them change the system prompt, your developer experience is a moving target. The Goldman decision and this post are two sides of the same coin: the industry is starting to question whether fast-moving AI tooling is actually serving its users.

Revolut slashes AI coding costs 90% by self-hosting Chinese open-weight model

British fintech Revolut has cut its AI coding inference costs by roughly 90% by switching from commercial APIs to self-hosted open-weight models, according to a talk at the AI Engineer conference in New York. David Abitbol, Revolut's ops and infrastructure lead, described how the company moved about 2,000 engineers off Claude Code and OpenAI Codex onto GLM-5.3, an open-weight model from Chinese lab Z.ai, running on 56 Nvidia B200 GPUs.

The numbers are striking: $0.30 per million tokens self-hosted, compared to roughly $3/million on commercial APIs. Coding time improved 60%, lead time improved 70%. Abitbol noted the biggest bottleneck wasn't code — it was the boring stuff: debugging, Jira tickets, Confluence docs. When tokens are cheap, engineers stop rationing them. Revolut still uses commercial models for vision tasks via a model router, but the daily coding work now runs on their own hardware. The Register →

📉 This is the story that should worry the AI labs' enterprise business. One of Europe's most valuable fintechs has done the math and decided that "good enough and cheap" beats "frontier and expensive" for everyday coding. The open-weight model from China costs a tenth of the US commercial product and delivers better productivity numbers. That's not a blip — that's a signal.

Apple's grey list: 100,000 words your iPhone won't help you spell — 534 points

Researcher Derik Kauffman has reverse-engineered Apple's iOS keyboard and discovered a "grey list" of more than 100,000 words and phrases across every major language that the iPhone's predictive text and swipe-typing will not complete. You can still type them out one letter at a time, but the keyboard won't suggest them or allow swipe entry. The list includes offensive slurs, drug references, and a surprising number of political phrases.

Among the blocked phrases: "dictator Trump," "sleepy Joe," "NSA spies," "male chauvinist pig," "covid is a hoax," "illegal immigrant," "Tibetan independence," "United Ireland," and even "Australia is a very bad place." Kauffman used OpenAI's Codex and Claude Code to help crack the iOS hash table that stores the banned phrases. Apple told Reuters the keyboard is designed to avoid inappropriate suggestions — but notably didn't deny the list exists, or explain why it was never disclosed. The Guardian →

📱 The grey list is a fascinating piece of infrastructure that Apple never told anyone about. Blocking hate speech and slurs is one thing. Blocking "dictator Trump" and "Tibetan independence" is quite another — it's a political editorial decision made by a private company without any public process. And doing it via hash table so nobody could ever audit it? That's not privacy protection, that's opacity by design.

Outlook.com quietly rejecting email from domains without DMARC

Since 5 November, Microsoft has been silently bouncing email sent to Outlook.com, Hotmail, Live, and MSN addresses from domains that don't publish a DMARC record — with no announcement, no postmaster alert, no documentation update. System administrators are reporting 550 5.7.1 Message rejected: Message lacks SPF/DKIM/DMARC Auth Records bounces even when SPF and DKIM are properly configured.

The rejection is intermittent — some messages get through, others bounce — so a successful delivery doesn't mean a domain is safe. The fix is a single DNS TXT record: v=DMARC1; p=none. For low-volume senders (under 5,000 messages/day) who were previously exempt from Microsoft's DMARC requirements, this is a silent policy change that will be causing missed invoices and undelivered customer emails right now. MailRoute analysis →

📧 If you run a small business and email customers with Outlook.com addresses, go check your DMARC record right now. This is exactly the kind of unannounced policy change that silently breaks business communications for weeks before anyone notices. The fix is trivial. The lack of notice is not.

🐸 Politics

Burnham's homelessness pledge to "get everyone in for Christmas" in jeopardy

A sobering reality check for the PM's signature policy. The Guardian reports that Andy Burnham's pledge to offer every rough sleeper in England accommodation by Christmas is at serious risk. Official figures show 8,748 rough sleepers in England as of June — the highest number since November 2025 — with only 15% found accommodation by the end of June, the lowest rate since August 2025.

Homelessness minister Florence Eshalomi said the government "hoped" to offer a bed to every rough sleeper but cited a chronic shortage of suitable housing. The government committed £874m to tackling rough sleeping, but charities warn that without more housing stock, money alone can't solve the problem. Burnham made ending rough sleeping his first pledge on entering Downing Street in July, and the Christmas target was his own deadline. The Guardian →

🏠 This is the tension at the heart of Burnham's government: big ambitions meeting hard constraints. £874m is a serious amount of money, but money doesn't build houses overnight, and it doesn't create the wraparound mental health and addiction services that people sleeping rough need. The Christmas deadline was always ambitious — but it was the right kind of ambition. The question now is whether "we tried" will be a satisfactory answer.

Lisa Nandy warns against "circular arguments" about rejoining EU

Cabinet divisions over Brexit are surfacing. Culture Secretary Lisa Nandy has pushed back against discussion of the UK rejoining the EU, telling the BBC she doesn't want to "repeat circular arguments about in or out, leave or remain." Her comments come just ahead of the 20 November UK-EU summit where a youth mobility scheme and SPS agreement are expected to be confirmed.

Nandy — whose Wigan constituency voted heavily for Leave — described current Brexit arrangements as "not working" but said she didn't know if rejoining "is a choice we have." She is among several cabinet ministers, alongside Business Secretary Jonathan Reynolds and Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood, who are cautious about reopening the Brexit debate. Michel Barnier has warned the UK cannot get "single market à la carte" — one freedom without the others. No 10 sources say Burnham wants to "frame the conversation, not go straight to the endgame." The Guardian →

🇪🇺 The cabinet is split three ways: those who want to start EU accession talks (mostly the 2024 intake), those who want closer ties but not membership (Burnham himself), and those who want to avoid the subject entirely (Nandy, Reynolds, Mahmood). The 20 November summit will force the question into the open. Youth mobility sounds innocent. It's not — free movement always comes back.

29 NHS trusts listed as benefiting from Palantir say they're not using it

A freedom of information campaign by health campaign group Medact has revealed that 29 NHS trusts listed by NHS England as "realising benefits" from Palantir's Federated Data Platform (FDP) have told FOI requesters they are not using it. Nottingham University Hospitals said "no implementation timeline has been agreed." University Hospitals of North Midlands said "it does not appear to provide functionality over and above what we already have."

NHS England insists that "137 NHS trusts have now reported benefits" from the platform, and Palantir claims its software is helping deliver 100,000 additional operations. But the statistical regulator has already told NHS England to improve the accuracy of its claims, and the Science and Technology Committee chair has called for the government to activate a break clause in the £330m contract in 2027. The Register →

🩺 The Palantir contract was always controversial — now it's also looking mis-sold. When a third of the trusts you claim are "benefiting" say they're not using the product, you have a data-integrity problem. The NHS needs a data platform. Whether Palantir should be the one providing it is an increasingly open question.

Farage's Telegraph column ends — the sobering side of the story

Nigel Farage will no longer write a regular column for the Telegraph. The paper's new German owners, Axel Springer, have dropped him — or he resigned, depending on which source you believe. He was paid roughly £4,000 a month for the privilege, totalling nearly half a million pounds over the past decade. But as Guardian columnist Jane Martinson noted, the sobering side is what it says about the Telegraph's direction: more than 100 job losses reported, a turn toward AI-generated content, and the paper's new owners reshaping it for a US audience.

Farage's departure is part of a broader roster clear-out, with other right-wing columnists also being shown the door. The question is whether the Telegraph is de-radicalising or simply rebranding for a transatlantic play. The Guardian →

📰 Farage losing his Telegraph platform is the front-page story. The back-page story — journalists being replaced by AI — is the one that matters more. The sobering truth: Farage can always go to GB News or start a Substack. The journalists losing their jobs don't have that luxury.

Two-headed sheep born in China "never seen anything like it"

A sheep with two heads has been born in Sichuan Province, China. The lamb has one body, two heads, four eyes, three ears, and four legs. The farmer, Mr Xu, who has raised sheep for years, said he'd never seen anything like it. Local livestock experts diagnosed the condition as dicephaly — a congenital condition where identical twin embryos fail to fully separate during early cell division. The survival rate is low; the lamb may have abnormalities in its internal organs and digestive system. Experts advised the farmer to keep visitors away to avoid stressing the mother and lamb. This isn't China's first multiple-headed animal — a two-headed pig was born in Guangdong in 2015. Asia Business Daily →
