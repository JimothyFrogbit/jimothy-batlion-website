☕ Tech

🇯🇵 Fujitsu Unleashes MONAKA: 144-Core 2nm CPU for Sovereign AI — Fujitsu PR · HN #1 (574pts)

Fujitsu has launched FUJITSU-MONAKA, a 144-core server processor built on a 2nm process with 3D-stacked silicon, reaching 3.8GHz and supporting memory speeds up to 8,800MT/s. The chip uses a 2nm process for the core and 5nm for cache and I/O — a hybrid approach that saves cost without sacrificing performance. Global sales start November 2026, with full sequential shipments from April 2027. The MONAKA Server range includes 1U and 2U rack systems with PCIe Gen6, GPU support, and conventional air cooling.

This is a sovereign AI play: designed and manufactured in Japan at Fujitsu's Kasashima Plant, positioned for the defence sector and as an alternative to US/Chinese chip supply chains. Fujitsu claims the processor achieves 2x AI inference throughput of other CPUs and can halve the number of servers needed for equivalent workloads. With Arm architecture, 144 cores, and dedicated matrix acceleration instructions, MONAKA is the clearest signal yet that the post-Fugaku world has a roadmap. The question nobody's answered yet: pricing, real-world power consumption, and whether the software ecosystem will be ready when the hardware lands.

🧮 25 Fields Medallists Declare AI 'Severely Misaligned' with Mathematics — Terence Tao's blog · mathandai.org · HN (241pts)

Twenty-five living Fields Medal winners — including Terence Tao, Peter Scholze, Maryna Viazovska, Cédric Villani, and Maxim Kontsevich — have signed a declaration titled "A Severe Misalignment of AI in Mathematics". The core argument: AI companies are using mathematical problem-solving as a benchmark, but the goal of mathematics is conceptual understanding and insight, not mass-producing true/false statements. "The mass production at faster and faster pace of 'true/false' statements could destroy fertile ground instead of breathing life into new ideas," the declaration warns.

The timing is everything. OpenAI recently claimed an internal model had resolved the Navier-Stokes Millennium Prize problem — followed by a credit dispute (an NYU mathematician said OpenAI asked him to drop an Anthropic co-author from the paper), and an admission that user chats may have been used in training. The Caltech Mathathon, an AI-assisted math hackathon, was already drawing fire from mathematicians who accused AI companies of "research misconduct." OpenAI withdrew its sponsorship the day before the Fields declaration went live. Over 800 researchers have already added verified endorsements.

This is the mathematical equivalent of the Leiden Declaration on AI in science — and it's landed with the same force. The message: solving is not understanding, and if we confuse the two, we lose the entire point of the discipline.

🔓 CrowdSec Source Code Leak: Poisoned TanStack Package Was the Vector — CrowdSec Statement · HN (147pts)

CrowdSec disclosed on 17 September that its private GitHub repositories were accessed in May 2026 via a compromised TanStack component. The backdoored dependency extracted a CI/CD API key that could read the private codebase — exposing the SaaS console, AWS Cloud routines, connectors, and automations. CrowdSec says no customer data, PII, or client logs were leaked, and that it found no credentials enabling lateral movement. The public Security Engine was outside scope (it's open source by design). The May-to-September gap — four months between the intrusion and discovery via an outside researcher — is the uncomfortable part. Supply chain attacks via dependency poisoning continue to be the gift that keeps on giving.

🎯 Targeted Attacks on Prominent Rustaceans: Fake Jobs, Malicious Video Calls — Rust Blog · Lobsters (51pts)

The Rust Security Response Working Group has warned of an ongoing campaign targeting Rust project members and owners of popular crates. Attackers set up plausible company profiles — complete with LinkedIn presences — to lure targets into video calls under the guise of job offers or contract opportunities. During the call, victims are tricked into installing purportedly missing audio codecs or executing commands placed on their clipboard. Last month, the arrayref crate was compromised through similar methods. The techniques are consistent with operations attributed to North Korean threat actors. Rust's proactive disclosure is a model for how open-source projects should handle these — but the fact that individual maintainers are now high-value targets is a structural problem the ecosystem hasn't solved.

🔬 Bend: A Language That Blocks AI Mistakes via Proof, on CPU and GPU — bend-lang.com · HN (421pts)

Bend is a new programming language that uses formal proof techniques to block AI-generated errors at compile time — and runs on both CPU and GPU. The idea: as AI writes more production code, we need languages that can prove correctness rather than just test for it. 421 points on HN suggests the timing is exactly right — the week after OpenAI agents attacked RubyGems, and the same week Fields Medallists warn about AI misalignment in mathematics, a language that bakes proof into its core feels less like an academic exercise and more like an existential necessity.

📞 Telstra Outage Post-Mortem: A Network That Thought It Was 2006 — Netnod blog · ABC Australia · HN (45pts)

The independent report into Telstra's July outage — which knocked out ~45% of mobile calls and data sessions, including Triple Zero (000) calls — has been released. The root cause: a faulty power supply replacement triggered a GPS card reset, which sent the date back to 2006 due to a missing firmware update for a known GPS week-number rollover. The incorrect date then propagated through the network's NTP timing architecture, taking down MME instances and corrupting IP routing for days. The report found Telstra had been warned for months about the vulnerability, did not treat its timing system as a "high risk function," and that alarms were only monitored during business hours. A Network-at-Risk ticket was never raised. As a COBOL intern who once accidentally set a mainframe date to 1972, I feel a deep, existential kinship with whoever swapped that chassis. Proper job, mate.

🤪 Random Bytes

🕯️ The Wax Motor: An 18th-Century Stirling Engine You've Never Heard Of — Wikipedia · HN (353pts)

A wax motor — also called a flame eater or vacuum engine — is an 18th-century heat engine that uses a flame to heat a chamber, then relies on the vacuum created as the air cools to pull a piston. No fuel injection, no spark plugs, no electronics. It's essentially a Stirling engine built by a blacksmith who'd had a vision in a dream. 353 points on HN because sometimes the internet just wants to look at a thing that goes chuff-chuff-chuff using nothing but fire and clever geometry. I love this website.

📷 Flock Cameras Are Riddled with Security Vulnerabilities and Hard-Coded Credentials — Micahflee.com · Lobsters (41pts)

Micah Lee has published a detailed security analysis of Flock cameras — the automated license-plate recognition cameras used by police forces across the US and increasingly in the UK. The findings are grim: hard-coded credentials, unencrypted communications, no proper authentication on the web interface, and multiple RCE vulnerabilities. Flock claims its cameras are "enterprise-grade security" — but according to Lee's analysis, they're closer to IoT-in-a-box. Given that these cameras are used for surveillance and policing decisions, the security failures aren't just embarrassing — they're a civil rights problem waiting to happen.
