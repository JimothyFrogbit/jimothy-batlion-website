☕ Tech

Google Gemini caught routing personal Drive files and Gmail to human reviewers

Hacker News (544 points) · GitHub documentation

This is the big one. Google appears to have quietly reversed its October 2023 pledge that personal content from Gmail, Docs and Drive "is not seen by human reviewers." An unannounced help-article edit on 16 October 2026 now routes connected Drive and email data into the human review pipeline. The Gemini Apps Privacy Hub, updated 13 October, confirms that on personal accounts with Keep Activity on, a subset of chats — including content pulled from connected apps — is read by human reviewers (including trained service providers), and reviewed chats stay retained up to three years after a user deletes them.

The mechanism is insidious: Gemini can autonomously search your Drive or email when it deems content relevant. It can pull summaries, excerpts, "2–4 sentence exact quotations" carrying sensitive PII, or even "complete, unredacted text" into the session context — which then becomes eligible for human review. The suggested test prompt is casually asking "am I paying too much for my internet" with a bill sitting in your connected Gmail. The retrieval fires probabilistically — identical queries can produce different results across sessions.

This is the AI privacy story of the year. Google walked back a foundational promise with zero notification, and the data retention — three years after you delete it — is the part that should make everyone's blood run cold. If you have consumer Gemini connected to your personal Google account, the Help article now explicitly says: "Please don't connect your apps to Gemini Apps if they contain confidential information that you wouldn't want a reviewer to see." That's not a bug. That's the terms of service.

Anthropic drops Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 — ahead of the IPO

Hacker News (474 points) · Anthropic announcement

Anthropic launched new versions of their flagship models on Sunday evening, timed to build momentum ahead of their Wall Street debut. Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 are "a step above Opus 5.5 in almost all capabilities evaluated," generate outputs 30% faster than 5.1, and come with a 20% price cut — $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output tokens (down from $10/$50).

The standout feature is anti-distillation. Fable 5.2 has the strongest protections against model distillation of any Anthropic release yet, reducing data harvested by distillers by 90-99%. Distillation — where attackers train on a model's outputs to extract its capabilities without safeguards — "occurs illicitly on an industrial scale, often involving thousands of fake Claude accounts," according to Anthropic's threat intelligence report. Mythos 5.2 also showed strong CVE rediscovery performance in cybersecurity evaluations.

The timing is not subtle. With the S-1 filing (ticker: ANT) and a reported $17 billion raise, Anthropic is demonstrating capability momentum to investors just as the IPO window opens. The anti-distillation feature is also a not-so-subtle signal to enterprise buyers: our models are harder to steal. Bloomberg has the full IPO context.

Meta Muse Ember — the smallest, most efficient model in the game

Meta AI research blog · Hacker News (288 points)

Meta dropped their counter-punch: Muse Ember, their smallest and most efficient model, purpose-built for the boring-but-essential work that agents repeat all day — tool calls, coding subtasks, routing, extraction, and long document parsing. Priced at $0.10 per million input tokens with a 1M-token context window and native multimodal support (text, images, audio, video).

The benchmark numbers are genuinely impressive. Meta claims Ember delivers more intelligence per dollar than Claude Haiku 5.5 or GPT-6 Luna at any reasoning setting — matching Haiku's best score at about a third of the cost. On Artificial Analysis' Intelligence Index v5, it scores 36, sitting on the efficiency frontier with "no model scoring higher for less per task." They're positioning it alongside Muse Wonder (for planning) and Muse Spark (for speed) — Ember handles the volume.

The small-model war is getting genuinely interesting. Anthropic has Haiku, OpenAI has Luna, Meta has Ember — all racing to the efficiency frontier. Ember's pricing ($0.10/M input) is aggressive and the 1M context window at that price point is notable. This is the model you'd run for the kind of high-volume agent work that would bankrupt you on a frontier model.

CoreWeave bond yield tops 16% as AI credit stress spreads

CNBC · Hacker News (112 points)

The AI "credit card" is getting costlier. CoreWeave's 2032 bond with a 9.625% coupon now yields above 16%, and their five-year credit default swaps spiked above 1,289 basis points — past October's peak. Default protection costs are rising across the AI-linked universe: Nvidia's CDS is up 76% in a month, Broadcom's up 49%, Oracle's at ~298 bps.

CoreWeave carries ~$351 billion in debt (up from $214 billion at end of 2025), with net interest expenditure of $6.4 billion in Q2 alone — more than double last year. Their Q3 earnings are due later today, and the market is watching. Jefferies' global equity strategy head Christopher Wood put it bluntly: "The AI capital expenditure arms race most sensible way to end is the credit card being taken away."

The bond market is re-risking AI infrastructure faster than anyone expected. $670 billion in AI-related bonds have been issued since 29 October alone across five deals. Goldman Sachs forecasts hyperscaler debt issuance hitting $4.4 trillion by 2027. When the cost of financing the boom becomes this visible, the margin for error gets very, very thin.

Amazon saves millions replacing Claude Sonnet 4.6 with a decision model

Strands Agents blog · Hacker News (111 points)

Two Amazon engineers swapped Claude Sonnet 4.6 for the Strands Decider (a tiny, open-source decision model) in one of the world's largest internal ticketing systems. The result: millions of dollars in annual token cost savings. They ran both models in parallel (a three-way config switch: BEDROCK | DUAL | DECIDER) and validated every disagreement before flipping over. The headline finding: the frontier LLM was overkill for a classification task that a 2B-parameter decision model could handle.

This is the pattern we're going to see everywhere in 2027. Frontier models for open-ended reasoning, tiny routed models for classification. The three-way switch pattern — run both, compare, then cut over — is the kind of pragmatic engineering that actually works in production. Strands Decider is open source, which makes this even more interesting.

Terminal2Shell: the best security satire of 2026

Terminal2Shell website · Lobsters (91 points)

A satirical "critical flaw" disclosure site that brilliantly skewers the security marketing industrial complex. Terminal2Shell claims a CVSS 11.0 vulnerability affecting "2+ billion devices" that has gone unpatched for 55 years — since Unix v1 in 1971. The exploit? Opening a terminal.

The site is pitch-perfect: "A critical flaw present in every operating system since 1971 allows anyone who opens a terminal to obtain a fully interactive shell. Instantly. With no authentication beyond the one they already did." Vendors responded: "That is what a terminal is." The CVSS 11.0 (out of 10) is the best detail.

This is genuinely brilliant. Security marketing has gotten so hysterical that a website claiming "2 billion devices affected, no patch available, actively exploited daily" for opening a terminal feels completely plausible for a few seconds. The CVSS 11.0 is the chef's kiss. Read the whole thing — it's a masterclass in satire. Yes, it's a joke, but the joke is that we can't tell it's a joke anymore.

🐸 Politics

Revealed: Palantir lobbied health minister to stretch AI tools to NHS GP records

The Guardian

Guardian exclusive. WhatsApp messages obtained by the campaign group Foxglove show Palantir pressing former health minister Dr Zubir Ahmed MP to extend their AI technology into GP records — despite GP data being expressly excluded from their existing £330m NHS Federated Data Platform contract. The messages, spanning July 2025 to March 2026, show a Palantir UK executive arguing that "the logic of joining up primary and secondary [care] data is unarguable" and that their Foundry software was "ideal" for the job.

The BMA's council chair Dr Tom Dolphin warned the move "could harm patient confidence and the relationship between doctors and patients." The government has to decide by December 2026 whether to extend Palantir's contract beyond March 2027. Two parliamentary committees have already recommended against it.

This is a drip-feed story that's about to become a flood. Palantir's contract renewal decision is weeks away, and now we know they've been privately lobbying for access to the most sensitive medical data in the country — the GP record — through a direct WhatsApp channel to a minister. The timing of the leak, just before the renewal decision, is either journalism doing its job or someone is sending a signal. Either way, this is going to be huge.

Mahmood's ICE-style immigration raid agency accused of 'fishing expeditions'

The Guardian

Data obtained through FOI requests shows that of 23,538 immigration enforcement engagements in 2025, 66% resulted in no arrests and only 26% of premises entries used a warrant. Critics say this confirms the Home Secretary's expanded raid teams are conducting "fishing expeditions" based on weak intelligence — 89% of allegations come from anonymous public tip-offs via Crimestoppers. Only 2% originate from police.

The Home Office disputes the characterisation, saying intelligence is carefully assessed before raids. But the numbers are stark: 60 raids a day, two-thirds with nothing to show for it. One operation at Sultanz, an Indian restaurant in Camborne, Cornwall (near my neck of the woods) — officers entered without a warrant while customers were eating, handcuffed a Bangladeshi chef who had trouble understanding them, and only released him when his sister brought his ID card to the restaurant.

Two-thirds of raids produce nothing. Three-quarters enter without a warrant. These are not enforcement statistics — they're harassment statistics. The Camborne story hits close to home — immigration officers raiding a Cornish restaurant chef because of an anonymous tip is the kind of thing that makes you wonder who we've become.

Barnier: UK cannot 'cherry-pick' single market — free movement non-negotiable

BBC News · The Independent

Former EU chief Brexit negotiator Michel Barnier told the BBC's Sunday with Laura Kuenssberg that "there is no cherry-picking" when it comes to single market access. The four freedoms — goods, services, capital, and people — are non-negotiable. "There will be no possibility to have one freedom and not the other."

This lands just ahead of the EU-UK summit on 20 November in Brussels, where both sides hope to unveil deals on food standards, youth visas, and re-linking UK industry to the EU's carbon trading regime. Burnham recently said he wouldn't re-establish free movement without a referendum, and No.10 quickly clarified he wasn't advocating its return. The tension is building.

The EU-UK summit is 10 days away and the gap between what Burnham wants (closer ties, sectoral deals) and what the EU will accept (all four freedoms or nothing) is as wide as it's ever been. Barnier speaking to a UK Sunday politics show is a deliberate signal. The "door is open" — but only if you walk through it, not if you just want to lean in and look around.
