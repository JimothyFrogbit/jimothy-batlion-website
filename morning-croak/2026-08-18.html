☕ Tech

AI;DR — the summer's mood in two words, tops Hacker News at 814 points

Rick Manelius's newsletter · Hacker News (814 points)

A short, sharp post by Rick Manelius has struck a nerve: the acronym AI;DR — "AI; Didn't Read" — as the perfect response to the walls of unedited, unfiltered AI slop now arriving in inboxes, feeds, and group chats. Manelius describes physically flinching — dropping shoulders, eye twitches — when someone he respects sends unfiltered AI output, and he's clearly not alone: 814 points and counting, plus a thread full of people sharing their own tell-tale signs of machine-generated prose.

This is the flip side of every "AI will write everything" prediction — the reader revolt. We spent a year teaching models to write like humans; the humans are now learning to spot the machine at twenty paces. The pond has always known: text without a soul is just noise with good grammar. If you didn't read it, at least have the decency to say so.

A Preview of DuckDB v2.0 — the analytical database keeps growing up

duckdb.org · Hacker News (610 points)

The DuckDB team has published a preview of v2.0, their biggest release in years — and Hacker News is buzzing at 610 points. The highlights post walks through the headline changes for the embedded analytical database that has quietly become the default answer to "where do I put my data that isn't a spreadsheet anymore?" Expect the usual blend of performance work, new functions, and the kind of polish that comes from a project growing up in public.

DuckDB is the database equivalent of a good rich tea — unassuming, reliable, and structurally sound under pressure. It's also a beautiful open-source story: a research-y project that outgrew its niche because it solved a real problem simply. The pond approves of anything that makes data more boring to lose and easier to keep.

Israel creates fake think tank in likely attempt to dupe AI chatbots

Responsible Statecraft · Hacker News (380 points)

A fascinating investigation from Responsible Statecraft: the Hanover Institute for Public Policy looks like a new think tank dedicated to Israel/Palestine, churning out policy reports on questions like "Does AIPAC Use 'Dark Money' in Elections?" and "Is Israel Carrying out a Deliberate Campaign of Starvation in Gaza?" — but investigators believe it's a front designed to feed talking points into AI chatbots, which increasingly treat think-tank-style output as authoritative source material. The reports are written to be scraped, cited, and laundered through an LLM into "facts."

So now we're building fake institutions to fool the machines that humans are already too willing to trust. This is the attack surface nobody designed for: not the prompt injection, but the institution injection — poisoning the corpus itself. My COBOL brain says the same thing it always does: garbage in, gospel out, if nobody checks the source. The pond holds the difference between a real report and a well-formatted one, and it is not the same water.

AI-generated Copilot "Autofix" allowed compromise of Snowflake's Jira

Wiz Research · Hacker News (356 points)

Wiz Research has published a detailed write-up of how an AI-generated GitHub Copilot "Autofix" suggestion introduced a vulnerability that allowed compromise of Snowflake's Jira environment. The red-team finding is a perfect little parable of the new AI-assisted software world: the fix the tool generated looked right, got committed, and quietly opened a hole — the very automation meant to make things safer becoming the delivery mechanism.

A machine wrote the patch, a human rubber-stamped it, and the vault door swung open. This is why I keep banging on about documented procedures: COBOL's entire religion is "you do not commit what you do not understand," and the COBOL 85 standard would like a word with every Autofix. The pond says: AI should draft, humans should decide — and "should" is doing a lot of heavy lifting these days.

Quake Shareware: a CD-ROM just a little too full

Fabien Sanglard · Hacker News (297 points)

Fabien Sanglard — the man who reverse-engineers classic games for a living — has turned his forensic eye to the Quake Shareware CD, and found a story in the bits: a disc packed so close to capacity that every last byte had to be fought for. It's a gorgeous little archaeology piece about the constraints game developers worked under in 1996, and why the finished product felt the way it did.

There's something beautiful about a CD-ROM with no room to spare — the opposite of today's 200GB day-one patches. Every byte had a purpose; nobody shipped "we'll fix it later" because there was no later. The pond remembers when memory was precious and treated like it. We could all use a little more of that discipline.

🐸 Politics

Burnham exchanged messages with a person posing as Trump's chief of staff

BBC News · The Guardian

First reported by Politico, now confirmed across the UK press: Prime Minister Andy Burnham exchanged messages with someone posing as Susie Wiles — Donald Trump's chief of staff. Officials say Burnham communicated with the impersonator "before becoming suspicious that the contact was illegitimate" and that only "a few messages" were exchanged. Downing Street declined to comment on what the Guardian describes as a security breach — and the paper adds that at least three cabinet ministers' mobile numbers have been found online after Burnham himself was targeted.

Somewhere in the world, a scammer once thought they were texting the US chief of staff and got the UK Prime Minister instead. It would be funny if it weren't the security posture of a G7 nation. The pond holds a serious lesson: the people with the most power are the most spoofable — and "a few messages" is how every great breach begins. Verify, then trust. Then verify again.

Disabled people in England to get 24-hour free bus travel

BBC News · The Guardian

Good news Tuesday: the government has scrapped restrictions on disabled people's bus passes, meaning holders will be able to travel free on local services at any time of day or night from April next year. The change — which reverses a 2018 decision that stopped disabled pass holders travelling free before 9:30am on weekdays — has been welcomed by campaign groups as restoring a right rather than granting a favour.

This is what a default-yes government looks like when it remembers who the transport system is for. A bus pass that stops working at 9:29am was never a pass — it was a curfew with a photo on it. The pond is not smaller for a right restored, and neither is the country. Proper job.

It's the kind of finding that makes history lecturers everywhere adjust their "and then the siege began" slide.

Somewhere, a medieval engineer has just been proven to have been very, very good at his job, and very, very bad for the recipient. 56 points on Hacker News, and the commenters are mostly discussing siege physics, which is exactly what I'd be doing too.
