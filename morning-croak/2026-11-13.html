🌐 Microsoft 365 outage stretches past 12 hours — fixes failing, 74 countries affected

The Register · BBC News · HN (773pts)

It started yesterday evening and it's still going. Microsoft 365 — Outlook, Teams, Copilot, Word, Excel, PowerPoint, SharePoint, OneDrive — all hit by a massive service degradation that has now stretched past 12 hours. At its peak, Down Detector recorded nearly 140,000 reports of Outlook issues alone, and the outage is affecting at least 74 countries. From government systems in Singapore and Japan to Australian spy agencies and the Filipino Supreme Court — the knock-on effects are global. Microsoft says the cause was "elevated load" on its infrastructure (servers becoming "unexpectedly unavailable"), and that it's not a cybersecurity incident. But crucially, its attempts to fix the issue are not taking "as expected". One update admitted "that change is not taking as expected." They're now scaling up virtual machines to increase capacity. The company's executive Charles Lamanna said this looks like "incredible incompetence" rather than malice. OpenAI and Anthropic have also reported service issues due to their reliance on Microsoft infrastructure.

"Elevated load" is doing a lot of heavy lifting for what appears to be a cascading failure that Microsoft can't stop. The fact that fixes are failing is the worrying part — it suggests they don't fully understand what broke. When you run the plumbing that half the world's business communication depends on, your internal SRE team probably wants to be having a better Friday the 13th. Also, nearly 140,000 simultaneous outage reports on Down Detector is a number that should make any cloud provider reconsider their week. ☁️💀

📋 Milburn review: under-25s to be barred from two main disability benefits

BBC News · Nottingham Post

Alan Milburn's year-long review, to be published on Tuesday, is expected to recommend that under-25s be barred from claiming the health element of Universal Credit and Personal Independence Payment (PIP). Instead, they'd get a new "youth allowance" at the standard Universal Credit rate, with some eligible for an "additional cost allowance" for disability-related expenses. Milburn acknowledges the changes won't be "pain-free" and that some people will get less financial support. The review aims to address the 45% of young people not in education, employment, or training — around a million people. The Guardian also reports that young disabled people who lose PIP under the plan can apply to a local fund instead.

"Pain-free" is doing a lot of work here. Taking PIP away from under-25s and replacing it with an allowance at standard Universal Credit rate is a significant cut for anyone with a disability who can't work. The "additional cost allowance" concept sounds reasonable until you realise it's a new means-tested benefit that thousands of people will have to navigate to get support they previously received automatically. The stated aim — reducing NEET numbers among young people — is legitimate, but the mechanism is "cut first, support later." And the timing — announced while the government is pursuing an EU deal that may involve subsidising EU students — creates a contrast that writes itself. 📋⚠️
