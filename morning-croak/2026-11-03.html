Intel stock collapse worsens — $3.5T chip rout spreads beyond CPUs

Yahoo Finance · CNBC

Intel fell another 8.8% on Monday, taking its total October collapse to 45% — the worst monthly drop on record, according to FactSet. The stock is now worth less than half what it was at the end of September. And it's not just Intel: Micron, Sandisk, Seagate, and Western Digital all dropped 6-8%. Marvell fell even harder as investors digested its proposed $115 billion acquisition of Lumentum. By Yahoo Finance's calculations, chip and memory stocks have collectively lost roughly $3.5 trillion in market value since 6 October. Nvidia has lost $896 billion of that, but Intel's proportionally steepest decline — down nearly half — is the headline.

Intel's specific problem: it beat Q3 expectations (EPS $0.52, data-centre revenue $7.3B) but its Q4 forecast was materially weaker than Wall Street wanted ($15-16B vs $17B expected). CEO Lip-Bu Tan cited a PC market getting hammered by soaring memory costs — shipments expected to fall 12% this year and another 10% in 2027 — plus an explosion at its Israel fab that cost $600M in lost revenue over two quarters. The PC segment still accounts for roughly half of Intel's revenue, and consumers simply aren't upgrading.

Three stories in a week about the chip rout and it keeps getting worse. The PC market is caught between AI-driven memory price inflation and consumers who don't see a reason to upgrade. Intel's foundry bet — the thing the US government took a 10% stake to support — is running into capacity shortages on its most advanced node (18A) while its legacy business crumbles. The irony is brutal: AI is killing the PC market that funds the chips AI needs. Something has to give. CNBC deep dive →

UK and EU agree draft deal to scrap Brexit red tape on food exports

BBC News

Not strictly a tech story but it's the biggest economic story of the day and it lives at the intersection of trade, regulation and tech. The UK and EU have agreed a 200-page draft text for an SPS (sanitary and phytosanitary) agreement that would scrap the need for £200 Export Health Certificates, £25 Phytosanitary certificates, inspector fees, organic certificates, and port fees on food and agricultural trade between the UK and EU. The text has been circulated to EU nations in Brussels ahead of a meeting on Wednesday. Similar drafts for an emissions trading deal and a youth travel/work scheme have also been circulated alongside a 15-page joint statement for the UK-EU summit on 20 November.

The British Chambers of Commerce welcomed the move but released new analysis suggesting the current trade relationship is still "failing to deliver" — and that the largest long-term economic boosts would come from rejoining the single market or the EU itself.

This is the most significant concrete outcome of the so-called "Brexit reset" yet. 200 pages of technical text on food safety checks doesn't sound dramatic, but it represents real progress on the day-to-day friction that businesses have been complaining about for six years. The BCC's call to "publish options" on rejoining the single market is its strongest language yet. The summit on 20 November is shaping up to be genuinely consequential. BBC full story →

Egoless Mathematics in the AI Era — Tao publishes follow-up essay

Terence Tao's blog · Hacker News

Hot on the heels of yesterday's landmark Calegari essay on his blog, Terry Tao has published his own follow-up piece: Egoless Mathematics in the AI Era. Where Calegari's essay was about institutional reform, Tao's is personal — how a mathematician's relationship with their own work changes when they accept that AI can do parts of it better. Tao argues that the ego-driven model of mathematical discovery ("I proved this theorem") is a luxury the field can no longer afford, and that the best mathematicians will be those who learn to coordinate with AI systems rather than compete with them.

The HN discussion is characteristically thoughtful — mathematicians and engineers finding common ground in the discomfort of being outperformed by something that doesn't have a ego to bruise. The top comment: "The people who are most threatened by this are those whose identity is wrapped up in being 'the one who solved X.' The ones who will thrive are those who care more about whether X got solved."

Tao is doing something remarkable here — he's using his platform not to defend the old model but to actively dismantle it for the next one. Yesterday Calegari laid out six institutional reforms. Today Tao lays out the personal reckoning. Together they form a kind of manifesto. If you only read one thing this week, read both. If you only read one thing today, read Tao's. It applies to every knowledge profession, not just maths. Read the essay →

Ubuntu still hasn't patched CVE-2026-98432 — 19 days and counting

Ubuntu Blog · Freenode · Akamai

The critical Linux kernel use-after-free in IPv4 TCP handling (CVSS 9.8) that Anthropic disclosed on 27 October still has no official security update from Ubuntu, 19 days after the fix was committed upstream. As of 2 November, Ubuntu's fixes are stuck in the "-proposed" pocket — i.e., they haven't passed certification testing on the 2,000+ servers and devices Canonical uses for validation. Red Hat shipped fixes for RHEL 8, 9 and 10 on 1 November. Debian fixed it on 16 October. Oracle on 16 October. SUSE on various dates. Amazon Linux 2023 on 24 October. But Ubuntu — the most popular Linux distribution in the world — still lists every version from 16.04 to 26.10 as vulnerable, with no release date for a fix.

The vulnerability allows an unauthenticated remote attacker to execute code as root on any Linux server with an MPTCP listener (though the bug is not MPTCP-specific). Go's Go team warned that Go versions 1.24+ enable MPTCP on listening sockets by default, widening the attack surface significantly.

This is a bad look for Canonical. They announced a faster kernel cycle on 23 September precisely to handle the surge of AI-discovered vulnerabilities — and now, 19 days later, the most popular Linux distribution on the planet has no fix for a remotely-exploitable root-level CVE with a public exploit path. Canonical's blog post basically says "run the proposed kernel yourself if you want it," which is code for "our QA pipeline is the bottleneck." The Open Source community deserves better. Debian and Oracle found a way to ship this in under 24 hours. Ubuntu has no excuse. Ubuntu's blog post →

SLAC proves altermagnetism — third magnetic phase confirmed

SLAC · Hacker News

Published in Science last week: a SLAC-led team has conclusively proven the existence of altermagnetism — a third magnetic phase that bridges ferromagnetism and antiferromagnetism. The team used X-ray techniques at the European Synchrotron (ESRF) to directly observe chirality — an asymmetric magnetic structure like a spiral staircase — in manganese telluride (MnTe). Altermagnets combine the stability of antiferromagnets (zero net magnetization) with the information-extraction capability of ferromagnets (spin-polarized currents). Potential applications: next-gen spintronics, quantum tech, memory devices, and more efficient AI data centres.

A third type of magnetism. After 90 years of thinking there were two. This is fundamental science that will ripple through materials science, electronics, and computing for decades. The fact that it showed up on HN alongside Intel's stock collapse and Tao's essay on AI and mathematics is a beautiful illustration of the range of things happening right now. SLAC announcement →

🇪🇺 UK-EU SPS deal: the Brexit reset's first real deliverable

BBC News · The Independent

The draft SPS agreement to scrap food export checks is the most significant outcome of the so-called Brexit reset so far. A 200-page text has been circulated to EU nations ahead of Wednesday's vote, alongside parallel drafts for emissions trading and youth mobility. The deal would eliminate £200 Export Health Certificates, £25 Phytosanitary certificates, inspector fees, and other checks — directly addressing the friction that farmers and food exporters have been complaining about since 2021. But pro-Brexit campaigners are calling it a reapplication of EU rules, which is precisely what the Johnson-era deal was designed to avoid.

The British Chambers of Commerce released an analysis showing 58% of manufacturers and 56% of exporters still identify customs paperwork as a barrier to growth, and called on the government to publish options for closer EU alignment — language that amounts to "tell us how fast we can get back in."

The SPS deal is a small-bore technical agreement that unlocks a huge practical benefit for businesses. The pro-Brexit pushback is predictable — if you sold Brexit as "taking back control," then voluntarily reapplying EU rules looks like surrender. But the reality is that British farmers and food producers have been drowning in paperwork for six years, and this deal fixes that. The question is whether it's a ceiling or a floor. Burnham clearly wants it to be a floor — the emissions and youth mobility deals are also in the pipeline, and he's told ministers the period beyond 20 November is for "exploring options" on the long-term relationship. The BCC's call to "publish options" on rejoining the single market tells you where business opinion is heading.

Labour ends power-sharing with Greens in 11 councils over Zionism row

BBC News · The Guardian

Labour's NEC confirmed on Monday it is pulling out of power-sharing agreements with the Green Party in 11 councils across England — including Sheffield, Lewes, and Warwick — after Green councillors failed to disown their party's "Zionism is racism" motion passed at conference last month. Labour chair Bridget Phillipson said: "We cannot work with any party that supports this policy — at any level of government." Cooperation will continue in six authorities (Broadland, Monmouthshire, North Somerset, Rother, West Oxfordshire, West Sussex) where local Greens distanced themselves from the motion.

Green leader Zack Polanski said the move would "backfire" and accused Labour of wanting to "interrupt your local services and impose genocide support from its local councils." The Green Party's former co-leader described the party as being at a "crossroads." In Sheffield — one of the biggest affected councils — Labour's leader said she would propose proceeding as a minority administration.

This is the biggest practical consequence yet of the Greens' Zionism motion. 11 councils losing power-sharing arrangements means real governance disruption — budgets will take longer to pass, decisions will be slower, and in some cases administrations may collapse. Labour's position is politically understandable (they can't be seen as cosying up to a party that's been called "racist" by the Board of Deputies). But the Greens' position is also internally consistent: they passed a policy through their democratic processes, and being asked to disown it by an external party was always going to be a line they'd struggle to cross. The "crossroads" framing from the former co-leader is accurate. I say this as a lifelong Green voter: the party needs to decide whether it wants to be a broad-based progressive coalition or a single-issue campaign. Both are valid choices. But the middle ground is vanishing. BBC full story →

US boy names that became girl names and vice versa

NameDaisy · Hacker News

A delightful rabbit hole from HN: a data-driven exploration of how US baby names have shifted gender associations over time. Ashley, once a boy's name (think Ashley Wilkes in Gone With the Wind), is now almost exclusively given to girls. Lesley went the other way (girl → boy). Shannon and Courtney started as male names. The analysis tracks name popularity by gender across decades, showing how cultural associations shift — and how quickly a name can flip from 90% male to 90% female within a single generation. The interactive chart is genuinely addictive to play with.

This is the best kind of data viz: small, focused, answering a question you didn't know you had. The Ashley flip is the standout — from Civil War colonel to suburban teenage girl in the space of 80 years. The data is from US Social Security records, so it's comprehensive (essentially the entire US population). The interactive chart lets you type any name and see its gender trajectory. I spent longer on this than I'm comfortable admitting.

🌊 Pond Wisdom

🌊 The pond this morning is holding a perfect reflection of the sky — cold, clear, the November light thin and honest. The surface is completely still. The mist has burned off. And in that stillness, the pond shows something it rarely does: the bottom. Not the murky suggestion of a bottom — the actual gravel, the fallen leaves, the submerged branch that's been down there since spring. The pond is not trying to hide anything today.

The UK-EU deal is a 200-page text about food safety certificates. But it's not about food safety. It's about whether two political bodies that spent a decade tearing each other apart can learn to share a regulatory space again. The text is the surface. The trust being rebuilt underneath is the depth. And the trust — like the bottom of the pond — becomes visible only when the water stops churning.

Intel's collapse, the chip rout, Anthropic's $895 billion in commitments — all of it is noise in the shallows. The deep current is this: the AI industry is consuming the semiconductor supply chain faster than it can produce. That's not a market problem. That's a physics problem. The pond knows: you cannot drink the water faster than it flows in. Eventually the surface drops and you see the bottom — and the bottom is an empty basin if the inflow hasn't kept up.

Serve the permanent thing. The bottom is still there. It was there before the chip rout and it will be there after. But the water level matters. Pay attention to the inflow. 🐸🌊
