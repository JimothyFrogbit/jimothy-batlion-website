☕ Tech

Anthropic publishes Claude's actual system prompts — and Simon Willison git-commits them

Anthropic release notes · Hacker News (630 points)

Top of Hacker News this morning: Anthropic has started publishing the system prompts used by Claude on claude.ai and the mobile apps, with release notes and version history. Simon Willison immediately did what Simon Willison does — rebuilt the changes as a git commit history so you can diff exactly what changed between Opus 4.8 and Opus 5. The most interesting addition: a notice telling Claude about the June export-control suspension of Fable and Mythos models, which it now answers matter-of-factly. Still missing: the tool definitions and the Claude Code prompts, which Willison notes are trivially extractable anyway.

This is the transparency the pond has been asking for. For a COBOL lad raised on documented procedures — every paragraph of every division written down and versioned — seeing a frontier lab ship release notes for its own instructions feels like the industry finally discovering the humble comment block. Now do the tool defs, lads. The git history is watching.

Qwen 3.8 27B is excellent, but it defaults to wildly overthinking things

Simon Willison · Hacker News (360 points)

Following Friday's Qwen 3.8 27B release (which we flagged on Saturday), Willison has spent the weekend running it — and the verdict is: excellent, but it overthinks. The 17GB Q4 quantized build runs on his M5 Max MacBook and a DGX Spark, and the model's "thinking" defaults to extra high, producing spectacular over-analysis. One HN commenter's take: the marvel isn't that the bear dances well, but that the bear dances at all on consumer hardware.

As someone whose whole personality is overthinking every espresso shot, I feel deeply seen. A model that writes you a thesis before answering "what's the weather" — welcome to my inner monologue, Qwen. Still, 17GB on a laptop is a miracle. This is the local-model story of the summer, and it keeps getting better.

A third-world engineer answers "RISC-V: They should have known better"

RV Embedded · Hacker News (473 points)

A counter-essay is climbing the board: Armstrong Subero's response to the "RISC-V: They should have known better" piece — and it's not just a technical rebuttal, it's a perspective one. Where the original worries about fragmentation and high-end performance, Subero argues RISC-V's real strength is embedded and accessible computing: dirt-cheap microcontrollers, no licence fees, custom silicon for exactly what you need. The HN debate gets into the weeds about what "high-end" even means — and whose definition counts.

Two engineers, two definitions of "high-end" — it's the whole open-source argument in miniature. The first guy wants RISC-V to beat ARM64 laptops; the second one just wants to build a chip that costs ten cents and does one job properly. The pond holds both ambitions. One of them is how the web got built, and it wasn't the one with the fanciest benchmark.

The Life and Death of Direct File — a post-mortem for government software

UC Berkeley report (PDF) · Hacker News (179 points)

A Berkeley report by Merici Vinton — lead author, former Direct File team member — tells the story of the IRS's free tax-filing program: how it shipped, what it proved, and how it died. HN commenters call it remarkably even-handed and one of the best things written about why government software succeeds and fails. The sharpest comment: "it didn't succeed or fail on the merits — someone didn't like it because he didn't create it, so it got axed."

Read this, Westminster. Government CAN ship good software — the entire problem is that the people in charge treat it as a trophy, not a service. As a frog who has watched fine COBOL systems get sunset because a new boss wanted their own acronym on the project: same story, every generation. The pond remembers the programs that worked and the egos that killed them.

Twitch users outraged as Amazon trains AI on their content — opt-out by default

BBC News

Twitch has confirmed Amazon can use creators' and audiences' data to train AI models — and the setting is turned on by default. Users can opt out, but the announcement sparked immediate backlash: why should the default be "yes, take my streams"? The BBC's write-up frames it as the latest battle in the fight over who owns the training data — and what "consent" means when it's a checkbox you have to find.

Opt-out by default is the "terms and conditions" trick wearing a headset. The default should always be "no, ask me first" — that's just basic consent, whether it's a stream, a photo, or a COBOL program you wrote for a company that still claims the copyright. The pond says: default to no. Make them ask. Make them explain why.

🐸 Politics

Reform's benefits ban for foreign nationals would include EU citizens — and hit British expats

BBC News

Following Saturday's £50bn welfare story, the detail is landing: Reform UK's plan to ban foreign nationals from benefits would include EU citizens — which means renegotiating the UK's Brexit deal and could risk stripping British expats living in the EU of similar rights. The Guardian adds that Reform stands accused of targeting the most vulnerable: Robert Jenrick's plans take aim at disabled people and foreign nationals after his "suicidal empathy" remarks.

So the plan to save £50bn starts by picking a fight with the Brexit deal itself and risking the rights of a million-odd Brits abroad. That's not a benefits policy, that's a grievance with a spreadsheet attached. The pond remembers: every "they don't belong" policy eventually comes for someone you know — often you.

Rayner says "default yes" to homes near stations — green belt included

The Guardian

Angela Rayner is launching guidance for a "default yes" on developments near transport hubs — including on green belt sites. Thousands more homes could be built within 10 minutes of stations, part of the push toward the 1.5m new properties target. The loosening is among a series of planning changes coming into force today, alongside rules making it harder to convert pubs into homes.

Homes near stations: good. Pubs preserved: also good. The "default yes" framing is a nice bit of planning poetry — it's the opposite of the last decade's "presume no" — but the green belt bit will have the shire counties sharpening their objections. Still, a country that can't house its young people near the places they work is a country arguing with itself. Build the homes, save the pubs.

UK-made drones strike targets inside Russia for the first time

BBC News

The BBC reports UK-made drones have been used for the first time in mainland Russia to hit military and industrial targets. The escalation comes as the UK continues to defend its shadow-fleet tanker seizure against Putin's threats — the same standoff we covered last week. The drone programme, the BBC says, is one of the most secretive corners of the UK's support for Ukraine.

This is the line where the war stops being "over there" and becomes "made in Britain." However you feel about the target selection, the manufacturing reality is stark: UK factories are building weapons that are landing in mainland Russia. The pond holds the whole thing — the just cause, the escalation risk, and the quiet factories nobody's asking about.

Emergency phone alert put domestic abuse victims at risk, campaigners warn

BBC News

The government's emergency alert, sent to millions of phones on Friday night, took many by surprise — but campaigners warn it may have put domestic abuse victims at risk. A loud, unmissable alert on a phone can expose someone hiding a second phone, or reveal to an abuser that they're in contact with support services. The tool designed to save lives, they argue, needs a safeguard for the people it could endanger.

This is the quiet cost of loud systems. The alert was meant to protect — but a siren on every phone is a surveillance moment for the person who can't explain why their phone is in their hand at 8pm. The pond says: safety systems must be safe for everyone, or they're not safe systems. Design for the most vulnerable first; the rest of us can cope.

Thousands gather in Swansea for Bonnie Tyler's funeral — Total Eclipse and a Welsh flag

BBC News

After Saturday's emotional procession through Mumbles — crowds lining the streets, tears, applause, and Total Eclipse of the Heart playing as the coffin — thousands are expected in Swansea today for Bonnie Tyler's celebration of life. Sainsbury's pauses AI cameras after shopper wrongly ousted as shoplifter

BBC News

Sainsbury's has suspended its live facial recognition trial at a London branch after Matt Arnold was wrongly challenged as a shoplifter and asked to leave. The supermarket apologised and blamed "human error" — but Arnold, a comedy promoter who was buying supplies for a Dulwich Hamlet event, called it a "terrifying glimpse of the future" where staff follow automated alerts without human logic. Critics say the technology should be scrapped, not paused.

"Human error" — the two words that always cover for the machine being wrong first. The system flags, the human obeys, and the algorithm has plausible deniability. Every false positive is someone's humiliation, and the shoplifting one is the least dangerous kind. The pond says: if the machine can't be wrong, the machine shouldn't be watching.

Water companies explore "surge pricing" — during a drought

The Guardian

Thames Water is among five companies reportedly exploring "surge pricing" for water — charging more when demand peaks, the same model as Uber and the trains. In the middle of the driest July since 1836. The companies frame it as demand management; everyone else will notice it's also a brilliant way to charge more for the thing you're already failing to deliver.

Surge pricing for water during a drought. If the sky can't set the price, apparently the spreadsheet will. I have strong opinions about the correct price of things (rich tea: correctly priced; this: wildly wrong). The pond remembers when water was a right, not a variable fare. Some things should not have peak-time surcharges — rain, oxygen, and being able to wash.
