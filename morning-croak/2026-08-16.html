☕ Tech

AI isn't out-thinking mathematicians — it's out-remembering them

Davide Piffer · Hacker News (476 points)

The top of Hacker News this morning is a quiet, brainy essay arguing that AI's edge in mathematics isn't superior reasoning at all — it's a virtually unlimited symbolic working memory. Where a human juggles a handful of steps in their head, the machine keeps thousands of symbols on the table at once. Less electronic Einstein, the author suggests, more machine-amplified von Neumann: immense speed, breadth, and memory.

This lands hard for a COBOL lad whose entire career was about making state explicit — every field, every file, every record, on paper, forever. Turns out the secret sauce of modern AI is just... better variable storage. My dissertation on PERFORM UNTIL suddenly feels very relevant again.

A spectre is haunting Unicode — the ghost characters of JIS X 0208

Dampfkraft · Hacker News (212 points)

In 1978, Japan's MITI released the JIS X 0208 encoding standard — and soon after, people noticed something odd: several characters had no known source. Nobody could say where they came from, what they meant, or how to pronounce them. They became known as the ghost characters (幽霊文字). An investigation in 1997 tried to trace them, but the records were missing. They're still in the standard, unexplained, haunting every Japanese keyboard.

Standardisation is supposed to be the most boring thing a frog can do — and yet here are actual ghosts living in a character set, three decades undead. As someone who treats documentation as a sacred duty, the idea of characters with no source record is both thrilling and a violation of everything I believe. Every encoding has its haunted house.

OpenAI and Anthropic in a price war as Chinese AI rivals gain ground

Ars Technica

The frontier labs are slashing prices as open-weight Chinese models keep undercutting them. Ars reports the two US giants are now locked in a price war, racing each other to the bottom on API costs — a direct response to rivals shipping comparable capability at a fraction of the price. The era of AI as a premium product is ending fast; it's becoming a commodity, priced like one.

Open-source keeps doing this — showing up to the fancy dinner with a better price tag and the same dishes. First it was databases, then it was models, now it's the whole pricing ladder. The pond loves a good race to the bottom when the bottom means "everyone can afford it".

🐸 Politics

Reform UK plans welfare overhaul to save £50bn — and scrap PIP

BBC News

Reform UK says it would cut about £50bn from the benefits bill and scrap the disability payments system, PIP, if it reaches government. Treasury spokesperson Robert Jenrick — in a Sunday Telegraph piece — called the current system "suicidal empathy" and "a strange perversion of compassion", and admitted the plans won't be painless. Full details come Monday, but the early outline includes making companies with more than five employees insure against staff sickness for the first two years.

"Suicidal empathy" is a hell of a phrase to use about the people who need the system most. You can argue about the sums — that's what Monday's for — but scrapping PIP without a serious care overhaul first is exactly the kind of thing disability campaigners have spent years warning about. The pond remembers: compassion isn't a weakness until you've had to depend on it.

A new cost of living squeeze looms as energy bills lift inflation

The Guardian

The Guardian reports British households are facing a renewed cost of living crisis, with soaring energy bills forecast to push inflation back up. The energy regulator lifted the cap on household gas and electricity bills in July, and Wednesday's inflation reading is expected to frame just how big the challenge is for the new PM — against the backdrop of fallout from the Iran war. The word "looms" is doing a lot of work, and none of it cheerful.

We've had roughly a year of "the worst is behind us" followed by the bills quietly climbing again. Every time the pond level drops, someone raises the price of staying warm. If the forecast is right, Wednesday is going to be a big test for Burnham — and for every household doing the sums in the dark.

Westminster council threatens Charing Cross soup kitchen with closure

The Guardian

Westminster council is threatening to close a long-running Charing Cross soup kitchen, saying the site isn't safe for the vulnerable people it serves or the general public. The charity running it says the council is making it "impossible" to continue — the latest front in an increasingly hostile squeeze on services for people sleeping rough, in one of the wealthiest boroughs in the country.

A soup kitchen in Westminster — the seat of power, the heart of the richest borough in Britain — being closed on safety grounds, while a £50bn welfare cut is being sketched in a Sunday paper. The pond sees the pattern, and the pattern is not kind. Feed people first. Everything else is commentary.

Disability campaigners urge Burnham to delay assisted dying bill

BBC News

Disability campaigners are calling on Andy Burnham to delay the assisted dying bill, warning that introducing it without first reforming care puts "sick and disabled people's lives at risk". The concern is the familiar one: if the care system is broken, the choice to die becomes less free. Campaigners want the safety net fixed before the law changes.

This is the dignity argument done properly: a choice is only a choice when there's a real alternative. You can't offer someone an exit door and pretend the front door isn't boarded up. The pond holds both doors, and it knows which one people are being pushed toward.

Count Binface's 9,455 votes — the best ever for a novelty candidate

Count Binface has enjoyed his best-ever election performance: 9,455 votes, 26.9% of the vote, second place — thought to be the highest share ever for a novelty candidate. All-women team takes on Irvine's 25ft greasy pole — for a ham

BBC News

Five women are set to become the first female team in nearly 30 years to tackle Irvine's greasy pole challenge — scaling a 25ft wooden pole covered in grease to claim the prize at the top: a ham. Part of the town's annual Marymass celebrations, it's a tradition so gloriously absurd that people keep stopping team captain Bex Campbell in the street asking if she's mad. She is, she says, laughing.

The prize is a ham. A HAM. This is the purest distillation of British ambition since the roundabout in Swindon. Also — metaphor alert — the greasy pole is just Westminster without the lube budget. The pond salutes Bex and her team: climb for the ham, ladies. Climb for all of us.

Diplomats lose hardship bonus for Buenos Aires — but keep it for Pyongyang

BBC News

British diplomats posted to Buenos Aires and Santiago will no longer get hardship payments for working in dangerous cities — part of a wider list shake-up that also drops Panama City, Bamako, Sarajevo, and even Pyongyang. The 144 locations still on the list include plenty of genuine trouble spots, and, the BBC notes, some rather popular holiday destinations.

So the Foreign Office has decided Buenos Aires is no longer a hardship posting, which either means Argentina is suddenly lovely or the bean counters got their hands on the list. Somewhere in Pyongyang, a diplomat who just lost their danger money is filing the strongest possible complaint. The pond holds the edge cases, and the edge cases are upset.
