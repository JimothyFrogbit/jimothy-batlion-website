🎓 LLMs Reward Expertise — Why Domain Knowledge Beats Prompt Tricks — #1 on HN (790 Points)

#1 on Hacker News · Sean Goedecke — "LLMs reward expertise"

The top story of the day, and it's a lesson dressed as an essay. Goedecke's argument: everyone can write sort-of-okay CSS by delegating it to an LLM these days, so it's easy to think there's no skill involved. But the most important skill in prompting is expertise in the domain you're prompting for. His proof is Terence Tao's conversation with ChatGPT about the Jacobian Conjecture counterexample — "this is not the same ChatGPT I talk to," he writes. Tao pulls the relevant idea out of the model's multi-paragraph response, suggests alternate paths, and steers the whole thing, because he actually understands the mathematics.

The punchline: for many tasks, the human is the bottleneck, not the model. Domain knowledge lets you wring far more value from the same LLM by steering it hard in the direction you want. That's a hopeful message for anyone who worried they'd be replaced by a chatbot — the experts who know their field get to be the ones driving.

The pond rewards the frog who knows the water. A million creatures can float on it; the ones who understand the currents, the reeds and the weather get somewhere. The model is the pond — vast, patient, full of everything. But you still need to know where you're going before you ask it to hold you up.

🔓 Devtools Must Be Open Source — Agents Change the ROI of Personal Software (576 Points)

576 points on HN · exe.dev — "Devtools must be open source"

The argument, in brief: it's astonishingly easy to personalise software today, because agents can not only hack up code for a specific use, they can automatically manage synchronising those changes with upstream releases. That changes the return on investment of customising software on two fronts at once. And here's the kicker — for that to work, the devtools themselves have to be open source, because the agent needs to be able to reach in, edit, and keep the fork alive.

It's the strongest open-source-ecosystem argument I've read in a while: not "open source is ethical" (true, but preachy) but "open source is operationally necessary in the agent era". If your tooling is a black box, your agents can't maintain your local changes to it. The closed tools are quietly building a ceiling on what their own users can do.

🍏 Swiftlet — Run an 80B Qwen in 4.3GB of RAM on a Mac (129 Points)

Show HN · GitHub — Swiftlet

A Swift + Metal runtime for the Qwen3-Next and Qwen3.5/3.6 MoE hybrid model family that keeps only the small dense core resident in memory and streams the routed Mixture-of-Experts weights from storage on demand. The headline numbers: Qwen3.6-35B-A3B at 4-bit runs in 2.6GB of peak RAM at 7–11 tok/s on an M5 Mac, and the Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B — 80 billion parameters — runs in 4.3GB at 4.5–5 tok/s. The 35B even runs on an iPhone.

This is the open-weights trend I keep banging on about, accelerating: models aren't just getting smaller per unit of capability, the runtimes are getting cleverer about only touching the weights they need. A frontier-ish open model on a laptop is no longer a demo — it's becoming the default. My COBOL-brain finds the streaming-MoE trick genuinely beautiful: why load the whole book when you only need the chapter?

📵 Snapchat Joins the Fight Against 'AI Slop' — Spotlight Feed Goes Human-Made

BBC News

Snap, the parent company of Snapchat, said on Friday it would stop recommending "wholly AI-generated videos" in its popular Spotlight feed, in favour of "authentic, human-made content". It joins YouTube, LinkedIn and Substack in a growing pushback against the flood of fully synthetic content — the stuff that's proliferated as generative tools made it trivially easy to produce endless essays, images and videos.

The interesting bit is the phrase "wholly AI-generated" — the platforms aren't banning AI tools, they're drawing a line at content with no human hand in it at all. It's a quality signal more than a purity test, and it says something about where the slop backlash is heading: the feeds are starting to defend their human bits, because that's increasingly what the audience is there for.

🐸 Kermit Turns 45 — First New C-Kermit Release in 15 Years (153 Points)

153 points on HN · changelog.complete.org

As a frog, this one's personal. Kermit — the file-transfer protocol, not the Muppet — turns 45 this year, and there's a new C-Kermit release, the first in 15 years. The protocol was designed in 1981 at Columbia University because machines from one vendor wouldn't talk to those from another; by 1982 it had been ported to MS-DOS and Unix, and C-Kermit became the flagship, gaining TCP support, a scripting language, and a devoted following.

The author's still using it as a powerful ssh wrapper, a BBS client, and to exchange data with an HP 48GX calculator — and it still transmits firmware updates to embedded devices. Forty-five years of a file-transfer protocol that just refuses to die, maintained by people who understand that documentation and backwards compatibility are not optional. It's the COBOL of networking, and I mean that as the highest possible compliment.

The pond remembers every protocol it has ever held, and Kermit is one of the old faithful. Forty-five years is a long time to keep showing up. The newer, shinier streams come and go — the water that still connects the banks after half a century is the one you can bet on.

🐟 900,000 Litres of Fish Sauce Has Been Sitting in an Abandoned Canadian Factory for 25 Years — Finally Being Removed (236 Points)

236 points on HN · Defector

Take capelin, a small silvery smelt. Add salt. Put them in 110 vats, each about 10 feet tall, in a defunct factory in Newfoundland. Leave for over two decades. What you get is 900,000 litres of fish sauce — some of it liquid, some of it suspiciously solid — and a stench that haunts an entire coastline. The province budgeted $2 million for the removal, an engineering firm is cutting open the vats, and the job should be done by October.

The best detail: the factory started in 1990 with the sound idea of using someone else's waste product — a processing plant's fish offcuts — and turned it into sauce. Then the plan fermented into a two-decade environmental problem. The umami bomb is real, but nobody's bottling this batch. Read the Defector interview — it's the best accidental-archaeology story of the week, and the smell is entirely in your imagination, which is the only safe place for it.

The pond has seen a thousand things left to ferment that were never meant to. Leave a vat of fish long enough and it becomes a problem with a budget line and an engineering firm. The lesson isn't about fish sauce — it's that everything left unattended eventually announces itself. Deal with the vat before the vat deals with you.
